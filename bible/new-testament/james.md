# James

The Letter of James is a short letter of moral teaching in the New Testament. It opens with greetings from "James, a servant of God and of the Lord Jesus Christ, to the twelve tribes which are in the Dispersion" ([James 1:1](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JAS01.htm#V1)). The introduction to the New American Bible describes it as a letter "only in the most conventional sense": it is exhortation (*parenesis*) in the tradition of Jewish wisdom literature, made of chains of short sayings, many of which echo Jesus' teaching in the Sermon on the Mount ([USCCB, Introduction to the Letter of James](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/james/0)). Its theme, in BibleProject's summary, is how believers should live: when they listen to God's word and obey it, they show their love for God and for others ([BibleProject, James](https://bibleproject.com/videos/james/)).

Christian tradition names the author as James, "the brother of the Lord" ([Matthew 13:55](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MAT13.htm#V55), [Galatians 1:19](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/GAL01.htm#V19)), who led the church in Jerusalem ([Acts 15:13–21](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ACT15.htm#V13), [Galatians 2:9](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/GAL02.htm#V9)) and, according to the historian Josephus, was stoned to death in AD 62. Scholars are divided. Some hold that he wrote it, perhaps with a secretary who gave it its fine Greek, as one of the earliest writings of the New Testament. Others regard it as a pseudonymous work, and many recent interpreters date it to AD 90–100 ([USCCB, Introduction to the Letter of James](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/james/0)).

In the fourth century, Eusebius of Caesarea counted James among the "disputed" books, "which are nevertheless recognized by many": few early writers mention it, though it was read publicly in very many churches ([Eusebius, Church History 2.23.25](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/250102.htm), [Church History 3.25.3](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/250103.htm)). Every church now reads it as part of the New Testament, where it stands first among the letters that Eusebius called "catholic", or general.

## How the churches read it

Where the churches, or their interpreters, read the book differently, or where it has played a part in their history. Each entry starts with its source's score.

- `10` [Joint Declaration on the Doctrine of Justification, 37–39](https://www.christianunity.va/content/dam/unitacristiani/Relazioni%20ecumeniche/Sezione%20occidentale/Dialogo%20multilaterale/Edizione_20_Anniversario_DCDJ/EN%2020th%20Anniversary%20Edition%20joint_declaration_2019.pdf) (Lutheran World Federation and Catholic Church, 1999, 20th anniversary edition): on faith and works ([James 2:14–26](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JAS02.htm#V14)), Lutherans and Catholics "confess together that good works … follow justification and are its fruits", and that both Jesus and the apostolic Scriptures urge Christians to "bring forth the works of love"; each then sets out its own emphasis on growth in grace. The World Methodist Council (2006), the Anglican Consultative Council (2016) and the World Communion of Reformed Churches (2017) later affirmed the declaration, and the Reformed statement quotes James: faith without works is dead.
- `7` [Timothy J. Wengert, Building on the One Foundation with Straw: Martin Luther and the Epistle of James](https://wordandworld.luthersem.edu/wp-content/uploads/pdfs/35-3_James/Building%20on%20the%20One%20Foundation%20with%20Straw%3B%20Martin%20Luther%20and%20the%20Epistle%20of%20James.pdf) (Word & World, 2015): in the preface to his German New Testament of 1522, **Martin Luther** called James "a real strawy epistle" in comparison with the books that "show you Christ", borrowing Paul's picture of building with straw ([1 Corinthians 3:12](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1CO03.htm#V12)). He doubted that an apostle wrote it, because it reads Abraham's story as justification by works ([James 2:21–23](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JAS02.htm#V21)), where Paul reads it as justification by faith ([Romans 4](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ROM04.htm)), yet he praised it for driving God's law hard, and preached on it. The "straw" remark was left out of every edition of his complete Bible.
- `7` [Council of Trent, Session VI, Decree on Justification, chapter 10](https://www.papalencyclicals.net/councils/trent/sixth-session.htm) (1547): the **Catholic Church** teaches that the justified, "faith co-operating with good works", grow in the justice they have received, and quotes [James 2:24](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JAS02.htm#V24) for it. Canon 24 rejects the view that good works are merely the fruits and signs of justification.
- `7` [Catechism of the Catholic Church, 1510–1513](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P4K.HTM): on the anointing of the sick ([James 5:14–15](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JAS05.htm#V14)), the **Catholic Church** recognises in this rite one of the seven sacraments, "promulgated by James the apostle and brother of the Lord". Once given mostly to the dying as "Extreme Unction", it is now given to those who are seriously ill.
- `7` [Orthodox Church in America, Holy Unction](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/worship/the-sacraments/holy-unction): the **Orthodox churches** celebrate holy unction, quoting James 5:14–16, as a sacrament of healing and forgiveness for the physically and mentally sick. The full rite calls for seven priests, and it is "not the 'last rites'".
- `7` [Church of England, Articles of Religion, XXV](https://www.churchofengland.org/liturgy/book-of-common-prayer/articles-religion#XXV): **Anglican** teaching counts two "Sacraments of the Gospel", baptism and the Lord's Supper, and says that extreme unction is "not to be counted" among them. Yet the Church of England's [Common Worship: Wholeness and Healing](https://www.churchofengland.org/liturgy/common-worship/book/pastoral-services/wholeness-and-healing) gives prayer with laying on of hands or anointing with oil "a proper place within the public prayer of the Church", and the British [Methodist Worship Book's healing services](https://media.methodist.org.uk/media/documents/mwb-healing-and-wholeness-20220227.pdf) also offer anointing with oil and read James 5:13–16.

## Open questions

Questions on which the churches or scholars disagree. These guidelines don't pick a side.

- `7` [USCCB, Introduction to the Letter of James](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/james/0): who wrote the letter, and when? Some scholars hold that James of Jerusalem wrote it, with a secretary's help, before the fall of Jerusalem in AD 70. Others think it a pseudonymous work of AD 90–100, pointing to its polished Greek, its silence about James's own life, and its apparent knowledge of a debate about works later than Paul's.
- `7` [Matthew J. Thomas, Justification, 3.4: Justification in canonical context](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Christianity/Justification#section3.4) (St Andrews Encyclopaedia of Theology): how does "by works a man is justified, and not only by faith" ([James 2:24](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JAS02.htm#V24)) fit with Paul's "a man is justified by faith apart from the works of the law" ([Romans 3:28](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ROM03.htm#V28))? Most interpreters read the two as compatible: they speak of different moments of justification, of different kinds of works, or of different kinds of faith. Others conclude that the two contradict each other.
