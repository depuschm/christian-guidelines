# Galatians

Paul's Letter to the Galatians is addressed "to the assemblies of Galatia" ([Galatians 1:2](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/GAL01.htm#V2)). The introduction to the New American Bible describes its readers as Paul's own converts from paganism ([Galatians 4:8–9](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/GAL04.htm#V8)), who were being urged by other missionaries to add the observances of the Jewish law, including circumcision, to faith in Christ as a means of salvation. Paul wrote to defend his authority as an apostle and the gospel he had preached: the unique importance of Christ's cross, freedom from the burdens of the law, the sufficiency of faith in Christ, and the new life of the Spirit. The introduction calls the letter "a summary of basic Pauline theology", whose themes the Letter to the Romans develops "more fully and less polemically" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Letter to the Galatians](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/galatians/0)).

The letter is also Paul's own account of his life. He tells how God called him and revealed his Son to him ([Galatians 1:11–24](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/GAL01.htm#V11)), how James, Cephas and John, "reputed to be pillars", gave him "the right hand of fellowship" for his mission to the Gentiles ([Galatians 2:1–10](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/GAL02.htm#V1)), and how he opposed Cephas, that is Peter, to his face at Antioch, when Peter stopped eating with Gentile believers ([Galatians 2:11–14](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/GAL02.htm#V11)). Paul ends the letter in his own hand ([Galatians 6:11](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/GAL06.htm#V11)). Scholars disagree about whether Paul wrote every letter that bears his name, but Galatians is among the letters that are generally agreed to be his ([Bible Odyssey, Paul](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/paul/)). Which churches in Galatia it was written to, and when, is an open question below. Every church reads it in the New Testament among Paul's letters.

## How the churches read it

Where the churches, or their interpreters, read the book differently, or where it has played a part in their history. Each entry starts with its source's score.

- `10` [Joint Declaration on the Doctrine of Justification, 31–33](https://www.christianunity.va/content/dam/unitacristiani/Relazioni%20ecumeniche/Sezione%20occidentale/Dialogo%20multilaterale/Edizione_20_Anniversario_DCDJ/EN%2020th%20Anniversary%20Edition%20joint_declaration_2019.pdf) (Lutheran World Federation and Catholic Church, 1999, 20th anniversary edition): on **the law and justification**, Lutherans and Catholics confess together that people are justified by faith in the gospel apart from works of the law, that Christ "has fulfilled the law and by his death and resurrection has overcome it as a way to salvation", and that "God's commandments retain their validity for the justified". The declaration describes justification as liberation "from the curse of the law" ([Galatians 3:10–14](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/GAL03.htm#V10)) and the life of the justified as faith "active through love" ([Galatians 5:6](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/GAL05.htm#V6)). Lutherans add that the distinction of law and gospel is essential, and Catholics that the righteous are bound to observe God's commandments.
- `7` [Martin Luther, Commentary on St. Paul's Epistle to the Galatians](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/luther/galatians.html) (translated by Theodore Graebner, 1939, Christian Classics Ethereal Library): **Luther**'s lectures on Galatians of 1531, which the translator's introduction says were his favourite biblical book: "The Epistle to the Galatians is my epistle." On justification Luther answers with Paul: "By faith only in Christ are we pronounced righteous, and not by works", and adds, "Not that we reject good works." The CCEL summary says the commentary presents "the justification of the sinner for the sake of Christ's merits alone" like no other of his writings.
- `7` [Council of Trent, Session VI, Decree on Justification, chapter 7](https://www.papalencyclicals.net/councils/trent/sixth-session.htm) (1547): the **Catholic Church** teaches that in justification faith, hope and charity are given together, since faith without hope and charity does not unite a person perfectly with Christ, and quotes Galatians: "In Christ Jesus neither circumcision, availeth anything, nor uncircumcision, but faith which worketh by charity" ([Galatians 5:6](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/GAL05.htm#V6)).
- `7` [Formula of Concord, Epitome, VI: The Third Use of the Law](https://bookofconcord.org/epitome/third-use-of-the-law/) (1577): on **the law in the Christian life**, the **Lutheran** Formula of Concord teaches that believers "have been freed and exempted from the curse and coercion of the Law" but are not "without Law", and that the good works the Spirit works in them are "fruits of the Spirit", citing [Galatians 6:2](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/GAL06.htm#V2). The **Westminster Confession** holds that true believers are not under the law "as a covenant of works", but that it serves them "as a rule of life" ([Westminster Confession of Faith, 19.6](https://www.opc.org/wcf.html)). The **Catechism of the Catholic Church** calls the New Law of the gospel "a law of freedom", which sets believers free "from the ritual and juridical observances of the Old Law" ([Catechism of the Catholic Church, 1972](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P6W.HTM)).

## Open questions

Questions on which the churches or scholars disagree. These guidelines don't pick a side.

- `7` [Matthew J. Thomas, Justification, 3.3: The means of justification](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Christianity/Justification#section3.3) (St Andrews Encyclopaedia of Theology): does Paul speak of "faith in Christ" or "the faith of Christ"? The Greek phrase *pistis Christou* ([Galatians 2:16](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/GAL02.htm#V16)) can be read as faith in Christ, as Christ's own faith or faithfulness, or as Christian faith, and the article calls the debate "voluminous". Translations differ: the WEBBE and the New American Bible read "faith in Jesus Christ", while the NRSVUE reads "the faith of Jesus Christ", with "faith in Jesus Christ" in a footnote ([Bible Gateway, Galatians 1–6 (NRSVUE)](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Galatians%201-6&version=NRSVUE)).
- `7` [USCCB, Introduction to the Letter to the Galatians](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/galatians/0): to whom, and when, was the letter written? The introduction thinks the readers most likely lived in the north, around Ancyra (modern Ankara), and that Paul wrote around AD 54 or 55, probably from Ephesus; some scholars think the letter went to Paul's churches in the south of the Roman province of Galatia, which would date it earlier, perhaps AD 48–50. The question bears on how Paul's visit to Jerusalem in [Galatians 2:1–10](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/GAL02.htm#V1) relates to the council in [Acts 15](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ACT15.htm).
- `7` [Shelly Matthews, Baptism's Effect on Social Relationships](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/baptisms-effect-on-social-relationships/) (Bible Odyssey, 2021): what follows for the church's life from "neither Jew nor Greek ... neither slave nor free man ... neither male nor female" ([Galatians 3:28](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/GAL03.htm#V28))? Matthews writes that many scholars see a baptismal formula older than Paul here, that how far it changed social relations "seems to have been argued about from the start", and that in the fourth century it was cited in support of women's leadership in churches. The churches still read it differently on the roles of husbands and wives ([Marriage, family and singleness](../../general/family.md#open-questions)) and on the ordination of women, as the church pages show for the [Anglican](../../traditions/anglican.md#differences-within-the-tradition), [Lutheran](../../traditions/lutheran.md#differences-within-the-tradition), [Methodist](../../traditions/methodist.md#differences-within-the-tradition) and [Reformed](../../traditions/reformed.md#differences-within-the-tradition) churches.
