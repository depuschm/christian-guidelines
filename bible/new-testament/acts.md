# Acts

The Acts of the Apostles is the second part of a two-volume work. It opens by addressing Theophilus and recalling "the first book" about all that Jesus "began both to do and to teach" ([Acts 1:1–2](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ACT01.htm#V1), [Luke 1:1–4](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/LUK01.htm#V1)). The introduction to the New American Bible describes it as a continuation of Luke's account, showing how the salvation promised to Israel and accomplished by Jesus "has now under the guidance of the holy Spirit been extended to the Gentiles" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Acts of the Apostles](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/acts/0)). Its programme is set out in Jesus' last words before the ascension: "You will be witnesses to me in Jerusalem, in all Judea and Samaria, and to the uttermost parts of the earth" ([Acts 1:8](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ACT01.htm#V8)). The story runs from Jerusalem to Rome, where it ends with Paul under house arrest, preaching "with all boldness, without hindrance" ([Acts 28:30–31](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ACT28.htm#V30)).

Early Christian tradition, from the late second century on, names the author of both the Gospel of Luke and Acts as Luke, the companion of Paul mentioned in [Colossians 4:14](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/COL04.htm#V14), [Philemon 24](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/PHM01.htm#V24) and [2 Timothy 4:11](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2TI04.htm#V11) ([USCCB, Introduction to the Gospel according to Luke](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/luke/0)). In four passages the narrative changes from "they" to "we" ([Acts 16:10–17](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ACT16.htm#V10), [Acts 20:5–15](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ACT20.htm#V5), [Acts 21:1–18](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ACT21.htm#V1), [Acts 27:1–28:16](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ACT27.htm#V1)). The New American Bible's notes say that scholars debate whether this is a literary device, or whether the "we" includes Luke or another companion of Paul whose account Luke used ([USCCB, Acts 16, note on 16:10–17](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/acts/16)). For the date, its introduction refers to the Gospel of Luke, which most scholars date after AD 70, many to AD 80–90; some scholars date Acts as late as the second century ([USCCB, Introduction to the Gospel according to Luke](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/luke/0), [Todd Penner, Paul and Acts](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/paul-and-acts/)).

The introduction asks readers to weigh Luke's history "without either exaggerating their literal accuracy or underestimating their factual worth": Luke tells it as the story of a Spirit-guided community through which the word of God spreads ([USCCB, Introduction to the Acts of the Apostles](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/acts/0)). Acts is in the New Testament of every church.

## Outline

The New American Bible divides Acts into the preparation for the mission ([Acts 1:1–2:13](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ACT01.htm#V1)), the mission in Jerusalem ([Acts 2:14–8:3](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ACT02.htm#V14)), the mission in Judea and Samaria ([Acts 8:4–9:43](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ACT08.htm#V4)), the beginning of the mission to the Gentiles ([Acts 10:1–15:35](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ACT10.htm#V1)) and the mission of Paul to the ends of the earth ([Acts 15:36–28:31](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ACT15.htm#V36)) ([USCCB, Introduction to the Acts of the Apostles](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/acts/0)). ## How the churches read it

Where the churches, or their interpreters, read the book differently, or where it has played a part in their history. Each entry starts with its source's score.

- `9` [World Council of Churches, The Church: Towards a Common Vision, 30](https://www.oikoumene.org/sites/default/files/Document/The_Church_Towards_a_common_vision.pdf): the churches together read the meeting in Jerusalem ([Acts 15:1–35](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ACT15.htm#V1)) as the first time the church, "with the aid of the Holy Spirit", faced a question of unity and diversity, and find in its letter, "it seemed good to the Holy Spirit and to us" ([Acts 15:28](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ACT15.htm#V28)), "a fundamental principle governing unity and diversity", which later ecumenical councils such as Nicaea followed. The **Reformed** Westminster Confession holds that synods and councils are needed to settle controversies, but that all councils "since the Apostles' times … may err", and so are "a help" rather than "the rule of faith, or practice" ([Westminster Confession of Faith, 31](https://www.opc.org/wcf.html)).
- `8` [Church of God (Cleveland, Tennessee), Declaration of Faith](https://churchofgod.org/beliefs/declaration-of-faith/): **Pentecostal** churches read Pentecost ([Acts 2:1–13](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ACT02.htm#V1)) and the later outpourings of the Spirit in Samaria, Caesarea and Ephesus ([Acts 8:14–17](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ACT08.htm#V14), [Acts 10:44–48](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ACT10.htm#V44), [Acts 19:1–7](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ACT19.htm#V1)) as showing a baptism in the Spirit for believers today. The Church of God confesses "the baptism with the Holy Ghost subsequent to a clean heart", with speaking in tongues as its "initial evidence", and Elim speaks of "an enduement of power as the gift of Christ through the baptism in the Holy Spirit with signs following" ([Elim, Our Beliefs](https://www.elim.org.uk/Articles/417857/Our_Beliefs.aspx)). The St Andrews Encyclopaedia of Theology explains that Pentecostals understand this gift chiefly as power for witness ([Acts 1:8](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ACT01.htm#V8)), and have long debated whether it must follow conversion in time ([St Andrews Encyclopaedia of Theology, Pentecostal Theology, 3.3](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Christianity/PentecostalTheology#section3.3)).
- `7` [Catechism of the Catholic Church, 1286–1292](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P3Q.HTM): on the same passages, the **Catholic Church** teaches that the apostles "imparted to the newly baptized by the laying on of hands the gift of the Spirit that completes the grace of Baptism", and recognises in this the origin of the sacrament of confirmation, "which in a certain way perpetuates the grace of Pentecost in the Church" (1288). The **Orthodox** churches, citing [Acts 8:14–17](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ACT08.htm#V14) and [Acts 19:1–7](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ACT19.htm#V1), celebrate chrismation with baptism as "our personal participation in Pentecost" ([Orthodox Church in America, Chrismation](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/worship/the-sacraments/chrismation)). The **Baptist** Faith and Message holds that "at the moment of regeneration" the Spirit "baptizes every believer into the Body of Christ" ([Baptist Faith and Message 2000, II](https://bfm.sbc.net/bfm2000/#ii)).
- `7` [St Andrews Encyclopaedia of Theology, Baptism, 4.2](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Christianity/Baptism#section4.2): the baptisms in Acts are read in both directions in the debate over infant baptism. Those who baptise only believers point out that the baptisms Acts reports are of people who repent and believe ([Acts 2:38](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ACT02.htm#V38), [Acts 8:36–38](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ACT08.htm#V36)); those who also baptise infants point to the baptism of whole households ([Acts 16:14–15](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ACT16.htm#V14), [Acts 16:30–34](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ACT16.htm#V30)). The Catechism of the Catholic Church holds it "quite possible" that infants were baptised when whole households were ([Catechism of the Catholic Church, 1252](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P3K.HTM)). The churches' positions are set out under the open questions of [Worship and the sacraments](../../general/worship.md#open-questions).
- `7` [Hutterites, Summary of Beliefs](https://hutterites.org/our-beliefs/summary-beliefs/): many **Hutterites** refer to the first community in Acts, where the believers "had all things in common" ([Acts 2:44–45](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ACT02.htm#V44), [Acts 4:32–35](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ACT04.htm#V32)), as "the primary biblical basis for communal living", and share both spiritual and material goods in community. Other churches read the passages as a call to generous sharing rather than common ownership: the Catholic Catechism, quoting "They had everything in common", teaches that a Christian is "a steward of the Lord's goods" ([Catechism of the Catholic Church, 952](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P2B.HTM)); the Westminster Confession says the communion of saints does not "take away, or infringe the title or propriety which each man hath in his goods and possessions" ([Westminster Confession of Faith, 26.3](https://www.opc.org/wcf.html)); and Anglican Article XXXVIII holds that Christians' goods "are not common", while every Christian ought "liberally to give alms to the poor" ([Church of England, Articles of Religion, XXXVIII](https://www.churchofengland.org/liturgy/book-of-common-prayer/articles-religion#XXXVIII)).

## Open questions

Questions on which the churches or scholars disagree. These guidelines don't pick a side.

- `7` [USCCB, Introduction to the Gospel according to Luke](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/luke/0): who wrote Acts, and when? Early tradition names Luke, Paul's companion, as the author of both volumes, and some read the "we" passages as his own presence on Paul's journeys; others think the "we" is a literary device or comes from a source ([USCCB, Acts 16, note on 16:10–17](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/acts/16)). Most scholars date the Gospel of Luke after AD 70, many to AD 80–90, with Acts after it; some date Acts to the second century ([Todd Penner, Paul and Acts](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/paul-and-acts/)).
- `7` [Todd Penner, Paul and Acts](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/paul-and-acts/) (Bible Odyssey): how far does Acts portray Paul and the first churches as they actually were? Acts and Paul's letters agree on much, but differ too: Paul's teaching on justification by faith is absent from his speeches in Acts, and the meeting in Jerusalem looks different in [Acts 15:1–35](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ACT15.htm#V1) and [Galatians 2:1–10](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/GAL02.htm#V1). Some scholars hold that Luke, who may have travelled with Paul, gives an accurate account; others that he shaped his portrait of Paul to reconcile Jewish and Gentile believers; others that Acts was written in the second century, using Paul's letters as a source.
