# Mark

The Gospel according to Mark tells the story of Jesus' ministry, death and resurrection. It opens with "The beginning of the Good News of Jesus Christ, the Son of God" ([Mark 1:1](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MRK01.htm#V1)), and sums up Jesus' preaching in one sentence: "The time is fulfilled, and God's Kingdom is at hand! Repent, and believe in the Good News" ([Mark 1:14–15](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MRK01.htm#V14)). The introduction to the New American Bible calls it the shortest of the gospels and likely the first to be written, a vivid, "almost breathless narrative" of Jesus' deeds in which Jesus is "the Son whom God has sent to rescue humanity by serving and by sacrificing his life" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Gospel according to Mark](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/mark/0), [Mark 10:45](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MRK10.htm#V45)).

The book is anonymous apart from the ancient heading "According to Mark". Christian tradition assigns it to John Mark, in whose mother's house the Jerusalem church met ([Acts 12:12](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ACT12.htm#V12)) and who appears with Paul and with Peter ([Colossians 4:10](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/COL04.htm#V10), [1 Peter 5:13](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1PE05.htm#V13)). Around AD 135, Papias recorded that "Mark, having become the interpreter of Peter, wrote down accurately, though not in order, whatsoever he remembered of the things said or done by Christ" ([Eusebius, Church History 3.39](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/250103.htm)). Tradition dates the gospel shortly before AD 70 and places it in Rome; modern research often proposes an unknown Hellenistic Jewish Christian, possibly in Syria, writing shortly after 70 ([USCCB, Introduction to the Gospel according to Mark](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/mark/0)). Most scholars agree that Mark was a source for the Gospels of Matthew and Luke ([Bible Odyssey, Did the Authors of the Canonical Gospels Know Each Other?](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/did-the-authors-of-the-canonical-gospels-know-each-other/)).

Mark is in the New Testament of every church. In the most ancient manuscripts it ends at the empty tomb, with the women saying nothing to anyone, "for they were afraid" ([Mark 16:8](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MRK16.htm#V8)); most manuscripts continue with the "longer ending" ([Mark 16:9–20](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MRK16.htm#V9)), which the WEBBE prints (see "Open questions").

## Outline

The USCCB introduction divides the gospel into the preparation for Jesus' ministry, the mystery of Jesus, its gradual revelation and its full revelation, followed by the endings added later. The table follows the gospel's larger units.

| Passage | Contents |
| --- | --- |
| [Mark 1:1–13](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MRK01.htm#V1) | John the Baptist prepares the way; Jesus is baptised, the Spirit descends and a voice from heaven calls him "my beloved Son"; Jesus is tempted in the wilderness. |
| [Mark 1:14–3:6](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MRK01.htm#V14) | Jesus proclaims God's Kingdom in Galilee, calls his first disciples, heals, forgives sins and eats with sinners, and meets growing opposition. |
| [Mark 3:7–6:6](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MRK03.htm#V7) | The Twelve are appointed; parables of the Kingdom; Jesus calms the storm, frees the possessed and raises a girl from death; he is rejected in Nazareth. |
| [Mark 6:7–8:26](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MRK06.htm#V7) | The Twelve are sent out; John the Baptist is killed; Jesus feeds the crowds, walks on the water, teaches about what makes a person unclean and heals among the Gentiles. |
| [Mark 8:27–9:1](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MRK08.htm#V27) | Peter confesses Jesus as the Christ; Jesus first foretells his suffering and death, and calls his followers to take up their cross. |
| [Mark 9:2–10:52](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MRK09.htm#V2) | The transfiguration; on the way to Jerusalem Jesus teaches about greatness, marriage, children and wealth, foretells his death twice more, and heals blind Bartimaeus. |
| [Mark 11:1–13:37](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MRK11.htm#V1) | In Jerusalem: the entry into the city, the cleansing of the temple, disputes with the authorities, the great commandment, the widow's offering, and the discourse on the end and the coming of the Son of Man. |
| [Mark 14:1–15:47](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MRK14.htm#V1) | The passion: the anointing at Bethany, the Last Supper, Gethsemane, the arrest and trials, Peter's denial, the crucifixion and the burial. |
| [Mark 16:1–8](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MRK16.htm#V1) | The women find the tomb empty, and a young man tells them that Jesus has been raised and is going before the disciples to Galilee. |
| [Mark 16:9–20](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MRK16.htm#V9) | The longer ending: appearances of the risen Jesus, the sending of the disciples to preach to the whole creation, and the ascension. |

## How the churches read it

Where the churches, or their interpreters, read the book differently, or where it has played a part in their history. Each entry starts with its source's score.

- `9` [Baptism, Eucharist and Ministry, Eucharist 1 and 13](https://www.oikoumene.org/sites/default/files/Document/FO1982_111_en.pdf) (World Council of Churches, 1982): the churches' agreed text cites Mark's account of the Last Supper ([Mark 14:22–25](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MRK14.htm#V22)) among the accounts of the institution of the eucharist, and says that "the words and acts of Christ at the institution of the eucharist stand at the heart of the celebration". How Christ is present in the Lord's Supper, and whether it is a sacrifice, is an open question in [Worship and the sacraments](../../general/worship.md).
- `7` [Catechism of the Catholic Church, 1257](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P3M.HTM): the **Catholic Church** teaches that "Baptism is necessary for salvation for those to whom the Gospel has been proclaimed and who have had the possibility of asking for this sacrament", citing [Mark 16:16](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MRK16.htm#V16), "He who believes and is baptised will be saved", from the longer ending. **Luther's Small Catechism** quotes the same verse as the promise that makes baptism give "forgiveness of sins" and "eternal salvation to all who believe" ([Luther's Small Catechism, The Sacrament of Holy Baptism](https://bookofconcord.org/small-catechism/the-sacrament-of-holy-baptism/)). Whom to baptise is an open question in [Worship and the sacraments](../../general/worship.md).
- `7` [Catechism of the Catholic Church, 2380–2391](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P87.HTM): on Jesus' teaching about divorce ([Mark 10:2–12](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MRK10.htm#V2)), the **Catholic Church** teaches that a ratified and consummated marriage between the baptised cannot be dissolved, while the **Orthodox churches** have a service of second marriage "through penance" ([Orthodox Church in America, Marriage](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/worship/the-sacraments/marriage)), and the **Westminster Confession** allows divorce for adultery or desertion that "can no way be remedied" ([Westminster Confession of Faith, 24.5–24.6](https://www.opc.org/wcf.html)). The question is set out in [Marriage, family and singleness](../../general/family.md).

## Open questions

Questions on which the churches or scholars disagree, for example about the book's authorship or the meaning of a passage. These guidelines don't pick a side.

- `7` [USCCB, Introduction to the Gospel according to Mark](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/mark/0): who wrote the gospel, where and when? Tradition, from Papias onwards, names John Mark, Peter's "interpreter", writing in Rome shortly before AD 70; the introduction adds that Petrine influence "should not, however, be exaggerated". Modern research often proposes an unknown Hellenistic Jewish Christian, possibly in Syria, writing shortly after 70.
- `7` [Mark 16 in the NRSVUE, with notes](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark%2016&version=NRSVUE) (Bible Gateway): how did Mark end? The NRSVUE notes that "some of the most ancient authorities bring the book to a close at the end of 16.8", that one ends instead with a shorter "intermediate ending", and that in most the longer ending follows, sometimes marked as doubtful. The New American Bible notes that the longer ending "has traditionally been accepted as a canonical part of the gospel and was defined as such by the Council of Trent", though its "vocabulary and style indicate that it was written by someone other than Mark" ([USCCB, Mark 16, note on 16:9–20](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/mark/16)). It adds that the abrupt end at 16:8 "causes some to believe that the original ending of this gospel may have been lost".
- `7` [Sarah E. Rollens, Did the Authors of the Canonical Gospels Know Each Other?](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/did-the-authors-of-the-canonical-gospels-know-each-other/) (Bible Odyssey): how are Mark, Matthew and Luke related, the "Synoptic Problem"? Most scholars hold that Matthew and Luke used Mark. The Two-Source Hypothesis adds a lost sayings source, "Q", for the material Matthew and Luke share; the Farrer Hypothesis has one of them use the other instead; the Griesbach Hypothesis has Mark combine Matthew and Luke.
