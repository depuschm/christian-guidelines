# 1 Timothy

The First Letter to Timothy is written in the name of Paul, "an apostle of Jesus Christ", to "Timothy, my true child in faith" ([1 Timothy 1:1–2](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1TI01.htm#V1)). Acts introduces Timothy as a disciple at Lystra, "the son of a Jewess who believed, but his father was a Greek", whom Paul took with him on his travels ([Acts 16:1–3](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ACT16.htm#V1)). The letter says that Paul urged him to stay at Ephesus "that you might command certain men not to teach a different doctrine" ([1 Timothy 1:3](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1TI01.htm#V3)), and it states its purpose: "that you may know how men ought to behave themselves in God's house, which is the assembly of the living God, the pillar and ground of the truth" ([1 Timothy 3:14–15](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1TI03.htm#V14)). Between these it deals with prayer and worship, with choosing overseers and servants (the offices the New American Bible's introduction calls bishop and deacon), with widows and elders, with slaves and masters, and with the duties of the rich ([USCCB, Introduction to the First Letter to Timothy](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/1timothy/0)).

With 2 Timothy and Titus, the letter belongs to the Pastoral Epistles, a name given in the eighteenth century "because they all are concerned with the work of a pastor in caring for the community or communities under his charge". The same introduction notes that "from the late second century to the nineteenth, Pauline authorship of the three Pastoral Epistles went unchallenged". Since then, most scholars have been convinced that Paul could not have been responsible for their vocabulary and style, their view of church organisation or their theology, while others hold that the vocabulary and style are Paul's after all, or suggest that he used a secretary, or that a later compiler drew on traditions about Paul and fragments of his letters. If Paul wrote them, they date from AD 63–67; if not, perhaps from as late as the early second century. "In spite of these problems of authorship and dating, the Pastorals are illustrative of early Christian life and remain an important element of canonical scripture" ([USCCB, Introduction to the First Letter to Timothy](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/1timothy/0)). Bible Odyssey lists 1 and 2 Timothy and Titus among the New Testament letters written in Paul's name in which scholars find pseudepigraphy, writing in the name of another ([Bible Odyssey, Pseudepigraphy](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/pseudepigraphy/)). The letter is in the New Testament of every church ([The Bible: which books each church reads](../README.md#which-books-each-church-reads)).

## How the churches read it

Where the churches, or their interpreters, read the book differently, or where it has played a part in their history. Each entry starts with its source's score.

- `7` [Catechism of the Catholic Church, 1554–1571](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P4U.HTM): on the **overseers, elders and servants** of [1 Timothy 3:1–13](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1TI03.htm#V1) and [1 Timothy 5:17–22](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1TI05.htm#V17), and the gift given "with the laying on of the hands of the elders" ([1 Timothy 4:14](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1TI04.htm#V14)), the churches order their ministry differently. The **Catholic Church** teaches that "the divinely instituted ecclesiastical ministry is exercised in different degrees by those who even from ancient times have been called bishops, priests, and deacons", and that the apostles "by the imposition of hands" passed on "the gift of the Spirit", citing this verse. The **Orthodox** Church in America teaches that "the bishops, priests and deacons give order to the Church" and "guarantee the continuity and unity of the Church from age to age" ([Orthodox Church in America, Holy Orders](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/worship/the-sacraments/holy-orders)). The **Lutheran** Augsburg Confession holds that "no one should publicly teach in the Church or administer the Sacraments unless he be regularly called" ([Augsburg Confession, XIV](https://bookofconcord.org/augsburg-confession/of-ecclesiastical-order/)). The **Presbyterian Church in America** holds that the titles bishop, pastor, presbyter and elder "do not indicate different grades of office, but all describe one and the same office", and examines its elders and deacons on "the qualifications set out in 1 Timothy 3:1-7 and Titus 1:6-9" ([PCA Book of Church Order, 8-1 and 24-1](https://www.pcaac.org/book-of-church-order/part-1-the-form-of-government/)); the **Southern Baptist Convention** names two scriptural offices, "pastor/elder/overseer and deacon", citing 1 Timothy 3:1–15 ([Baptist Faith and Message 2000, VI](https://bfm.sbc.net/bfm2000/#vi)). How the church should be ordered is an open question in [Belonging to a church](../../general/church.md#open-questions).
- `7` [Augsburg Confession, XXIII: Of the Marriage of Priests](https://bookofconcord.org/augsburg-confession/of-marriage-of-priests/): on the overseer as **"the husband of one wife"** ([1 Timothy 3:2](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1TI03.htm#V2)), the **Lutheran** reformers argued that "in the ancient Church priests were married men. For Paul says, 1 Tim. 3:2, that a bishop should be chosen who is the husband of one wife", and they cited [1 Timothy 4:3](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1TI04.htm#V3) against forbidding marriage. In the **Latin Catholic Church**, ordained ministers other than permanent deacons are normally chosen from men "who live a celibate life", while in the **Eastern Churches** "married men can be ordained as deacons and priests" and bishops are chosen from celibates ([Catechism of the Catholic Church, 1579–1580](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P4X.HTM)). The **Orthodox** Church in America says that "a married man may be ordained to the priesthood", but "his marriage, however, must be the first for both him and his wife" ([Orthodox Church in America, Holy Orders](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/worship/the-sacraments/holy-orders)). Whether clergy must be celibate is an open question in [Marriage, family and singleness](../../general/family.md#open-questions).
- `7` [Luther's Small Catechism: Table of Duties](https://bookofconcord.org/small-catechism/table-of-duties/): the **Lutheran** Small Catechism ends with "certain passages of scripture for various holy orders and positions", much of it from this letter: the qualities of a bishop ([1 Timothy 3:2–7](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1TI03.htm#V2)), the "double honor" owed to elders who labour in the Word ([1 Timothy 5:17–18](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1TI05.htm#V17)), the duty of widows ([1 Timothy 5:5–6](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1TI05.htm#V5)), and, for all in common, to "persevere in prayer for all men" ([1 Timothy 2:1–2](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1TI02.htm#V1)).

## Open questions

Questions on which the churches or scholars disagree. These guidelines don't pick a side.

- `7` [USCCB, 1 Timothy, chapter 2, with notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/1timothy/2) (New American Bible, Revised Edition): what do "Let a woman learn in quietness with full submission" and "I don't permit a woman to teach, nor to exercise authority over a man" ([1 Timothy 2:11–12](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1TI02.htm#V11)) mean for the church today? The notes read the verses as rules for the liturgical assembly, in which women were not to take part in "the charismatic activity of the assembly" or exercise authority, compare them with [1 Corinthians 14:34](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1CO14.htm#V34), and note that "a man" "could also mean 'her husband'". Behind this lies a wider question on which the churches differ: who may be ordained. The **Catholic Church** teaches that "only a baptized man (vir) validly receives sacred ordination" ([Catechism of the Catholic Church, 1577](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P4X.HTM)), and an answer on the **Orthodox** Church in America's website says that "the Orthodox Church precludes the ordination of women to the priesthood and episcopacy", as "a matter of Holy Tradition" ([Orthodox Church in America, Ordination of Women](https://www.oca.org/questions/priesthoodmonasticism/ordination-of-women)). The **Southern Baptist Convention** holds, citing this chapter, that "while both men and women are gifted for service in the church, the office of pastor/elder/overseer is limited to men as qualified by Scripture" ([Baptist Faith and Message 2000, VI](https://bfm.sbc.net/bfm2000/#vi)), and the **Presbyterian Church in America** that its offices are "open to men only" ([PCA Book of Church Order, 7-2](https://www.pcaac.org/book-of-church-order/part-1-the-form-of-government/)). The **Methodist Church in Britain** has ordained women as presbyters since 1974 ([Methodist Church in Britain, 50 years of women's ordination](https://www.methodist.org.uk/about/news/the-methodist-church-in-great-britain-celebrates-50-years-of-womens-ordination-as-ministers/)), predecessors of the **Presbyterian Church (U.S.A.)** have ordained women as ministers since 1956 ([Presbyterian Mission Agency, Women in the church](https://pma.pcusa.org/ministries/101/women/)), and the **Church of England** made it possible for women to be bishops in 2014 ([Church of England, Legislation on women bishops](https://www.churchofengland.org/news/legislation-women-bishops-becomes-law-general-synod)).
- `7` [USCCB, Introduction to the First Letter to Timothy](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/1timothy/0) (New American Bible, Revised Edition): who wrote the Pastoral Epistles, and when? The introduction sets out four views: most scholars hold that Paul could not have written them; a second group holds that their vocabulary and style are Paul's, and sees their theology as close to that of his undisputed letters; others suggest that Paul used a secretary; and a fourth group sees them as the work of a compiler, based on traditions about Paul's later years and including fragments of his letters. It dates them to AD 63–67 if Paul wrote them, or as late as the early second century if not. Bible Odyssey lists the letters among those in which scholars find pseudepigraphy, and notes that "a significant motivation for pseudepigraphy was interpretation" of famous figures from the past ([Bible Odyssey, Pseudepigraphy](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/pseudepigraphy/)). The letter itself names Paul as its sender ([1 Timothy 1:1–2](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1TI01.htm#V1)).
