# Romans

Paul's Letter to the Romans is the longest of his letters. It opens with greetings from "Paul, a servant of Jesus Christ, called to be an apostle" ([Romans 1:1](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ROM01.htm#V1)) to the Christians in Rome, a church he had not founded and had not yet visited, though he had long wanted to ([Romans 1:8–15](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ROM01.htm#V8), [Romans 15:22–29](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ROM15.htm#V22)). The introduction to the New American Bible calls it "the longest and most systematic unfolding of the apostle's thought". It dates the letter to between AD 56 and 58, written from Greece, likely Corinth, as Paul was about to take a collection to the poor believers in Jerusalem and hoped to go on to Rome and from there to Spain ([USCCB, Introduction to the Letter to the Romans](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/romans/0), [Romans 15:24–28](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ROM15.htm#V24)). A scribe named Tertius wrote it down ([Romans 16:22](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ROM16.htm#V22)).

The church in Rome was older than Paul's letter. The same introduction notes an edict of the Emperor Claudius around AD 49 expelling Jews from Rome, which brought Prisca and Aquila to Corinth ([Acts 18:2](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ACT18.htm#V2)), and says that opinions vary on whether Jewish or Gentile believers were the majority in the house churches of Rome. Paul writes to both, and sets out the gospel he preaches: God's power to save everyone who believes, Jew and Gentile alike ([USCCB, Introduction to the Letter to the Romans](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/romans/0)).

Scholars disagree about whether Paul wrote every letter that bears his name, but not about Romans: the letters that many scholars regard as written in Paul's name by others are Ephesians, Colossians and the letters to Timothy and Titus ([Bible Odyssey, Paul](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/paul/), [Bible Odyssey, Pseudepigraphy](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/pseudepigraphy/)). The USCCB introduction says that of all Paul's letters, Romans "has long held pride of place" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Letter to the Romans](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/romans/0)). It stands first among Paul's letters in the New Testament.

## How the churches read it

Where the churches, or their interpreters, read the book differently, or where it has played a part in their history. Each entry starts with its source's score.

- `10` [Joint Declaration on the Doctrine of Justification, 10 and 15](https://www.christianunity.va/content/dam/unitacristiani/Relazioni%20ecumeniche/Sezione%20occidentale/Dialogo%20multilaterale/Edizione_20_Anniversario_DCDJ/EN%2020th%20Anniversary%20Edition%20joint_declaration_2019.pdf) (Lutheran World Federation and Catholic Church, 1999, 20th anniversary edition): on **justification by faith** ([Romans 3:21–28](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ROM03.htm#V21)), Lutherans and Catholics set out Paul's message in Romans together, from the gospel as "the power of God for salvation" to Abraham's faith, and confess: "By grace alone, in faith in Christ's saving work and not because of any merit on our part, we are accepted by God". The World Methodist Council, which later affirmed the declaration, says of [Romans 4:16](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ROM04.htm#V16) that it "could well be the motto of the Methodist movement".
- `8` [Christian Reformed Church in North America, The Canons of Dort, First Main Point of Doctrine, 9–10](https://www.crcna.org/welcome/beliefs/confessions/canons-dort) (1618–1619): on **election** ([Romans 9:11–13](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ROM09.htm#V11)), the **Reformed** Canons teach that God's election is "not on the basis of foreseen faith" but rests "exclusively" on "the good pleasure of God", citing this passage. The Synod of Dort wrote them against the followers of Jacob Arminius, who taught election based on foreseen faith. **John Wesley** rejected the reading of "I will have mercy on whom I have mercy" ([Romans 9:15](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ROM09.htm#V15)) as meaning that God has mercy only on the elect, and taught that God's grace is "a free gift, in all, and for all" ([Wesley, Sermon 128: Free Grace](https://wesley.nnu.edu/john-wesley/the-sermons-of-john-wesley-1872-edition/sermon-128-free-grace/), [St Andrews Encyclopaedia of Theology, Methodist Theology, 1.3](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Christianity/MethodistTheology#section1.3)). The Catholic Catechism teaches that God's eternal plan of "predestination" includes "each person's free response to his grace" ([Catechism of the Catholic Church, 600](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P1O.HTM)).
- `8` [Second Vatican Council, Nostra aetate, 4](https://www.vatican.va/archive/hist_councils/ii_vatican_council/documents/vat-ii_decl_19651028_nostra-aetate_en.html) (1965): on **the Jewish people** ([Romans 11:28–29](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ROM11.htm#V28)), the Catholic Church teaches, "such is the witness of the Apostle", that "God holds the Jews most dear for the sake of their Fathers; He does not repent of the gifts He makes or of the calls He issues". The Catechism links Christ's coming in glory to "his recognition by 'all Israel'" ([Catechism of the Catholic Church, 674](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P1V.HTM)).
- `7` [Martin Luther, Preface to the Letter of St. Paul to the Romans](https://www.ccel.org/l/luther/romans/pref_romans.html) (translated by Andrew Thornton from Luther's German Bible of 1545): **Luther** called Romans "truly the most important piece in the New Testament. It is purest Gospel", and taught from it that "faith alone makes someone just and fulfills the law", and that good works then "proceed from faith itself". The Augsburg Confession, citing Romans 3 and 4, teaches that people are "freely justified for Christ's sake, through faith" ([Augsburg Confession, IV](https://bookofconcord.org/augsburg-confession/of-justification/)).
- `7` [Council of Trent, Session VI, Decree on Justification, chapter 8](https://www.papalencyclicals.net/councils/trent/sixth-session.htm) (1547): the **Catholic Church** teaches that when Paul says a person is justified "by faith and freely", this means that faith is "the beginning of human salvation, the foundation, and the root of all Justification", and that nothing before justification, "whether faith or works", merits its grace. Canon 9 rejects the view that faith alone justifies in the sense that nothing else needs to co-operate.
- `7` [Matthew J. Thomas, Justification, 4.5.6: Orthodox](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Christianity/Justification#section4.5.6) (St Andrews Encyclopaedia of Theology): the **Orthodox churches** have restated the early church's reading of justification. In letters exchanged between 1574 and 1581, Patriarch Jeremiah II of Constantinople answered Lutheran theologians from Tübingen on the Augsburg Confession, and the correspondence ended without agreement on justification.
- `7` [Catechism of the Catholic Church, 385–421](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P1C.HTM): on **sin and death through Adam** ([Romans 5:12](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ROM05.htm#V12)), Catholic teaching holds, quoting this verse, that "all men are implicated in Adam's sin", and that how original sin is passed on "is a mystery that we cannot fully understand" (402–404). The Augsburg Confession teaches that all are "born with sin" ([Augsburg Confession, II](https://bookofconcord.org/augsburg-confession/original-sin/)), and the Westminster Confession that "the guilt of this sin was imputed" to all Adam's descendants ([Westminster Confession of Faith, 6.3](https://www.opc.org/wcf.html)). Orthodox teaching, in Thomas Hopko's account, speaks of all the children of Adam sharing a "tragic fate", born into a world under the power of evil, corruption and death ([Orthodox Church in America, The Orthodox Faith: Sin](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/doctrine-scripture/the-symbol-of-faith/sin)).

## Open questions

Questions on which the churches or scholars disagree. These guidelines don't pick a side.

- `7` [Matthew J. Thomas, Justification, 5.1: New Perspective](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Christianity/Justification#section5.1) (St Andrews Encyclopaedia of Theology): what did Paul mean by "works of the law"? Since E. P. Sanders' study of 1977, scholars of the "new perspective on Paul", among them James Dunn and N. T. Wright, have argued that traditional Protestant readings projected Luther's quarrels onto Paul's Jewish opponents, and tend to read "works of the law" as the Torah's practices, such as circumcision and keeping the Sabbath. Defenders of the Reformation readings continue to dispute this ([Bible Odyssey, The New Perspective on Paul](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/the-new-perspective-on-paul/)).
- `7` [A. Andrew Das, Romans and the Fate of Israel](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/romans-and-the-fate-of-israel/) (Bible Odyssey): what does "all Israel will be saved" ([Romans 11:26](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ROM11.htm#V26)) mean? Some interpreters read "all Israel" as the church of Jews and Gentiles together; others, Das among them, as the Jewish people coming to faith in Jesus as Messiah; and dispensational readings link the verse to a role for the modern nation of Israel at the end of time.
- `7` [USCCB, Romans, chapter 16, notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/romans/16) (New American Bible, Revised Edition): how did the letter end? The notes say that some regard chapter 16 as a later addition, though the evidence favours it being original, and that manuscripts place the closing doxology at the end of chapter 14, 15 or 16, or leave it out; the WEBBE, following the Majority Text, prints it at [Romans 14:24–26](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ROM14.htm#V24). The notes also say that Junia, whom Paul greets in [Romans 16:7](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ROM16.htm#V7), is a woman's name, though most editors have read it as a man's name, Junias.
