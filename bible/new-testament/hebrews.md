# Hebrews

Hebrews reads more like a sermon than a letter. Its author calls it "the word of exhortation" ([Hebrews 13:22](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/HEB13.htm#V22)), the term Acts uses for a sermon in the synagogue ([Acts 13:15](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ACT13.htm#V15)), and the introduction to the New American Bible concludes that it is "probably therefore a written homily, to which the author gave an epistolary ending". Its readers were in danger of giving up their faith, the same introduction says, not because of persecution but because of "a weariness with the demands of Christian life". Its main theme is the priesthood and sacrifice of Jesus: the Son, greater than the angels, than Moses and than the priests of the old covenant, who offered himself once for all and opened a new covenant. It calls the Greek of the letter "in many ways the best in the New Testament" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Letter to the Hebrews](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/hebrews/0)).

The work names no author. Its mention of "our brother Timothy" ([Hebrews 13:23](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/HEB13.htm#V23)) links it to the circle of Paul, and in Greek manuscripts it was usually placed with Paul's letters ([USCCB, Introduction to the Letter to the Hebrews](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/hebrews/0)). Early writers already disagreed. Eusebius reports that Clement of Alexandria held that Paul wrote it in Hebrew and Luke translated it into Greek, and that Origen judged "the thoughts are those of the apostle", but the wording that of someone who remembered his teaching, and concluded: "who wrote the epistle, in truth, God knows" ([Eusebius, Church History 6.14 and 6.25](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/250106.htm)). Eusebius also records that some rejected it as "disputed by the church of Rome, on the ground that it was not written by Paul" ([Eusebius, Church History 3.3](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/250103.htm)).

The USCCB introduction traces what followed: by the end of the second century the church of Alexandria accepted Hebrews as Paul's, which became the common view in the East; in the West Paul's authorship was contested into the fourth century, and then accepted; doubts were raised again in the sixteenth century, and "the modern consensus is that the letter was not written by Paul", with no agreement on another author, such as Barnabas, Apollos or Prisca and Aquila ([USCCB, Introduction to the Letter to the Hebrews](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/hebrews/0)). Every church reads Hebrews as part of the New Testament.

## How the churches read it

Where the churches, or their interpreters, read the book differently, or where it has played a part in their history. Each entry starts with its source's score.

- `7` [Catechism of the Catholic Church, 1356–1381](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P41.HTM): on **Christ's once-for-all sacrifice and the Eucharist** ([Hebrews 9:25–28](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/HEB09.htm#V25), [Hebrews 10:10–14](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/HEB10.htm#V10)), the **Catholic Church** teaches that Christ offered himself "once and for all" on the cross, and that the Eucharist "re-presents (makes present) the sacrifice of the cross": "The sacrifice of Christ and the sacrifice of the Eucharist are one single sacrifice" (1366–1367). **Orthodox** teaching, in the Orthodox Church in America's account, calls the liturgy a "spiritual and bloodless sacrifice" ([Orthodox Church in America, The Orthodox Faith: Holy Eucharist](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/worship/the-sacraments/holy-eucharist)). The Church of England's Article XXXI holds that "the Offering of Christ once made is that perfect redemption" and that "there is none other satisfaction for sin, but that alone", and rejects the "sacrifices of Masses" ([Church of England, Articles of Religion, XXXI](https://www.churchofengland.org/liturgy/book-of-common-prayer/articles-religion#XXXI)); the Westminster Confession teaches that in the Lord's Supper Christ is "not offered up to his Father", and that it is "only a commemoration of that one offering up of himself, by himself, upon the cross, once for all" ([Westminster Confession of Faith, 29.2](https://www.opc.org/wcf.html)). The wider question is open in [Worship and the sacraments](../../general/worship.md#open-questions).
- `7` [Catechism of the Catholic Church, 1539–1553](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P4T.HTM): on **Christ's priesthood and the church's** ([Hebrews 7:26–27](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/HEB07.htm#V26)), **Catholic** teaching, quoting Hebrews, holds that Christ is the "unique 'high priest after the order of Melchizedek'", and that his one priesthood "is made present through the ministerial priesthood without diminishing the uniqueness of Christ's priesthood" (1544–1545); the whole community of believers is also "priestly", sharing in it through baptism (1546–1547, citing [Revelation 1:6](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/REV01.htm#V6) and [1 Peter 2:9](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1PE02.htm#V9)). The **Baptist** Faith and Message stresses instead "the principles of soul competency and the priesthood of believers" ([Baptist Faith and Message 2000, Preamble](https://bfm.sbc.net/preamble/)).

## Open questions

Questions on which the churches or scholars disagree. These guidelines don't pick a side.

- `8` [Christian Reformed Church in North America, The Canons of Dort, Fifth Main Point of Doctrine](https://www.crcna.org/welcome/beliefs/confessions/canons-dort) (1618–1619): **can those who believe fall away?** Hebrews warns that it is "impossible to renew" to repentance those who have shared in the Holy Spirit "and then fell away" ([Hebrews 6:4–6](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/HEB06.htm#V4), [Hebrews 10:26–31](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/HEB10.htm#V26)), and the churches relate such warnings differently to God's faithfulness. The **Reformed** Canons of Dort teach the perseverance of the saints, and the Westminster Confession holds that those whom God has effectually called "can neither totally nor finally fall away from the state of grace" ([Westminster Confession of Faith, 17.1](https://www.opc.org/wcf.html)). The **Methodist** Articles of Religion teach that "we may depart from grace given, and fall into sin, and, by the grace of God, rise again" ([The United Methodist Church, Articles of Religion, XII](https://www.umc.org/en/content/articles-of-religion)). The **Catholic** Council of Trent teaches that the grace of justification is lost by any mortal sin, though not faith itself, and can be recovered through penance ([Council of Trent, Session VI, chapters 14–15](https://www.papalencyclicals.net/councils/trent/sixth-session.htm)).
- `7` [USCCB, Introduction to the Letter to the Hebrews](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/hebrews/0) (New American Bible, Revised Edition): **who wrote Hebrews?** The tradition that Paul wrote it, accepted in the East from the end of the second century and in the West from the fourth, has given way to a modern consensus that he did not, because of its vocabulary and style, its way of citing the Old Testament and its closeness to Alexandrian Jewish thought; but there is no agreement on another author. Origen's verdict was "who wrote the epistle, in truth, God knows" ([Eusebius, Church History 6.25](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/250106.htm)).
- `7` [USCCB, Introduction to the Letter to the Hebrews](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/hebrews/0) (New American Bible, Revised Edition): **when was it written?** Clement of Rome most probably cites it around AD 96, which gives the latest date. Many older commentators and "a growing number of recent ones" hold that the temple was still standing and date it before AD 70, though its present-tense descriptions of the sacrifices do not prove this; if so, the readers' earlier sufferings ([Hebrews 10:32–34](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/HEB10.htm#V32)) may be linked to the expulsion of Jews from Rome under Claudius in AD 49.
