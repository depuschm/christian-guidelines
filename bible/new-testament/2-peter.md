# 2 Peter

The Second Letter of Peter presents itself as a letter from "Simon Peter, a servant and apostle of Jesus Christ" ([2 Peter 1:1](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2PE01.htm#V1)), written as he expects to die soon ([2 Peter 1:13–15](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2PE01.htm#V13)). It calls itself his "second letter" ([2 Peter 3:1](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2PE03.htm#V1)), and counts its author among the witnesses of Jesus' transfiguration ([2 Peter 1:16–18](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2PE01.htm#V16)). The introduction to the New American Bible describes it as "a general exhortation cast in the form of a letter": it seeks to strengthen its readers in faith, hope, knowledge, love and other virtues, warns against false teachers, and answers "scoffers" who say that Christ will not come again, and God's "promises" are especially precious to it. Its long central section on false teachers shares much with the Letter of Jude, and scholars generally agree that 2 Peter draws on Jude, not the other way round ([USCCB, Introduction to the Second Letter of Peter](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/2peter/0)).

The letter's way into the canon was slow. The USCCB introduction notes that the oldest certain reference to it comes from Origen in the early third century, and that some local churches still left it out in the fifth century. Eusebius of Caesarea wrote that it was not part of the canon, "yet, as it has appeared profitable to many, it has been used with the other Scriptures", and counted it among the disputed books, "which are nevertheless recognized by many" ([Eusebius, Church History 3.3.1 and 3.25.3](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/250103.htm)). It was received everywhere in the end, and it is in the New Testament of every church ([The Bible: which books each church reads](../README.md#which-books-each-church-reads)). Most modern scholars regard it as written in Peter's name by a later author, while some defend Peter's authorship (see [Open questions](#open-questions)).

## Outline

The USCCB introduction divides the letter into an address ([2 Peter 1:1–2](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2PE01.htm#V1)), an exhortation to Christian virtue ([2 Peter 1:3–21](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2PE01.htm#V3)), the condemnation of the false teachers ([2 Peter 2:1–22](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2PE02.htm#V1)), the delay of the second coming ([2 Peter 3:1–16](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2PE03.htm#V1)), and a final exhortation and doxology ([2 Peter 3:17–18](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2PE03.htm#V17)). ## How the churches read it

Where the churches, or their interpreters, read the book differently, or where it has played a part in their history. Each entry starts with its source's score.

- `7` [Catechism of the Catholic Church, 460](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P1J.HTM): on "partakers of the divine nature" ([2 Peter 1:4](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2PE01.htm#V4)), the **Catholic Church** teaches that "The Word became flesh to make us 'partakers of the divine nature'", and quotes Athanasius: "For the Son of God became man so that we might become God." **Orthodox** teaching calls this deification (*theosis*), and sees "personal deification and cosmic transfiguration" as the goal of the Christian life: union with God that "does not imply identity with God's essence, but participation in the uncreated 'energies' of God", so that one "becomes by grace what God is by nature" ([Christian Ethics: Eastern Orthodox perspectives](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Christianity/ChristianEthics#section4.2), [The Eastern Orthodox churches](../../traditions/eastern-orthodox.md)). John Calvin, commenting on this verse, wrote that "the end of the gospel is, to render us eventually conformable to God, and, if we may so speak, to deify us", but added that "the word nature is not here essence but quality" ([John Calvin, Commentary on 2 Peter 1:1–4](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/calvin/calcom45.vii.ii.i.html)).

## Open questions

Questions on which the churches or scholars disagree. These guidelines don't pick a side.

- `7` [USCCB, Introduction to the Second Letter of Peter](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/2peter/0): who wrote the letter, and when? The letter names Simon Peter as its author, and the churches received it under his name. Among modern scholars there is "wide agreement" that it is pseudonymous, perhaps the latest book of the New Testament, from the first or second quarter of the second century: it speaks of the apostles' generation as past, knows a collection of Paul's letters, draws on Jude, and answers doubts raised by the delay of Christ's coming. Some scholars dissent: Michael J. Kruger argues that the case for pseudonymity is "too incomplete and insufficient" to support firm conclusions, and that "we have no reason to doubt the epistle's own claims in regard to authorship" ([Michael J. Kruger, The Authenticity of 2 Peter](https://www.galaxie.com/article/jets42-4-04)).
