# Revelation

Revelation, also called the Apocalypse, is the last book of the Bible. It presents itself as "the Revelation of Jesus Christ, which God gave him to show to his servants the things which must happen soon", calls itself a prophecy, and is framed as a letter: "John, to the seven assemblies that are in Asia" ([Revelation 1:1–3](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/REV01.htm#V1), [Revelation 1:4](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/REV01.htm#V4)). The New American Bible's introduction calls it "an outstanding example" of apocalyptic literature, a kind of writing popular among Jews and Christians from about 200 BC to AD 200, and says that its "symbolic descriptions are not to be taken as literal descriptions" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Revelation](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/revelation/0)). After a vision of the risen Christ and his messages to seven churches in Asia Minor, the book unfolds visions of God's throne and the Lamb, of seven seals, seven trumpets and seven bowls, of the fall of "Babylon the great", and of "a new heaven and a new earth" ([Revelation 21:1](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/REV21.htm#V1)). It ends with the prayer "Amen! Yes, come, Lord Jesus!" ([Revelation 22:20](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/REV22.htm#V20)).

The author calls himself John and says he was "on the isle that is called Patmos because of God's Word and the testimony of Jesus Christ" ([Revelation 1:9](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/REV01.htm#V9)). Early Christians often read this as exile, though other explanations are possible ([Bible Odyssey, Patmos](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/patmos/)). Irenaeus wrote that the revelation was seen "at the end of the reign of Domitian", and Eusebius reports that, it is said, the apostle John was condemned to Patmos in Domitian's persecution ([Eusebius, Church History 3.18](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/250103.htm)). Justin, Irenaeus, Tertullian and other early writers identified the author with John the apostle; Dionysius of Alexandria, Eusebius and others did not ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Revelation](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/revelation/0)). Dionysius accepted the book as "the work of a holy and inspired man", but, judging from its style and language, could not "readily admit that he was the apostle, the son of Zebedee" ([Eusebius, Church History 7.25](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/250107.htm)). The New American Bible's introduction holds that vocabulary, grammar and style "make it doubtful" that the same person wrote Revelation and the Fourth Gospel, suggests that the author may have been a disciple of the apostle, and dates the book in its present form "probably near the end of the reign of Domitian (A.D. 81–96)". Some scholars call him "John the seer" or "John of Patmos" ([Bible Odyssey, Which John?](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/which-john-the-elder-the-seer-and-the-apostle/)).

Revelation was among the New Testament books that took longer to be recognised as Scripture ([Bible Odyssey, The New Testament Canon](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/the-new-testament-canon/)). Dionysius reports that some before him rejected it altogether, while he "could not venture to reject the book, as many brethren hold it in high esteem" ([Eusebius, Church History 7.25](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/250107.htm)). Eusebius counted it among the accepted books "if it really seem proper", and also among the disputed ones, as a book "which some, as I said, reject, but which others class with the accepted books" ([Eusebius, Church History 3.25](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/250103.htm)). The Orthodox Church in America notes "a certain hesitation on the part of the early Church" to include it, and that it "came to be the last book listed in the Bible, although it is never read liturgically in the Orthodox Church" ([Orthodox Church in America, Book of Revelation](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/doctrine-scripture/new-testament/book-of-revelation)). Centuries later, Martin Luther "famously had little esteem for the Book of Revelation" ([St Andrews Encyclopaedia of Theology, Millenarianism](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Christianity/Millenarianism)).

## How the churches read it

Where the churches, or their interpreters, read the book differently, or where it has played a part in their history. Each entry starts with its source's score.

- `8` [Jayne Svenungsson, Millenarianism](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Christianity/Millenarianism) (St Andrews Encyclopaedia of Theology): on **the thousand years** ([Revelation 20:1–6](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/REV20.htm#V1)), Svenungsson writes that Justin Martyr, Irenaeus and Tertullian read the passage as "prophesying a future earthly kingdom, with Jerusalem at its centre", and that Augustine reinterpreted it symbolically. Literal readings of the millennium have survived "mainly within low-church Protestantism", and have been "almost unanimously denounced by Roman Catholicism as well as mainstream (Anglican, Reformed, and Lutheran) Protestantism"; millenarianism has played a "relatively marginal role" in **Eastern Orthodox** Christianity. The **Catholic** Catechism teaches that "the Church has rejected even modified forms of this falsification of the kingdom to come under the name of millenarianism" ([Catechism of the Catholic Church, 676](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P1V.HTM)), and the **Lutheran** Augsburg Confession condemns the opinion "that before the resurrection of the dead the godly shall take possession of the kingdom of the world" ([Augsburg Confession, Article XVII](https://bookofconcord.org/augsburg-confession/of-christs-return-to-judgment/)).
- `7` [Lynn R. Huber, Millennium](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/millennium/) (Bible Odyssey): scholars often group Christian readings of the thousand years ([Revelation 20:1–6](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/REV20.htm#V1)) into three. **Amillennialism**, the view of Augustine, which "became the dominant perspective for the church", takes the reign as "metaphorical or allegorical". **Premillennialism**, which Huber calls "the dominant perspective among most evangelical Christians", expects Christ's physical return "to usher in a literal thousand-year reign". **Postmillennialism** holds that Christ's return and reign depend on his followers "creating a peaceful and just kingdom on earth". **Dispensationalist** Protestants hold "a futurist premillennial perspective", and have usually placed the gathering of the church, the rapture, before a seven-year period of tribulation ([Darrell L. Bock, Dispensationalism, 1.3](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Christianity/Dispensationalism#section1.3)).
- `7` [Orthodox Church in America, The Orthodox Faith: Book of Revelation](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/doctrine-scripture/new-testament/book-of-revelation): *The Orthodox Faith*, published by the **Orthodox** Church in America, says that "the wrong method of interpreting the book of Revelation is to give some sort of exclusive meaning to its many visions, equating them with specific, concrete historical events and persons". It reads the letters to the seven churches ([Revelation 2:1–3:22](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/REV02.htm#V1)) as having "both a historical and a universal meaning", and the book's numbers as symbols: 666 ([Revelation 13:18](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/REV13.htm#V18)) for "total depravity", and 144,000 for "the full number of the saved".
- `7` [Jamie Davies, Antichrist](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/antichrist/) (Bible Odyssey): who are **the beast and Babylon** ([Revelation 13:1–18](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/REV13.htm#V1), [Revelation 17:1–18:24](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/REV17.htm#V1))? Davies notes that the word "antichrist" appears only in the letters of John, "and not in the book of Revelation, as is sometimes assumed", and that there is "a long tradition of attempts to identify this individual: popes, reformers, political leaders ancient and modern". During the Reformation, Luther "did not hesitate to identify Rome with Babylon and the institution of the papacy with the forces of evil" ([Jayne Svenungsson, Millenarianism](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Christianity/Millenarianism)). The New American Bible's introduction holds that "the harlot Babylon symbolizes pagan Rome, the city on seven hills" ([Revelation 17:9](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/REV17.htm#V9); [USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Revelation](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/revelation/0)), and the Orthodox Church in America that "the Harlot is interpreted by the Orthodox Church to be the Roman Empire, not the Roman Catholic Church" ([Orthodox Church in America, Questions and Answers: Book of Revelation](https://www.oca.org/questions/scripture/book-of-revelation)).
- `7` [Church of England, Common Worship: Canticles (Daily Prayer)](https://www.churchofengland.org/liturgy/common-worship/daily-prayer/canticles-daily-prayer): the songs of Revelation are sung in worship. The **Church of England's** daily prayer includes canticles from the book, such as ["Great and Wonderful"](https://www.churchofengland.org/liturgy/common-worship/daily-prayer/canticles-daily-prayer/71-great-and-wonderful) ([Revelation 15:3–4](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/REV15.htm#V3)) and ["A Song of the Holy City"](https://www.churchofengland.org/liturgy/common-worship/daily-prayer/canticles-daily-prayer/73-song-holy-city) ([Revelation 21:1–5](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/REV21.htm#V1)). The **Orthodox** Church does not read Revelation in its services, but, the Orthodox Church in America writes, its worship "has traditionally, quite consciously, been patterned after the divine and eternal realities revealed in this book" ([Orthodox Church in America, Book of Revelation](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/doctrine-scripture/new-testament/book-of-revelation)). The **Catholic** Catechism cites the book's closing prayer ([Revelation 22:17](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/REV22.htm#V17), [Revelation 22:20](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/REV22.htm#V20)) for the prayer "Maranatha! 'Our Lord, come!'", which Christians pray "above all in the Eucharist" ([Catechism of the Catholic Church, 671](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P1V.HTM)).

## Open questions

Questions on which the churches or scholars disagree. These guidelines don't pick a side.

- `7` [James F. McGrath, Which John? The Elder, the Seer, and the Apostle](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/which-john-the-elder-the-seer-and-the-apostle/) (Bible Odyssey): who wrote Revelation, and when? The book names its author as John ([Revelation 1:9](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/REV01.htm#V9)). Justin, Irenaeus, Clement of Alexandria, Tertullian, Cyprian and Hippolytus identified him with John the apostle, while Dionysius of Alexandria, Eusebius, Cyril of Jerusalem, Gregory Nazianzen and John Chrysostom did not ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Revelation](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/revelation/0)). McGrath notes that Revelation is written in "much poorer Greek" than the Gospel and letters of John and finds it "extremely unlikely that the same person wrote all of these books"; since apocalypses were typically written in the names of earlier authorities, he adds, the possibility must also be considered that it was written in the apostle's name. On the date, Irenaeus placed the vision "at the end of the reign of Domitian" ([Eusebius, Church History 3.18](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/250103.htm)), and the New American Bible's introduction dates the book in its present form probably near the end of Domitian's reign (AD 81–96).
- `7` [David A. deSilva, The Sign of the Beast](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/the-sign-of-the-beast/) (Bible Odyssey): does Revelation speak of its own time or of the end of history? deSilva holds that John wrote to unlock "what was happening all around his congregations in Asia Minor in their own time…, not in some distant future": the beast stands for the Roman Empire, and the letters of "Nero Caesar" add up to 666 in Hebrew ([Revelation 13:18](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/REV13.htm#V18)). The New American Bible's introduction reads the book as "resistance literature" written for a crisis, which nevertheless "remains valid and meaningful for Christians of all time" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Revelation](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/revelation/0)), and the Orthodox Church in America holds that, while it "does speak of events yet to come—such as the Second Coming of Our Lord", it "is not a book that was written primarily to reveal contemporary or coming events" ([Orthodox Church in America, Questions and Answers: Book of Revelation](https://www.oca.org/questions/scripture/book-of-revelation)). Lorenzo DiTommaso writes, on the other hand, that "the vast majority of Christians throughout history have believed that the Revelation of John and the other apocalyptic books of the Bible reveal the imminent end of the present age" ([Bible Odyssey, Apocalyptic Thought after Revelation](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/apocalyptic-thought-after-revelation/)), and dispensationalists read it from "a futurist premillennial perspective" ([Darrell L. Bock, Dispensationalism, 1.3](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Christianity/Dispensationalism#section1.3)).
