# 1 John

The First Letter of John has no greeting, no named sender and no closing, so the introduction to the New American Bible calls it "more akin to a theological treatise than to most other New Testament letters". It opens with a witness to "the Word of life" that the writers have "seen with our eyes" and "our hands touched" ([1 John 1:1–4](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1JN01.htm#V1)), and it is written to a community that some members have left: "They went out from us, but they didn't belong to us" ([1 John 2:19](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1JN02.htm#V19)). Those who left, the introduction explains, refused to acknowledge Jesus as the Christ and denied that he was truly human ([1 John 2:22](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1JN02.htm#V22), [1 John 4:1–6](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1JN04.htm#V1)). Against them the letter holds together right belief about Jesus and love for one another: "knowledge of God and love for one another are inseparable" ([USCCB, Introduction to the First Letter of John](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/1john/0)).

Early Christian tradition identified the letter as the work of John the apostle. Because it resembles the Gospel of John in style, vocabulary and ideas, the New American Bible's introduction says it is "generally agreed that both works are the product of the same school of Johannine Christianity", probably written after the Gospel, and it dates the letter "toward the end of the first century" ([USCCB, Introduction to the First Letter of John](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/1john/0)). Many scholars link the Gospel and the three letters of John with a network of churches in Asia Minor ([Bible Odyssey, The Johannine Community](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/the-johannine-community/)); the questions of authorship are set out on the page on [John](john.md#open-questions). The letter states its own purpose: "that you may know that you have eternal life" ([1 John 5:13–21](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1JN05.htm#V13)). It is in the New Testament of every church ([The Bible: which books each church reads](../README.md#which-books-each-church-reads)).

## How the churches read it

Where the churches, or their interpreters, read the book differently, or where it has played a part in their history. Each entry starts with its source's score.

- `7` [New Revised Standard Version Updated Edition, 1 John](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1%20John%201-5&version=NRSVUE) (Bible Gateway): **the "Johannine comma"**. Some Bibles add Trinitarian words to [1 John 5:7](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1JN05.htm#V7): the **King James Version** reads "there are three that bear record in heaven, the Father, the Word, and the Holy Ghost: and these three are one" ([Bible Gateway, 1 John 5:7–8 (KJV)](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1%20John%205%3A7-8&version=KJV)). Modern translations used across the churches leave the words out of the text: the NRSVUE gives them only in a footnote, as the reading of "other ancient authorities", and the Catholic New American Bible reads simply "So there are three that testify, the Spirit, the water, and the blood" ([USCCB, 1 John 5](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/1john/5)). The WEBBE, which this page quotes, notes that "only a few recent manuscripts add" them ([1 John 5:6–8](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1JN05.htm#V6)).
- `7` [Catechism of the Catholic Church, 1846–1848](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P69.HTM): on **confessing sin** ([1 John 1:8–9](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1JN01.htm#V8)), the **Catholic Church** quotes these verses to teach that "to receive his mercy, we must admit our faults". The **Westminster Confession** holds that everyone "is bound to make private confession of his sins to God, praying for the pardon thereof" ([Westminster Confession of Faith, 15.6](https://www.opc.org/wcf.html)). Whether sins should also be confessed to a priest is an open question in [Repentance and forgiveness](../../general/repentance.md#open-questions).
- `7` [Catechism of the Catholic Church, 1854–1864](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P6C.HTM): on **"sin leading to death"** ([1 John 5:16–17](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1JN05.htm#V16)), the **Catholic Church** teaches that "the distinction between mortal and venial sin, already evident in Scripture, became part of the tradition of the Church", referring to this passage: mortal sin "destroys charity", while venial sin "allows charity to subsist". The **Westminster** standards teach that some sins are "more heinous in the sight of God than others", yet "every sin, even the least" deserves God's wrath ([Westminster Larger Catechism, Q. 150–152](https://www.opc.org/lc.html)), and that "there is no sin so small, but it deserves damnation; so there is no sin so great, that it can bring damnation upon those who truly repent" ([Westminster Confession of Faith, 15.4](https://www.opc.org/wcf.html)). The New American Bible's note reads "deadly sin" as "probably referring to apostasy" ([USCCB, 1 John 5](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/1john/5)).

## Open questions

Questions on which the churches or scholars disagree. These guidelines don't pick a side.

- `7` [USCCB, Introduction to the First Letter of John](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/1john/0): who wrote the letter, and how does it relate to the Gospel of John? Early tradition named John the apostle. The New American Bible's introduction holds that the letter and the Gospel come from the same school; some scholars think the letter was a short treatise on ideas the Gospel develops more fully, and others that it was written "as part of a debate on the proper interpretation of that gospel". The wider question of the Johannine writings is on the page on [John](john.md#open-questions).
- `7` [USCCB, Introduction to the First Letter of John](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/1john/0): what did those who left the community teach? The introduction says that the teaching the letter opposes "cannot be identified exactly", but calls it "a form of docetism or gnosticism": docetism denied that Christ was truly human.
