# 1 Thessalonians

The First Letter to the Thessalonians is a short letter of encouragement to a young church in Thessalonica, a port city and the capital of the Roman province of Macedonia. It opens with greetings from "Paul, Silvanus, and Timothy, to the assembly of the Thessalonians in God the Father and the Lord Jesus Christ" ([1 Thessalonians 1:1](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1TH01.htm#V1), [Bible Odyssey, Thessalonica](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/thessalonica/)). According to Acts, Paul founded the church on his second missionary journey and soon had to leave the city because of opposition ([Acts 17:1–10](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ACT17.htm#V1)).

The introduction to the New American Bible tells the story behind the letter. Paul arrived in Greece about AD 50, was persecuted in Philippi and Thessalonica, and moved on to Athens. He sent Timothy back to strengthen the new church ([1 Thessalonians 3:1–5](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1TH03.htm#V1)), and Timothy's report, which reached Paul in Corinth, "probably in the early summer of A.D. 51", was the occasion for the letter ([1 Thessalonians 3:6–8](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1TH03.htm#V6), [Acts 18:1–5](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ACT18.htm#V1), [USCCB, Introduction to the First Letter to the Thessalonians](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/1thessalonians/0)).

Scholars agree that Paul wrote it: 1 Thessalonians is among the letters with "some consensus" as authentic ([Bible Odyssey, Paul](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/paul/)), and it is "generally viewed as Paul's earliest surviving authentic letter" ([Bible Odyssey, Thessalonica](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/thessalonica/)). It is in the New Testament of every church.

## How the churches read it

Where the churches, or their interpreters, read the book differently, or where it has played a part in their history. Each entry starts with its source's score.

- `7` [Darrell L. Bock, Dispensationalism, 1.2: What is dispensationalism?](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Christianity/Dispensationalism#section1.2) (St Andrews Encyclopaedia of Theology): on **the Lord's coming** and being "caught up together… to meet the Lord in the air" ([1 Thessalonians 4:16–17](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1TH04.htm#V16)), **dispensationalist** Protestants, a movement that emerged in the 19th century, expect the "imminent return of Christ in the rapture": a gathering of the church that most of them place before a seven-year period of tribulation and a thousand-year reign of Christ on earth ([Dispensationalism, 1.3](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Christianity/Dispensationalism#section1.3)). Bock notes that the wider Christian tradition, since Augustine, was often amillennial, and that critics from that tradition hold there is no earthly millennium and that Christ's return leads straight to the new heaven and new earth ([Dispensationalism, 5.1](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Christianity/Dispensationalism#section5.1)). The **Catholic Church** reads this passage of the resurrection of the dead "at the last day", which it closely associates with Christ's return ([Catechism of the Catholic Church, 1001](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P2H.HTM)), and the Westminster Confession speaks of one day appointed for Christ's judgement of all ([Westminster Confession of Faith, 33](https://www.opc.org/wcf.html)). The shared hope of the creeds is set out in [What the churches confess together](../../general/beliefs.md).

## Open questions

Questions on which the churches or scholars disagree. These guidelines don't pick a side.

- `7` [Darrell L. Bock, Dispensationalism, 5.1: Non-dispensational critiques](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Christianity/Dispensationalism#section5.1) (St Andrews Encyclopaedia of Theology): how should the coming of the Lord in [1 Thessalonians 4:13–18](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1TH04.htm#V13) be read alongside the rest of Scripture? Dispensationalists read it as a gathering of the church that comes before the tribulation and an earthly millennium; the amillennial view, which Bock describes as common in the tradition since Augustine, holds that Christ's return leads directly to the new heaven and earth, and dispensationalists reply, among other things, that the earliest church was premillennial.
