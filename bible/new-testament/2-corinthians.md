# 2 Corinthians

The Second Letter to the Corinthians is a letter from Paul, with Timothy, "to the assembly of God which is at Corinth, with all the saints who are in the whole of Achaia" ([2 Corinthians 1:1](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2CO01.htm#V1)), a church Paul had founded on his first stay in the city ([Acts 18:1–11](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ACT18.htm#V1)). The introduction to the New American Bible calls it "the most personal of all of Paul's extant writings". It deals with a crisis between Paul and the church, the collection for the poor believers in Jerusalem, and Paul's defence of his ministry against visiting missionaries who had unsettled the Corinthians. The same introduction says that at least parts of it were written in Macedonia, and that it is "generally dated about the autumn of A.D. 57" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Second Letter to the Corinthians](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/2corinthians/0)).

Paul wrote more letters to Corinth than the two in the New Testament. The biblical scholar David Horrell counts "at least four", noting the letters mentioned in [1 Corinthians 5:9](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1CO05.htm#V9) and in [2 Corinthians 2:3–4](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2CO02.htm#V3) and [2 Corinthians 7:8](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2CO07.htm#V8) ([Bible Odyssey, Corinth](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/corinth/)). Of the letter mentioned in 2 Corinthians, Paul says he wrote it "out of much affliction and anguish of heart … with many tears". Scholars debate whether Paul wrote every letter that bears his name, but there is "some consensus" that 2 Corinthians is one of his own ([Bible Odyssey, Paul](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/paul/)). Every church reads it as part of the New Testament.

The letter ends with a blessing in the name of Christ, God and the Holy Spirit: "The grace of the Lord Jesus Christ, God's love, and the fellowship of the Holy Spirit be with you all" ([2 Corinthians 13:14](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2CO13.htm#V14)).

## Outline

The USCCB introduction divides the letter into an address, the crisis between Paul and the Corinthians, the collection for Jerusalem, Paul's defence of his ministry, and a conclusion. Its notes describe the long middle section on Paul's ministry as a ministry "of glory", "of life" and "of reconciliation" ([USCCB, 2 Corinthians, chapter 2, notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/2corinthians/2)). The WEBBE numbers the final blessing as verse 14 of chapter 13.

## How the churches read it

Where the churches, or their interpreters, read the book differently, or where it has played a part in their history. Each entry starts with its source's score.

- `9` [Heidelberg Catechism, Q&A 56 and 60](https://www.crcna.org/welcome/beliefs/confessions/heidelberg-catechism) (1563): on God "reconciling the world to himself, not reckoning to them their trespasses" ([2 Corinthians 5:18–21](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2CO05.htm#V18)), the **Reformed** catechism cites this passage when it teaches that, because of Christ's satisfaction, God "will no longer remember any of my sins", and when it teaches that God "grants and credits to me the perfect satisfaction, righteousness, and holiness of Christ", citing "him who knew no sin" being made "sin on our behalf" ([2 Corinthians 5:21](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2CO05.htm#V21)).
- `7` [Catechism of the Catholic Church, 1440–1449](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P4C.HTM): on "the ministry of reconciliation" ([2 Corinthians 5:18–20](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2CO05.htm#V18)), the **Catholic Church** teaches that Christ "entrusted the exercise of the power of absolution to the apostolic ministry which he charged with the 'ministry of reconciliation'", and quotes Paul's plea, "Be reconciled to God", in its teaching on the sacrament of Penance and Reconciliation (1442). **Orthodox** teaching, in Thomas Hopko's account, calls the sacrament of penance "our formal act of reconciliation with God in the Church" ([Orthodox Church in America, The Orthodox Faith: Penance](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/worship/the-sacraments/penance)). Whether confession to a priest is needed is an open question in [Repentance and forgiveness](../../general/repentance.md#open-questions).
- `7` [Baptist Faith and Message 2000, XIII: Stewardship](https://bfm.sbc.net/bfm2000/#xiii) (Southern Baptist Convention): on **giving** ([2 Corinthians 8:1–9:15](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2CO08.htm#V1)), the Baptist confession cites these chapters when it asks Christians to give "cheerfully, regularly, systematically, proportionately, and liberally". The Catholic Catechism traces the collection at the Eucharist to the same letter: the custom "is inspired by the example of Christ who became poor to make us rich" ([2 Corinthians 8:9](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2CO08.htm#V9), [Catechism of the Catholic Church, 1351](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P40.HTM)). Whether Christians must give a tenth is an open question in [Money, possessions and generosity](../../general/money.md#open-questions).

## Open questions

Questions on which the churches or scholars disagree. These guidelines don't pick a side.

- `7` [USCCB, Introduction to the Second Letter to the Corinthians](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/2corinthians/0) (New American Bible, Revised Edition): is 2 Corinthians one letter, or several joined together? Scholars have noticed "a lack of continuity": the section on Paul's ministry ([2 Corinthians 2:14–7:4](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2CO02.htm#V14)) seems "abruptly spliced" into the story of the crisis, and the collection and Paul's defence are each treated twice. "Many judge" that it gathers several shorter letters, edited together by Paul or, more likely, someone else; "others continue to regard it as a single letter", written as fresh news arrived. The notes also ask whether the earlier letter written "with many tears" survives elsewhere in the Corinthian correspondence, and judge that "none of these hypotheses is entirely convincing" ([USCCB, 2 Corinthians, chapter 2, notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/2corinthians/2)).
- `7` [USCCB, 2 Corinthians, chapter 12, notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/2corinthians/12) (New American Bible, Revised Edition): what was Paul's "thorn in the flesh" ([2 Corinthians 12:7](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2CO12.htm#V7))? The notes say it has been "variously interpreted as a sickness or physical disability, a temptation, or a handicap connected with his apostolic activity", and suggest it may be a persistent opponent, since the Hebrew image of a thorn in the side refers to people ([Numbers 33:55](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/NUM33.htm#V55)). The biblical scholar Meghan Henning reads it as a disability that Paul presents as "a source of strength" ([Bible Odyssey, Disabilities in the Bible](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/disabilities-in-the-bible/)).
