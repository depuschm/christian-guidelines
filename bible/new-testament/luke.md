# Luke

The Gospel according to Luke is the first of two volumes: it tells the story of Jesus from the announcements of his birth to his ascension, and the Acts of the Apostles continues the story with the church. Both are addressed to Theophilus, so that he "might know the certainty" of what he had been taught ([Luke 1:1–4](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/LUK01.htm#V1), [Acts 1:1–2](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ACT01.htm#V1)). The introduction to the New American Bible describes it as showing how God's promises to Israel are fulfilled in Jesus and how the salvation he brings reaches the Gentiles, and notes that "no gospel writer is more concerned than Luke with the mercy and compassion of Jesus", with the Holy Spirit, with prayer, and with Jesus' care for the poor, the outcast, sinners and women ([USCCB, Introduction to the Gospel according to Luke](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/luke/0)).

The gospel names no author. Its prologue says that the writer drew on accounts handed down by eyewitnesses and set them in order ([Luke 1:1–3](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/LUK01.htm#V1)). In the late second century, Irenaeus wrote that "Luke also, the companion of Paul, recorded in a book the Gospel preached by him" ([Irenaeus, Against Heresies, III.1](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0103301.htm)), and Eusebius describes Luke as "of Antiochian parentage and a physician by profession" ([Eusebius, Church History, III.4](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/250103.htm)), the "beloved physician" of [Colossians 4:14](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/COL04.htm#V14). The USCCB introduction follows this tradition, and adds that the author was not one of the first generation of disciples, that he drew on the Gospel of Mark, a collection of Jesus' sayings and material of his own, and that most scholars date the gospel after AD 70, many to AD 80–90 ([USCCB, Introduction to the Gospel according to Luke](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/luke/0)).

Luke is in the New Testament of every church. With Matthew and Mark it is one of the three "Synoptic" Gospels, so called from the Greek *synopsis*, "see together", because their stories are so alike that they are studied side by side ([Bible Odyssey, Did the Authors of the Canonical Gospels Know Each Other?](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/did-the-authors-of-the-canonical-gospels-know-each-other/)).

## How the churches read it

Where the churches, or their interpreters, read the gospel differently, or where it has shaped their prayer and practice. Each entry starts with its source's score.

- `7` [Luke 22:19–20](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/LUK22.htm#V19): every church celebrates the Lord's Supper as Jesus commanded here ([Worship and the sacraments](../../general/worship.md), `WOR-3`). The churches differ on how Christ is present in it and whether it is a sacrifice, as the open questions of [Worship and the sacraments](../../general/worship.md#open-questions) set out.
- `7` [Catechism of the Catholic Church, 2663–2679](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P9F.HTM): the **Catholic Church** prays the Hail Mary, which takes up the angel Gabriel's greeting and Elizabeth's greeting to Mary ([Luke 1:28](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/LUK01.htm#V28), [Luke 1:42](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/LUK01.htm#V42)), and makes "Elizabeth's greeting our own" (2676).
- `7` [Theo Andreou, Mary, the Mother of God, in the Eastern Orthodox Tradition, 1.1: The New Testament](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Christianity/MaryintheEasternOrthodoxTradition#section1.1) (St Andrews Encyclopaedia of Theology): the **Orthodox Church** sees in Mary's words "all generations will call me blessed" ([Luke 1:48](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/LUK01.htm#V48)) an affirmation of its devotion to her, and its Akathist Hymn, sung in Lent, takes up Gabriel's greeting and Mary's answer ([Luke 1:38](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/LUK01.htm#V38); [section 1.4](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Christianity/MaryintheEasternOrthodoxTradition#section1.4)).
- `7` [Augsburg Confession, XXI: Of the Worship of Saints](https://bookofconcord.org/augsburg-confession/of-worship-of-saints/): **Lutheran** teaching holds that the memory of the saints may be set before Christians, "that we may follow their faith and good works", but that Scripture does not teach the invocation of saints, because it sets before us Christ as the one Mediator and Intercessor.
- `7` [United States Conference of Catholic Bishops, Liturgy of the Hours](https://www.usccb.org/prayer-and-worship/liturgy-of-the-hours): the songs of Luke's first chapters are prayed every day. In the **Catholic** Liturgy of the Hours, Morning Prayer includes the Benedictus ([Luke 1:68–79](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/LUK01.htm#V68)) and Evening Prayer the Magnificat ([Luke 1:46–55](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/LUK01.htm#V46)). The **Anglican** Book of Common Prayer sets the Benedictus at [Morning Prayer](https://www.churchofengland.org/liturgy/book-of-common-prayer/order-morning-prayer), and the Magnificat and the Nunc dimittis ([Luke 2:29–32](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/LUK02.htm#V29)) at [Evening Prayer](https://www.churchofengland.org/liturgy/book-of-common-prayer/order-evening-prayer).

## Open questions

Questions on which the churches or scholars disagree. These guidelines don't pick a side.

- `7` [Sarah E. Rollens, Did the Authors of the Canonical Gospels Know Each Other?](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/did-the-authors-of-the-canonical-gospels-know-each-other/) (Bible Odyssey): what sources did Luke use? Most scholars agree that Matthew and Luke both used Mark, and many that they also used a lost collection of Jesus' sayings called Q (the Two-Source Hypothesis). Others hold that Luke used both Mark and Matthew, so that no Q is needed (the Farrer Hypothesis). A third view, first proposed by Johann Jakob Griesbach, holds that Mark combined Matthew and Luke. The USCCB introduction notes that some hold that Luke used Mark only to complete material from other traditions ([USCCB, Introduction to the Gospel according to Luke](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/luke/0)).
- `7` [USCCB, Introduction to the Gospel according to Luke](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/luke/0): who wrote the gospel, and when? From the late second century, Christian writers name Luke, Paul's companion ([Irenaeus, Against Heresies, III.1](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0103301.htm)). The gospel itself names no author. Most scholars date it after AD 70, many to AD 80–90, because it draws on Mark and some passages seem to know the Romans' destruction of Jerusalem ([Luke 19:43–44](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/LUK19.htm#V43), [Luke 21:20](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/LUK21.htm#V20)).
