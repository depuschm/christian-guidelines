# Jude

The Letter of Jude is a short letter from "Jude, a servant of Jesus Christ, and brother of James" ([Jude 1](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JUD01.htm#V1)), written "to those who are called". The introduction to the New American Bible notes that since the author does not call himself an apostle, he is almost certainly not Judas of the Twelve, but the Jude named among the relatives of Jesus ([Matthew 13:55](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MAT13.htm#V55), [Mark 6:3](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MRK06.htm#V3)), brother of the James to whom the Letter of James is attributed. It calls the letter "an urgent note": the author had meant to write about salvation, but the danger from false teachers who had slipped into the community made him write a warning instead, which ends in a "majestic closing doxology" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Letter of Jude](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/jude/0)). Eusebius of Caesarea records, from the early writer Hegesippus, that grandchildren of "Jude, who is said to have been the Lord's brother according to the flesh" were brought before the emperor Domitian ([Eusebius, Church History 3.20.1](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/250103.htm)).

Jude is closely related to 2 Peter: [Jude 4–16](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JUD01.htm#V4) and [2 Peter 2:1–18](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2PE02.htm#V1) are so alike that one writer must have used the other. Most scholars think Jude is the earlier, partly because it quotes two Jewish writings outside the Bible, the Assumption of Moses ([Jude 9](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JUD01.htm#V9)) and the Book of Enoch ([Jude 14–15](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JUD01.htm#V14)), which 2 Peter leaves out ([USCCB, Introduction to the Letter of Jude](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/jude/0)). Eusebius counted Jude among the disputed books, noting that "not many of the ancients have mentioned it", though it had been "read publicly in very many churches" ([Eusebius, Church History 2.23.25](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/250102.htm)). Jude is in the New Testament of every church ([The Bible: which books each church reads](../README.md#which-books-each-church-reads)).

## Outline

The USCCB introduction describes the letter's movement: its purpose ([Jude 3–4](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JUD01.htm#V3)), a warning against the false teachers ([Jude 5–16](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JUD01.htm#V5)), admonitions ([Jude 17–23](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JUD01.htm#V17)) and a closing doxology ([Jude 24–25](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JUD01.htm#V24)).

## How the churches read it

Where the churches, or their interpreters, read the book differently, or where it has played a part in their history. Each entry starts with its source's score.

- `9` [Dei Verbum, 8](https://www.vatican.va/archive/hist_councils/ii_vatican_council/documents/vat-ii_const_19651118_dei-verbum_en.html) (Second Vatican Council): **Catholic** teaching reads [Jude 3](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JUD01.htm#V3) as a witness to the apostolic tradition. The apostles, "handing on what they themselves had received", warn the faithful to hold fast to the traditions they have learned "either by word of mouth or by letter", citing [2 Thessalonians 2:15](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2TH02.htm#V15), "and to fight in defense of the faith handed on once and for all". How Scripture and the church's tradition relate is an open question in [Reading Scripture](../../general/scripture.md#open-questions).
- `7` [Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church, The Canonical Books](https://www.ethiopianorthodox.org/english/canonical/books.html): the **Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church** counts 81 books in its Bible, and its Old Testament includes Enoch, so the prophecy that Jude quotes ([Jude 14–15](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JUD01.htm#V14)) is part of its Scripture. The other churches don't count 1 Enoch among the books of the Bible ([The Bible: which books each church reads](../README.md#which-books-each-church-reads)), and the whole of 1 Enoch survives only in Classical Ethiopic ([Kelley Coblentz Bautch, Enoch](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/enoch/)).

## Open questions

Questions on which the churches or scholars disagree. These guidelines don't pick a side.

- `7` [USCCB, Introduction to the Letter of Jude](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/jude/0): who wrote the letter, and when? Many interpreters today regard it as pseudonymous, written at the end of the first century or later: it speaks of the apostles as belonging to the past, treats the faith as a body of teaching handed down, is written in competent Greek, and opposes errors that resemble the gnosticism of the early second century. The introduction finds these arguments "not entirely compelling", and allows for writing around AD 80, "when the historical Jude may still have been alive".
- `6` [Andrew M. Mbuvi, Use of Apocryphal Writing in Jude](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/use-of-apocryphal-writing-in-jude/) (Bible Odyssey): what does it mean that Jude quotes writings outside the Bible? Mbuvi notes that Jude "quotes approvingly" from 1 Enoch, which "was considered Scripture by some Jewish communities", and that the letter was written before anyone had raised the question of the canon; what counted as Scripture for its author "differs from modern canons". In the fourth century, Jerome reported that because the letter quotes Enoch "it is rejected by many", but that "by age and use it has gained authority and is reckoned among the Holy Scriptures" ([Jerome, Lives of Illustrious Men, 4](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/2708.htm)). Today every church reads Jude; the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church also counts 1 Enoch as Scripture, and the other churches don't.
