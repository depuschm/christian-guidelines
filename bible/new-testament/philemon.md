# Philemon

The Letter to Philemon is the shortest of Paul's letters: one chapter of 25 verses. It comes from "Paul, a prisoner of Christ Jesus, and Timothy" and is addressed to Philemon, to Apphia and Archippus, "and to the assembly in your house" ([Philemon 1–3](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/PHM01.htm#V1)). It concerns one man, Onesimus, whom Paul calls "my child … whom I have become the father of in my chains", and whom he is now sending back ([Philemon 10–11](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/PHM01.htm#V10), [Philemon 12](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/PHM01.htm#V12)). The introduction to the New American Bible dates the letter to one of Paul's imprisonments, "perhaps in Rome between A.D. 61 and 63", and describes Onesimus as a slave from Colossae who "had run away from his master, perhaps guilty of theft in the process"; Paul asks that he be welcomed "not just as a slave but as a brother in Christ" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Letter to Philemon](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/philemon/0)).

Scholars broadly agree that Paul wrote it: Bible Odyssey lists Philemon among the letters on which there is "some consensus" ([Bible Odyssey, Paul](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/paul/)). The Letter to the Colossians names Onesimus as "the faithful and beloved brother, who is one of you" ([Colossians 4:9](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/COL04.htm#V9), [Colossians](colossians.md)). Whether he is the Onesimus whom Ignatius of Antioch, in the early second century, calls bishop of Ephesus is disputed ([Demetrius K. Williams, Onesimus](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/onesimus/)). In the fourth century some thought the letter too trivial for Scripture, a view that John Chrysostom and Jerome answered ([Williams, "No Longer as a Slave"](https://ms.augsburgfortress.org/downloads/9780800663414Chapter1.pdf)). It is in the New Testament of every church.

## How the churches read it

Where the churches, or their interpreters, read the book differently, or where it has played a part in their history. Each entry starts with its source's score.

- `7` [Catechism of the Catholic Church, 2414](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P8B.HTM): the churches today teach that slavery is wrong. The **Catholic** Catechism holds that the seventh commandment forbids acts that "lead to the enslavement of human beings", calls it "a sin against the dignity of persons", and recalls Paul's plea that Philemon treat Onesimus "no longer as a slave but more than a slave, as a beloved brother" ([Philemon 16](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/PHM01.htm#V16)). The **Westminster Larger Catechism** counts "man-stealing" among the sins against the eighth commandment ([Westminster Larger Catechism, Q. 142](https://www.opc.org/lc.html)). In 1995 the **Southern Baptist Convention** acknowledged that "many of our Southern Baptist forbears defended the right to own slaves", and resolved to "lament and repudiate historic acts of evil such as slavery" ([Southern Baptist Convention, Resolution on Racial Reconciliation (1995)](https://www.sbc.net/resource-library/resolutions/resolution-on-racial-reconciliation-on-the-150th-anniversary-of-the-southern-baptist-convention/)).
- `7` [Demetrius K. Williams, "No Longer as a Slave"](https://ms.augsburgfortress.org/downloads/9780800663414Chapter1.pdf) (Fortress Press, 2012): how the letter has been read over the centuries. From **John Chrysostom** onwards, the "normative reading" held that Paul sends a runaway slave back to his master, changed by his conversion but not in his status. **Martin Luther** saw in the letter the love of Christ, "we are all [Christ's] Onesimi, if we believe it", yet held, in his 1527 lectures, that Paul respected the legal rights of property and did not seek to abolish slavery; **John Calvin** took it to show that the gospel does not overturn the established order. In the nineteenth-century United States, pro-slavery interpreters read the letter this way, while **abolitionist** interpreters read "no longer as a slave" ([Philemon 16](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/PHM01.htm#V16)) as a call for freedom. Williams concludes that both those who sought to keep things as they were and those who sought justice "flocked to Philemon for inspiration".

## Open questions

Questions on which the churches or scholars disagree. These guidelines don't pick a side.

- `7` [Demetrius K. Williams, Onesimus](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/onesimus/) (Bible Odyssey): why was Onesimus with Paul, and what does Paul ask for? The traditional reading is that he was a runaway who had stolen from Philemon ([Philemon 15](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/PHM01.htm#V15), [Philemon 18](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/PHM01.htm#V18)); others suggest he fled to Paul, his master's friend, to ask him to plead for him, or that Philemon or the church sent him to serve Paul in prison, as the Philippians sent Epaphroditus ([Philippians 2:25–30](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/PHP02.htm#V25)). Paul may be asking Philemon to welcome Onesimus as a brother while he remains a slave, to let him return to serve Paul, or to set him free ([Philemon 13–14](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/PHM01.htm#V13), [Philemon 21](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/PHM01.htm#V21)); "it is Paul's ambiguous language that has made the interpretation of this letter so challenging". The introduction to the New American Bible holds the traditional reading, and suggests that Paul would like "to have Onesimus work with him for the gospel" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Letter to Philemon](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/philemon/0)).
