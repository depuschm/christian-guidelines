# 2 Timothy

The Second Letter to Timothy is written in the name of Paul to "Timothy, my beloved child" ([2 Timothy 1:1–2](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2TI01.htm#V1)). It presents Paul as a prisoner ([2 Timothy 1:8](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2TI01.htm#V8), [2 Timothy 2:9](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2TI02.htm#V9)) in Rome ([2 Timothy 1:16–17](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2TI01.htm#V16)), deserted by many and expecting to die: "For I am already being offered, and the time of my departure has come. I have fought the good fight. I have finished the course. I have kept the faith" ([2 Timothy 4:6–8](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2TI04.htm#V6)). The introduction to the New American Bible calls its tone "more personal than in First Timothy" and says that it "takes on the character of a final exhortation and testament from Paul to the younger Timothy". It urges Timothy to hold to the gospel without fear, to prepare others to carry on the work, to protect the church from false teaching and to rely on the Scriptures; in passing, it asks for Paul's cloak and his books ([2 Timothy 4:13](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2TI04.htm#V13); [USCCB, Introduction to the Second Letter to Timothy](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/2timothy/0)).

The letter is one of the three Pastoral Epistles, with 1 Timothy and Titus, and the questions of who wrote them and when are discussed together ([USCCB, Introduction to the First Letter to Timothy](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/1timothy/0)). The introduction to 2 Timothy notes that if Paul wrote it, it is the last of the three; "the many scholars who argue that the Pastorals are products of the Pauline school often incline toward Second Timothy as the earliest of the three and the one most likely to have actual fragments of material from Paul himself" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Second Letter to Timothy](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/2timothy/0)). Bible Odyssey lists 1 and 2 Timothy and Titus among the letters written in Paul's name in which scholars find pseudepigraphy, writing in the name of another ([Bible Odyssey, Pseudepigraphy](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/pseudepigraphy/)). The letter is in the New Testament of every church ([The Bible: which books each church reads](../README.md#which-books-each-church-reads)).

## How the churches read it

Where the churches, or their interpreters, read the book differently, or where it has played a part in their history. Each entry starts with its source's score.

- `9` [Second Vatican Council, Dei Verbum, 11](https://www.vatican.va/archive/hist_councils/ii_vatican_council/documents/vat-ii_const_19651118_dei-verbum_en.html) (1965): on **"Every Scripture is God-breathed"** ([2 Timothy 3:16–17](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2TI03.htm#V16)), the churches agree that Scripture is inspired by God, and several cite this verse for it, while they differ on how Scripture relates to the church's tradition and in what sense it is without error. The **Catholic Church**, citing this verse, holds that the books of both Testaments "in their entirety, with all their parts, are sacred and canonical because written under the inspiration of the Holy Spirit, they have God as their author". The **Orthodox** Church in America teaches that "the Bible is the main written source of divine doctrine since God Himself inspired its writing by His Holy Spirit (see 2 Tim 3.16 and 2 Pet 1.20)" ([Orthodox Church in America, The Bible](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/doctrine-scripture/sources-of-christian-doctrine/bible1)). The **Lausanne Covenant**, citing this verse first, affirms "the divine inspiration, truthfulness and authority of both Old and New Testament Scriptures in their entirety as the only written word of God, without error in all that it affirms, and the only infallible rule of faith and practice" ([The Lausanne Covenant, 2](https://lausanne.org/statement/lausanne-covenant)), and the **Westminster Confession** holds that the books of the Old and New Testaments "are given by inspiration of God to be the rule of faith and life" ([Westminster Confession of Faith, 1.2](https://www.opc.org/wcf.html)). The open questions on Scripture and tradition, and on whether Scripture is without error, are in [Reading Scripture](../../general/scripture.md#open-questions).
- `7` [Catechism of the Catholic Church, 1554–1571](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P4U.HTM): on **"the gift of God which is in you through the laying on of my hands"** ([2 Timothy 1:6](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2TI01.htm#V6)), the **Catholic Church** teaches that the apostles, "by the imposition of hands", passed on to their helpers "the gift of the Spirit, which is transmitted down to our day through episcopal consecration", citing this verse and [1 Timothy 4:14](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1TI04.htm#V14). The **Orthodox** Church in America teaches that bishops, priests and deacons "guarantee the continuity and unity of the Church from age to age", receiving the gift of the Spirit with the "laying on of hands" ([Orthodox Church in America, Holy Orders](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/worship/the-sacraments/holy-orders)). The **Lutheran** Augsburg Confession holds that "no one should publicly teach in the Church or administer the Sacraments unless he be regularly called" ([Augsburg Confession, XIV](https://bookofconcord.org/augsburg-confession/of-ecclesiastical-order/)). How the church's ministry should be ordered, including the place of bishops as successors of the apostles, is an open question in [Belonging to a church](../../general/church.md#open-questions).

## Open questions

Questions on which the churches or scholars disagree. These guidelines don't pick a side.

- `7` [USCCB, Introduction to the Second Letter to Timothy](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/2timothy/0) (New American Bible, Revised Edition): did Paul write the letter, and where does it stand among the Pastoral Epistles? On Paul's authorship, it is the last of the three, written as he faced death in Rome; many scholars who see the Pastorals as the work of Paul's followers regard it as the earliest of them and the one most likely to contain fragments from Paul himself. The introduction to 1 Timothy sets out the four main views on who wrote the Pastorals ([USCCB, Introduction to the First Letter to Timothy](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/1timothy/0)), and Bible Odyssey lists them among the letters in which scholars find pseudepigraphy ([Bible Odyssey, Pseudepigraphy](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/pseudepigraphy/)). The letter itself names Paul as its sender and gives personal details of his imprisonment ([2 Timothy 1:1–2](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2TI01.htm#V1), [2 Timothy 4:9–18](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2TI04.htm#V9)).
