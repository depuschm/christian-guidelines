# Esther (Greek)

The Book of Esther "has come down to us in two versions: an older Hebrew version, and a Greek version based on a text similar to the Hebrew, but with additions and alterations" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Esther](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/esther/0)). The Hebrew book, which every church reads, has its own page: [Esther](../old-testament/esther.md). This page describes the Greek version and the passages it adds. The WEBBE translates the whole Greek book and explains: "The book of Esther in the Greek Septuagint contains 5 additions that the traditional Hebrew text doesn't have. These additions are recognised as Deuterocanonical Scripture by the Roman Catholic, Greek Orthodox, and Russian Orthodox Churches. Those additions are enclosed in [square brackets]" ([Esther (Greek) 1:1](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ESG01.htm#V1)). Most editions count six additions, which the New American Bible and the NETS translation label A to F; the WEBBE, which works the fourth into the Hebrew verses of chapter 5, and the HarperCollins Bible Dictionary count five ([Bible Odyssey, Esther, Additions to (HarperCollins Bible Dictionary)](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/dictionary/esther-additions-to/); [NETS, Esther, translated by Karen H. Jobes](https://ccat.sas.upenn.edu/nets/edition/17-esther-nets.pdf)). They are Mordecai's dream and the plot against the king (A), the king's decree against the Jews (B), the prayers of Mordecai and Esther (C), Esther before the king (D), the king's second decree (E), and the dream explained, with a closing note on the book's translation (F).

The additions change the book's character. "The Greek additions to Esther have many explicit references to God, as well as explicit descriptions of the beliefs and emotional states of Esther and Mordecai. They also elaborate on the content of the edicts from Ahasuerus as illustrations of Gentile attitudes toward Jews." The USCCB introduction adds that "While there are only a few contradictions between these Greek additions and the older Hebrew text, reading the book with these additions is a very different experience from reading the book without them", and that "The additions to Esther are an excellent example of a process that occurs throughout the Bible: further reflections on the story become part of the story itself" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Esther](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/esther/0)). The Greek translation names God even outside the additions: where the Hebrew says that "the king couldn't sleep" ([Esther 6:1](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/EST06.htm#V1)), the Greek says that "The Lord removed sleep from the king" ([Esther (Greek) 6:1](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ESG06.htm#V1)). The NETS translator Karen H. Jobes notes that in the Greek, "Explanatory phrases are sometimes added, especially to make God's presence explicit (e.g., 6.13)" ([Esther (Greek) 6:13](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ESG06.htm#V13)).

The Greek book ends with a note that "Dositheus, who said he was a priest and Levite, and Ptolemeus his son brought this letter of Purim", which, they said, "Lysimachus the son of Ptolemeus, who was in Jerusalem, had interpreted", "In the fourth year of the reign of Ptolemeus and Cleopatra" ([Esther (Greek) 10:14](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ESG10.htm#V14)). The New American Bible's note dates the Greek version from it "somewhere between 116 B.C. and 48 B.C." ([USCCB, Esther, chapter 10, with notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/esther/10)), and Jobes that the note, "if historically reliable, would allow for three possible dates for the origin of the translation: 114/13 BCE, 78/77 BCE, or 48 BCE". She finds that "There is no manuscript evidence that the six additional chapters found in the Greek versions ever existed in Hebrew or Aramaic", that "their style and syntax indicates that they were composed in Greek, with the possible exception of addition D", and that "Additions B and E are royal memos and exhibit an overworked style that satirizes the pompous and self-serving character of the king" ([NETS, Esther, translated by Karen H. Jobes](https://ccat.sas.upenn.edu/nets/edition/17-esther-nets.pdf)). The additions are not part of the Jewish Bible, but the 1909 Catholic Encyclopedia reports that "Origen tells us that they existed in Theodotion's version, and that they were used by Josephus" in his history of the Jews ([New Advent, Catholic Encyclopedia (1909), Esther](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/05549a.htm)).

The additions are printed in three ways. The church father Jerome, translating Esther into Latin from the Hebrew, moved them to the end of the book, and the medieval chapter divisions then "numbered the additions consecutively as though they formed a direct continuation of the Hebrew text" ([Bible Gateway, Greek Esther 1 (NRSVUE)](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Greek%20Esther%201&version=NRSVUE)). In the Latin Vulgate they follow the Hebrew book as chapters 10:4–16:24 ([Munteanu, Canoniques, non-canoniques ou bons à lire ?](https://www.diacronia.ro/ro/indexing/details/A25808/pdf); [New Advent, Catholic Encyclopedia (1909), Esther](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/05549a.htm)), and the King James Version, which printed them among the Apocrypha, kept those numbers. The Reformed commentator Matthew Henry describes "the apocryphal addition to it (which is not in the Hebrew, nor was ever received by the Jews into the canon), containing six chapters", which "begins thus, Then Mordecai said, God has done these things" ([Matthew Henry, Commentary on the Whole Bible, Esther: Introduction](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/henry/mhc2.Esth.i.html)). The NRSVUE prints the whole Greek book in its own order but keeps these chapter numbers; the New American Bible prints the additions in place and labels them A to F; and the WEBBE, as the outline below shows, prints them in place within the verses of the Hebrew chapters.

The churches disagree about the additions, though every church reads the Hebrew book ([which books each church reads](../README.md#which-books-each-church-reads)). The **Catholic Church** counts "Esther" among the 46 books of its Old Testament ([Catechism of the Catholic Church, 120](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__PR.HTM)): "the whole book, including the Greek additions, was included in the canon of Scripture by the Council of Trent" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Esther](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/esther/0); [Council of Trent, Session IV](https://history.hanover.edu/texts/trent/ct04.html)). The **Orthodox** churches read the Greek book, and count its additions among the *anaginoskómena*, the "books to be read" ([Munteanu, Canoniques, non-canoniques ou bons à lire ?](https://www.diacronia.ro/ro/indexing/details/A25808/pdf)). The **Church of England** names "The rest of the Book of Esther" among the books that "the Church doth read for example of life and instruction of manners; but yet doth it not apply them to establish any doctrine" ([Church of England, Articles of Religion, VI](https://www.churchofengland.org/liturgy/book-of-common-prayer/articles-religion#VI)). **Luther**'s Bible printed the additions to Esther among the Apocrypha, books "useful and good to read" ([Rösel, Luther und die Apokryphen](https://www.die-bibel.de/luther-und-die-apokryphen)). The **Reformed** Westminster Confession holds that the books called Apocrypha "are no part of the canon of the Scripture" and are not to be used otherwise "than other human writings" ([Westminster Confession of Faith, 1.3](https://www.opc.org/wcf.html)).

## How the churches read it

Where the churches, or their interpreters, read the book differently, or where it has played a part in their history. Each entry starts with its source's score.

- `7` [Council of Trent, Session IV](https://history.hanover.edu/texts/trent/ct04.html) (Hanover Historical Texts Project): **the whole book as Scripture.** The Council of Trent listed Esther among the sacred and canonical books in 1546 and condemned anyone who does not receive "the said books entire with all their parts, as they have been used to be read in the Catholic Church, and as they are contained in the old Latin vulgate edition". The 1909 Catholic Encyclopedia explains that the chapters from the Greek "were considered deuterocanonical, and, after St. Jerome, were separated from the ten chapters taken from the Hebrew which were called protocanonical", and that after the first Protestant reformers "wished only to reject the deuterocanonical parts", these "were declared by the Council of Trent… to be canonical and inspired" ([New Advent, Catholic Encyclopedia (1909), Esther](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/05549a.htm)). The **Catholic** Church reads Esther's prayer from the Greek, [Esther (Greek) 4:29–42](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ESG04.htm#V29), at Mass on Thursday of the first week of Lent, with [Matthew 7:7–12](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MAT07.htm#V7), and its Catechism names Esther among the holy women of Israel, with Mary as the purest figure among them (see [Esther: how the churches read it](../old-testament/esther.md#how-the-churches-read-it)).
- `7` [Munteanu, Canoniques, non-canoniques ou bons à lire ?](https://www.diacronia.ro/ro/indexing/details/A25808/pdf) (Diacronia, in French): **the Esther of the Orthodox Bible.** Ştefan Munteanu writes that Orthodox Bibles include the Greek additions to Esther, six passages that modern translations mark with letters, and that the Orthodox count them, with the other books of the Septuagint that are not in the Hebrew Bible, among the *anaginoskómena*. He notes that the first printed Slavonic Bible, at Ostrog in 1581, translated Esther afresh from the Septuagint and completed it, and that in the ninth century Patriarch Nicephorus of Constantinople still listed Esther among the books whose place in the canon was disputed. The Orthodox Church in America describes the Orthodox Bible as following "generally that of the Septuagint, the Greek translation of the Hebrew Bible" ([Orthodox Church in America, The Orthodox Faith: Old Testament, History](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/doctrine-scripture/old-testament/history)).
- `7` [Origen, Letter to Africanus](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0414.htm) (New Advent): **the additions in the early church.** The church father Origen knew that "Of the Book of Esther neither the prayer of Mardochaios nor that of Esther, both fitted to edify the reader, is found in the Hebrew. Neither are the letters". He asked whether, on noticing such differences, "we are immediately to reject as spurious the copies in use in our Churches", and answered with the proverb "You shall not remove the ancient landmarks which your fathers have set." Clement of Rome, writing to the church in Corinth, already praised Esther, who "being perfect in faith, exposed herself to no less danger", and "with fasting and humiliation… entreated the everlasting God, who sees all things" ([Clement of Rome, Letter to the Corinthians, 55](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/1010.htm)). Athanasius counted Esther among the books "to be read", outside the canon (see [Esther: how the churches read it](../old-testament/esther.md#how-the-churches-read-it)).
- `7` [Church of England, Articles of Religion, VI](https://www.churchofengland.org/liturgy/book-of-common-prayer/articles-religion#VI): **the Hebrew book in the canon, the additions read.** The **Church of England** counts "The Book of Esther" among the canonical books of the Old Testament, and "The rest of the Book of Esther" among the books read "for example of life and instruction of manners". The **Reformed** Westminster Confession holds that the books called Apocrypha "are no part of the canon of the Scripture" ([Westminster Confession of Faith, 1.3](https://www.opc.org/wcf.html)), and Matthew Henry notes that the "apocryphal addition" is "not in the Hebrew, nor was ever received by the Jews into the canon", while finding that in the Hebrew book "the finger of God is" at work even where his name is missing ([Matthew Henry, Commentary on the Whole Bible, Esther: Introduction](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/henry/mhc2.Esth.i.html)).
- `7` [Rösel, Luther und die Apokryphen](https://www.die-bibel.de/luther-und-die-apokryphen) (Deutsche Bibelgesellschaft, in German): **the additions in the Luther Bible.** Martin Rösel writes that Luther took the additions to Esther and Daniel into his Bible among the Apocrypha, which it printed between the Testaments, and that the 2017 revision of the Luther Bible translated some of the additions to Esther afresh from the Greek of the Göttingen Septuagint. Luther's own harsh words about the book of Esther are reported on the [Esther](../old-testament/esther.md#how-the-churches-read-it) page.
- `6` [Jerome, Prefaces to the Vulgate Old Testament: Esther](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/schaff/npnf206.vii.iii.vii.html) (Christian Classics Ethereal Library): **Jerome's Latin Esther.** The editors of the *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers* summarise Jerome's preface of 404: he "Merely assures them", his readers Paula and Eustochium, "that he is acting as a faithful translator, adding nothing of his own". The 1909 Catholic Encyclopedia reports that Jerome, "finding them in the Septuagint and the Old Latin version, placed them at the end of his almost literal translation of the existing Hebrew text, and indicated the place they occupied in the Septuagint" ([New Advent, Catholic Encyclopedia (1909), Esther](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/05549a.htm)). The NRSVUE's note traces the "disordered chapter numbers" of the Western Bibles to this displacement and to the chapter divisions of Stephen Langton ([Bible Gateway, Greek Esther 1 (NRSVUE)](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Greek%20Esther%201&version=NRSVUE)).

## Open questions

Questions on which the churches or scholars disagree. These guidelines don't pick a side. Questions about the story itself, such as whether it is history and why the Hebrew book never names God, are on the [Esther](../old-testament/esther.md#open-questions) page.

- `7` [USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Esther](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/esther/0) (New American Bible, Revised Edition): **why were the additions written, and do they change the book's meaning?** The USCCB introduction finds that the Greek additions make the book's faith explicit, so that reading it with them "is a very different experience", and its note on Mordecai's words about help "from another place" in the Hebrew ([Esther 4:14](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/EST04.htm#V14)) says that "probably Mordecai refers to divine aid; the Greek additions (C) are explicit about this" ([USCCB, Esther, chapter 4, with notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/esther/4)). Karen H. Jobes finds that "Additions A and F clearly function together, being the apocalyptic dream of Mardochaios (A) and its interpretation (F)", that E "was composed to answer the prayers of Mardochaios and Esther voiced in C", and that C "shows influence from the Greek translation of Moyses' intercessory prayer in Deut 9.26" ([NETS, Esther, translated by Karen H. Jobes](https://ccat.sas.upenn.edu/nets/edition/17-esther-nets.pdf)). The 1909 Catholic Encyclopedia answered critics who called Esther irreligious that "they have in mind only the first or protocanonical part, not the entire book, which is manifestly religious" ([New Advent, Catholic Encyclopedia (1909), Esther](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/05549a.htm)). BibleProject, by contrast, holds that "Both editions of Esther contain the same core message: God is always at work behind the scenes to fulfill his promises" ([BibleProject, Esther (Second Edition)](https://bibleproject.com/videos/esther-second-edition/)).
- `7` [NETS, Esther, translated by Karen H. Jobes](https://ccat.sas.upenn.edu/nets/edition/17-esther-nets.pdf) (International Organization for Septuagint and Cognate Studies): **which Greek text, and in what language were the additions first written?** "Esther is one of three books in the Hebrew canon to have survived in two distinct Greek versions": the Septuagint and a shorter "Alpha-text", and "It is not known with certainty which of the two Greek versions is the older or if one text is a revision of the other". The additions are "almost identical in both Greek versions", which shows, Jobes argues, "that they were not a part of the original Greek text of either, but were inserted later into both versions", and "there is some evidence that the AT preserves the older form of additions A, B, C, E, and F". On their language, she finds them composed in Greek, except perhaps D; the 1909 Catholic Encyclopedia held that "they are most probably translations from an original Hebrew or Chaldaic text" ([New Advent, Catholic Encyclopedia (1909), Esther](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/05549a.htm)).
- `6` [Bible Odyssey, Esther, Additions to (HarperCollins Bible Dictionary)](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/dictionary/esther-additions-to/): **which text is Scripture: the Hebrew, the Greek, or both?** "The Additions to Esther are treated as a part of the Apocrypha by Protestants, but are regarded as deuterocanonical by Roman Catholics." The Catholic Church receives the whole book, as it was read in the Latin Vulgate ([Council of Trent, Session IV](https://history.hanover.edu/texts/trent/ct04.html)); the Orthodox churches read the Greek book ([Munteanu, Canoniques, non-canoniques ou bons à lire ?](https://www.diacronia.ro/ro/indexing/details/A25808/pdf)); the Church of England and the Lutheran churches read the additions but do not base doctrine on them; and Reformed churches hold the Hebrew book alone to be canonical (see the entries above and [which books each church reads](../README.md#which-books-each-church-reads)). Origen defended the Greek text that the churches already read ([Origen, Letter to Africanus](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0414.htm)); Jerome followed the Hebrew and set the additions apart.
