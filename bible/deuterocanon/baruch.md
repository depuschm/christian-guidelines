# Baruch

The Book of Baruch presents itself as "the words of the book which Baruch the son of Nerias… wrote in Babylon" after the Chaldeans "took Jerusalem and burnt it with fire" ([Baruch 1:1–2](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/BAR01.htm#V1)). Baruch was the friend and scribe of the prophet Jeremiah, who wrote down Jeremiah's words and read them to the people in the temple ([Jeremiah 36:4](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JER36.htm#V4); see the [Jeremiah](../old-testament/jeremiah.md) page and [Bible Odyssey, Baruch (HarperCollins Bible Dictionary)](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/dictionary/baruch/)). The USCCB introduction calls the book "a collection of four very different compositions, ending with a work entitled 'The Letter of Jeremiah,' which circulated separately in major manuscripts of the Greek tradition". It notes that "The fictional setting is Babylon", that "The original language may have been Hebrew, but only the Greek and other versions have been preserved", and that "No certain date can be given for the book, but it may have been edited in final form during the last two centuries B.C." ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Baruch](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/baruch/0)). Who wrote it, and when, is one of the open questions below.

The USCCB introduction reads the book as an attempt "to explain the trauma of the exile in terms of a Deuteronomic cycle: sin (of Israel), punishment, repentance, and return". Its parts are a confession of sin and prayer for mercy, which "has remarkable similarities to Dn 9 and to parts of Jeremiah" (see the [Daniel](../old-testament/daniel.md) page); a poem on Wisdom, which takes up the question of [Job 28:12–28](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JOB28.htm#V12) and identifies Wisdom with the law, as [Sirach 24:8–23](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/SIR24.htm#V8) does (see the [Sirach](sirach.md) page); a poem of consolation for Jerusalem that "resembles parts of Is 40–66"; and the Letter of Jeremiah, "a polemic against idolatry" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Baruch](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/baruch/0)). The NETS translator Tony S. L. Michael finds that it is "held almost unanimously among scholars that the first section (1.1–3.8) was originally written in Hebrew", while the poems from 3:9 onward may have been composed in Greek, "likely… the work of a different author" ([NETS, Barouch, translated by Tony S. L. Michael](https://ccat.sas.upenn.edu/nets/edition/35-barouch-nets.pdf)).

The **Letter of Jeremiah** is printed in two ways. The WEBBE, like the Latin Vulgate and Catholic Bibles, prints it as the sixth chapter of Baruch. In the Septuagint it is a book of its own: "Although in the Septuagint the Letter of Ieremias (LetIer) is a discrete unit, in the Vulgate it forms chapter 6 of the book of Barouch" ([NETS, Letter of Ieremias, translated by Benjamin G. Wright](https://ccat.sas.upenn.edu/nets/edition/37-letier-nets.pdf)). The NETS edition prints the Greek books in the order Ieremias, Barouch, Lamentations and Letter of Ieremias ([NETS, electronic edition](https://ccat.sas.upenn.edu/nets/edition/)), and the Orthodox Church in America notes that "The books of Baruch and the Letter of Jeremiah from the apocrypha go together with this prophetic book in the Orthodox version of the Bible" ([Orthodox Church in America, The Orthodox Faith: Old Testament, Prophets](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/doctrine-scripture/old-testament/prophets); see the [Lamentations](../old-testament/lamentations.md) page). This page covers both.

Neither book is part of the Jewish Bible, and the churches disagree about their place in theirs ([which books each church reads](../README.md#which-books-each-church-reads)). The **Catholic Church** counts "Baruch" among the 46 books of its Old Testament ([Catechism of the Catholic Church, 120](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__PR.HTM)), and the Council of Trent's list of sacred and canonical books in 1546 named "Jeremias, with Baruch" ([Council of Trent, Session IV](https://history.hanover.edu/texts/trent/ct04.html)). The **Orthodox** churches read them as two of the *anaginoskómena*, the "books to be read" ([Munteanu, Canoniques, non-canoniques ou bons à lire ?](https://www.diacronia.ro/ro/indexing/details/A25808/pdf)). The **Church of England** names "Baruch the Prophet" among the books that "the Church doth read for example of life and instruction of manners; but yet doth it not apply them to establish any doctrine" ([Church of England, Articles of Religion, VI](https://www.churchofengland.org/liturgy/book-of-common-prayer/articles-religion#VI)). **Luther**'s Bible printed Baruch among the Apocrypha, books "useful and good to read" ([Rösel, Luther und die Apokryphen](https://www.die-bibel.de/luther-und-die-apokryphen)). The **Reformed** Westminster Confession holds that the books called Apocrypha "are no part of the canon of the Scripture" and are not to be used otherwise "than other human writings" ([Westminster Confession of Faith, 1.3](https://www.opc.org/wcf.html)).

The New American Bible and the NRSVUE divide the WEBBE's 3:34 into two verses, so the WEBBE's 3:35–37 are their 3:36–38 ([USCCB, Baruch, chapter 3, with notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/baruch/3); [Baruch 3:34–37](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/BAR03.htm#V34)). In the Letter, the WEBBE numbers the heading as 6:1, so its 6:2–73 are the New American Bible's 6:1–72 ([USCCB, Baruch, chapter 6, with notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/baruch/6)) and verses 1–72 of the Letter of Jeremiah in the NRSVUE and NETS.

## How the churches read it

Where the churches, or their interpreters, read the book differently, or where it has played a part in their history. Each entry starts with its source's score.

- `7` [Augustine, City of God, Book XVIII](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/120118.htm) (New Advent): **Wisdom on earth, and Christ.** In chapter 33, the church father Augustine quotes [Baruch 3:35–37](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/BAR03.htm#V35) among the prophecies "concerning Christ": "This is my God, and there shall none other be accounted of in comparison of Him… afterwards He was seen on the earth, and conversed with men." He adds that "Some attribute this testimony not to Jeremiah, but to his secretary, who was called Baruch; but it is more commonly ascribed to Jeremiah." The **Orthodox** Church reads [Baruch 3:35–4:4](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/BAR03.htm#V35), under the name of Jeremiah, at the services of the eve of the Nativity of Christ ([Orthodox Church in America, Scripture Readings for 24 December 2026 (Eve of the Nativity of Christ)](https://www.oca.org/readings/daily/2026/12/24)), and the New American Bible's cross-references for the verse point to [John 1:14](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JHN01.htm#V14), "The Word became flesh and lived amongst us" ([USCCB, Baruch, chapter 3, with notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/baruch/3)).
- `7` [Athanasius, Letter 39](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/2806039.htm) (New Advent): **the book in the early church.** The church father Athanasius of Alexandria, who listed the Wisdom of Solomon and Sirach among books "not indeed included in the Canon" (see the [Wisdom of Solomon](wisdom-of-solomon.md) page), counted Baruch and the Letter within the Old Testament itself: "Jeremiah with Baruch, Lamentations, and the epistle, one book". The 1907 Catholic Encyclopedia notes that the Latin text of Baruch in the Vulgate "goes back to the old Latin version anterior to St. Jerome", and reports that most critics of its day thought Jerome "was decidedly correct when he called this writing", the Letter, "pseudepigraphos, that is, inscribed with a false name" ([New Advent, Catholic Encyclopedia (1907), Baruch](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/02319c.htm)).
- `6` [USCCB, Readings for the Easter Vigil (4 April 2026)](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/readings/040426.cfm): **the poem on Wisdom at the Easter Vigil.** The **Catholic** Church reads [Baruch 3:9–15](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/BAR03.htm#V9) and [Baruch 3:32–4:4](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/BAR03.htm#V32), "Hear, O Israel, the commandments of life", as the sixth of the Old Testament readings of the Easter Vigil. The Revised Common Lectionary appoints the same reading at the Easter Vigil in all three years ([Vanderbilt Divinity Library, Revised Common Lectionary: Sunday citations](https://lectionary.library.vanderbilt.edu/sunday-citations/)).
- `6` [USCCB, Readings for the Second Sunday of Advent (8 December 2024)](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/readings/120824.cfm): **Jerusalem's homecoming in Advent.** The **Catholic** Church reads [Baruch 5:1–9](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/BAR05.htm#V1), "Jerusalem, take off your robe of mourning and misery", on the Second Sunday of Advent in Year C, and the Revised Common Lectionary appoints Baruch 5:1–9 for the same Sunday ([Vanderbilt Divinity Library, Revised Common Lectionary: Sunday citations](https://lectionary.library.vanderbilt.edu/sunday-citations/)). The **Church of England** sings the end of the poem as a canticle in its daily prayer, "A Song of Baruch", with the refrain "Lead us, O God, with joy to walk in the light of glory" ([Church of England, Common Worship: Daily Prayer, Canticle 49: A Song of Baruch](https://www.churchofengland.org/liturgy/common-worship/daily-prayer/canticles-daily-prayer/49-song-baruch); [Baruch 5:5–9](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/BAR05.htm#V5)).

## Open questions

Questions on which the churches or scholars disagree, for example about the book's authorship or the meaning of a passage. These guidelines don't pick a side.

- `7` [USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Baruch](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/baruch/0) (New American Bible, Revised Edition): **who wrote it, and when?** The book names Baruch, Jeremiah's scribe, as its author and sets it in Babylon "in the fifth year" after Jerusalem fell ([Baruch 1:1–2](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/BAR01.htm#V1)). The 1907 Catholic Encyclopedia gave the grounds on which Catholic scholars then held to Baruch's authorship, as the compiler of the book: "The book is ascribed to Baruch by its title; it has always been regarded as Baruch's work by tradition", and nothing in it need be later than his time; it reported the view of non-Catholic scholars that the contents "point to a much later compiler than Baruch" ([New Advent, Catholic Encyclopedia (1907), Baruch](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/02319c.htm)). The USCCB introduction today calls the setting "fictional" and suggests a final edition "during the last two centuries B.C.", and the NETS translator sees "considerable linguistic ties with the book of Ieremias" in the first section and a "different author" for the poems ([NETS, Barouch, translated by Tony S. L. Michael](https://ccat.sas.upenn.edu/nets/edition/35-barouch-nets.pdf)).
- `7` [NETS, Letter of Ieremias, translated by Benjamin G. Wright](https://ccat.sas.upenn.edu/nets/edition/37-letier-nets.pdf) (International Organization for Septuagint and Cognate Studies): **one book or two, and who wrote the Letter?** The Letter calls itself "A copy of a letter that Jeremy sent to those who were to be led captives into Babylon" ([Baruch 6:1](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/BAR06.htm#V1)). The 1907 Catholic Encyclopedia noted that "The fact that the sixth chapter of Baruch bears the title, 'The Epistle of Jeremiah', has been, and is still in the eyes of many, a decisive reason for holding the time-honoured view that the great prophet is its author", while "most contemporary critics argue that the Greek style… proves that it was originally written not in Hebrew, but in Greek" ([New Advent, Catholic Encyclopedia (1907), Baruch](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/02319c.htm)). The HarperCollins Bible Dictionary holds that it was "composed most likely in the fourth century BCE in order to provide Jews with arguments to counter their gentile neighbors' belief in the reality of idols" ([Bible Odyssey, Letter of Jeremiah, The (HarperCollins Bible Dictionary)](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/dictionary/letter-of-jeremiah-the/)). Wright weighs signs of translation from Hebrew against features of original Greek composition and concludes that "the evidence for LetIer being a translation is inconclusive".
- `7` [USCCB, Baruch, chapter 3, with notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/baruch/3) (New American Bible, Revised Edition): **does "she appeared upon earth" speak of the law or of Christ?** The WEBBE renders the verse "Afterward she appeared upon earth, and lived with men" ([Baruch 3:37](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/BAR03.htm#V37)), with Wisdom as its subject, and the next verse identifies Wisdom with "the book of God's commandments". The New American Bible reads it of Wisdom given to Israel: "As in Sir 24:8, Wisdom is given to Israel but also is said to live with all human beings (Prv 8:31)". The 1907 Catholic Encyclopedia reads the poem as saying that "only in the guise of the Law has Wisdom appeared on the earth and become accessible to man" ([New Advent, Catholic Encyclopedia (1907), Baruch](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/02319c.htm)). Augustine quotes the verse as "afterwards He was seen on the earth, and conversed with men", and takes it as a prophecy of Christ ([Augustine, City of God, Book XVIII](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/120118.htm)); the Orthodox Church reads the passage on the eve of the Nativity (see above).
