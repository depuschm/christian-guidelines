# Sirach

The Wisdom of Jesus son of Sirach, also called Ecclesiasticus, is a book of wisdom teaching written in Jerusalem. Its author signs it: "I Jesus, the son of Sirach Eleazar, of Jerusalem, who out of his heart poured forth wisdom" ([Sirach 50:27](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/SIR50.htm#V27)). The USCCB introduction calls him "a sage who lived in Jerusalem", who wrote to help his contemporaries "maintain religious faith and integrity through study of the books sacred to the Jewish tradition", in maxims on "friendship, education, poverty and wealth, laws, religious worship, and many other matters" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Wisdom of Ben Sira](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/sirach/0)). The HarperCollins Bible Dictionary describes it as "a book of instruction and proverbs written in Hebrew around 180 BCE in Jerusalem by an instructor of wealthy youths" ([Bible Odyssey, Sirach (HarperCollins Bible Dictionary)](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/dictionary/sirach/)). In chapter 24, Wisdom says that she "came out of the mouth of the Most High" and made her home in Israel, and Ben Sira identifies her with "the book of the covenant of the Most High God, the law which Moses commanded us" ([Sirach 24:1–12](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/SIR24.htm#V1), [Sirach 24:23](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/SIR24.htm#V23)). The book closes with a hymn in praise of Israel's ancestors, from Enoch to the high priest Simon son of Onias, a prayer of thanks and a poem on the search for wisdom ([Sirach 44:16](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/SIR44.htm#V16), [Sirach 50:1](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/SIR50.htm#V1), [Sirach 51:1–30](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/SIR51.htm#V1)).

The book has a foreword by the author's grandson, who translated it into Greek after he came "into Egypt in the thirty-eighth year of Energetes the king", and who asks pardon for his translation, "For things originally spoken in Hebrew don't have the same force in them when they are translated into another language" ([Sirach 1](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/SIR01.htm), where the WEBBE prints the prologue before the first verse). The USCCB introduction dates the Hebrew book "by ca. 175" and the translation "after 117 B.C."; the HarperCollins Bible Dictionary dates the translation "sometime after 132 BCE" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Wisdom of Ben Sira](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/sirach/0); [Bible Odyssey, Sirach (HarperCollins Bible Dictionary)](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/dictionary/sirach/)). "Until the close of the nineteenth century the Wisdom of Ben Sira was known to Christians in translations", but since 1896 "more than two thirds of the book in Hebrew" has been found ([USCCB, Introduction to the Wisdom of Ben Sira](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/sirach/0)): fragments of six manuscripts from the Cairo Geniza, a manuscript from Masada of the first century BCE with parts of chapters 39–44, and small fragments from Qumran ([NETS, Sirach, translated by Benjamin G. Wright](https://ccat.sas.upenn.edu/nets/edition/30-sirach-nets.pdf)). "In the Vulgate, the book is called Ecclesiasticus (the church's book)" (NETS); the USCCB introduction suggests the name is "perhaps due to the extensive use the church made of this book in presenting moral teaching to catechumens and to the faithful".

The book is not part of the Jewish Bible. "The Jews, who never admitted its canonicity", still quoted it, and "the book retained its popularity among the Jews despite its exclusion from the canon" ([Jewish Encyclopedia, Sirach, The Wisdom of Jesus the Son of (1906)](https://www.jewishencyclopedia.com/articles/8618-jesus-sirach)). The Talmud cites it as "the book of Ben Sira: Seek not things concealed from you" ([Sefaria, Chagigah 13a:2](https://www.sefaria.org/Chagigah.13a.2); compare [Sirach 3:21–22](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/SIR03.htm#V21)); one rabbi held that "It is also prohibited to read the book of ben Sira", yet "we teach the superior matters that are in it even in public" ([Sefaria, Sanhedrin 100b:3](https://www.sefaria.org/Sanhedrin.100b.3); [Sefaria, Sanhedrin 100b:9](https://www.sefaria.org/Sanhedrin.100b.9)). The churches differ on the book, as they do on the whole deuterocanon ([Which books each church reads](../README.md#which-books-each-church-reads)). The **Catholic Church** counts "Sirach (Ecclesiasticus)" among the 46 books of the Old Testament ([Catechism of the Catholic Church, 120](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__PR.HTM)), as the Council of Trent did in 1546 ([Council of Trent, Session IV](https://history.hanover.edu/texts/trent/ct04.html)). The **Orthodox churches** read it among the *anaginoskómena*, the books to be read ([Munteanu, Canoniques, non-canoniques ou bons à lire ?](https://www.diacronia.ro/ro/indexing/details/A25808/pdf)). The **Church of England** names "Jesus the Son of Sirach" among the books it reads "for example of life and instruction of manners", but not "to establish any doctrine" ([Church of England, Articles of Religion, VI](https://www.churchofengland.org/liturgy/book-of-common-prayer/articles-religion#VI)). **Luther's** Bible printed it among the Apocrypha, between the Testaments ([Rösel, Luther und die Apokryphen](https://www.die-bibel.de/luther-und-die-apokryphen)), and the **Reformed** Westminster Confession counts the Apocrypha "no part of the canon of the Scripture" ([Westminster Confession of Faith, 1.3](https://www.opc.org/wcf.html)).

## Outline

The USCCB introduction notes that the book's contents "are of a discursive nature, not easily divided into separate parts": "Chapters 1–43 deal largely with moral instruction; 44:1–50:24 contain a eulogy of the heroes of Israel", followed by two appendices ([USCCB, Introduction to the Wisdom of Ben Sira](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/sirach/0)). The outline groups the chapters by their main subjects. Verse numbers differ between translations in places: the WEBBE's 34:21–22, for example, is 34:25–27 in the New American Bible ([USCCB, Sirach, chapter 34, with notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/sirach/34)). All the Greek manuscripts reverse two blocks of text in chapters 30–36, an order that the critical Göttingen edition and the NRSV put right, and later Greek manuscripts add sayings that some translations print and others leave in footnotes ([NETS, Sirach, translated by Benjamin G. Wright](https://ccat.sas.upenn.edu/nets/edition/30-sirach-nets.pdf)). The WEBBE leaves out a few such verses, such as 1:5 and 1:7, noting that they are "omitted by the best authorities" ([Sirach 1](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/SIR01.htm)).

## How the churches read it

Where the churches, or their interpreters, read the book differently, or where it has played a part in their history. Each entry starts with its source's score.

- `7` [Athanasius, Letter 39](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/2806039.htm) (New Advent): **a book for teaching new Christians.** Athanasius of Alexandria listed "the Wisdom of Sirach" among books "not indeed included in the Canon, but appointed by the Fathers to be read by those who newly join us". Rufinus called it one of the books "our fathers call not Canonical but Ecclesiastical", which "the Latins called by the general title Ecclesiasticus", to be read in the churches "but not appealed to for the confirmation of doctrine" ([Rufinus, Commentary on the Apostles' Creed, 38](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/2711.htm)). Augustine, by contrast, held that Wisdom and Ecclesiasticus "are to be reckoned among the prophetical books, since they have attained recognition as being authoritative" ([Augustine, On Christian Doctrine, Book II, 8](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/12022.htm)). The Jewish Encyclopedia sums up the early church's division: the councils of Hippo (393) and Carthage (397), Pope Innocent I and Augustine "all regard it as a canonical book", against "the Council of Laodicea, of Jerome, and of Rufinus of Aquileia, which authorities rank it among the ecclesiastical books" ([Jewish Encyclopedia, Sirach, The Wisdom of Jesus the Son of (1906)](https://www.jewishencyclopedia.com/articles/8618-jesus-sirach)).
- `7` [Catechism of the Catholic Church, 2214–2231](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P7U.HTM): **Catholic teaching and worship.** The Catechism draws on Sirach for the duties of children: it quotes 7:27–28 on gratitude to parents, and 3:2–6, 12–13 and 16 on caring for them "in old age and in times of illness, loneliness, or distress" (2215, 2218). It also quotes 30:1–2 on bringing up children (2223; compare the open question on the "rod" on the [Proverbs](../old-testament/proverbs.md#open-questions) page), 15:14 on human freedom (1730, [Catechism of the Catholic Church, 1730](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P5M.HTM)), and 17:22 on almsgiving as "a work of justice pleasing to God" (2447, [Catechism of the Catholic Church, 2443–2449](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P8F.HTM)). At Mass, Sirach 3:2–6, 12–14 is the first reading on the feast of the Holy Family ([USCCB, Readings for the Feast of the Holy Family, 28 December 2025](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/readings/122825.cfm)), and Sirach 27:30–28:7, on forgiveness, is read with the parable of the unforgiving servant in Matthew 18:21–35 ([USCCB, Readings for the Twenty-fourth Sunday in Ordinary Time, 13 September 2026](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/readings/091326.cfm)).
- `7` [Munteanu, Canoniques, non-canoniques ou bons à lire ?](https://www.diacronia.ro/ro/indexing/details/A25808/pdf) (Diacronia, 2013): **the Orthodox Church.** Ştefan Munteanu, of the St Sergius Orthodox Theological Institute in Paris, notes that the Apostolic Constitutions (canon 85) tell Christians to teach their young people the Wisdom of Sirach, and that Epiphanius called Sirach and the Wisdom of Solomon useful and profitable but did not count them among the Scriptures. The Orthodox churches read the book among the *anaginoskómena*. The Orthodox Church in America notes that of Ecclesiastes, Sirach and the Wisdom of Solomon, "only the Wisdom of Solomon, which is considered to be the last of them written, is read liturgically in the Orthodox Church" ([Orthodox Church in America, The Orthodox Faith: Old Testament, Wisdom](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/doctrine-scripture/old-testament/wisdom)).
- `7` [Church of England, Articles of Religion, VI](https://www.churchofengland.org/liturgy/book-of-common-prayer/articles-religion#VI): **Anglican churches.** Article VI names "Jesus the Son of Sirach" among the books that "(as Hierome saith) the Church doth read for example of life and instruction of manners; but yet doth it not apply them to establish any doctrine". The Anglican theologian Stephen Noll notes that the title Ecclesiasticus is used "even in the Book of Common Prayer", and that "Let us now praise famous men" comes from "the assigned reading for All Saints' Day in the Anglican liturgy, Ecclesiasticus 44:1-14" ([Stephen Noll, Let Us Now Praise Famous (and Unfamous) Men: The Wisdom of Sirach, Anglican Compass](https://anglicancompass.com/let-us-now-praise-famous-and-unfamous-men-the-wisdom-of-sirach/)). The Revised Common Lectionary, prepared by Anglican, Lutheran, Methodist, Reformed, Catholic and other churches in North America, sometimes takes its Old Testament reading from the Apocrypha ([Vanderbilt Divinity Library, Revised Common Lectionary: FAQ](https://lectionary.library.vanderbilt.edu/faq/)), and offers passages from Sirach 10, 15, 24, 27 and 35 ([Vanderbilt Divinity Library, Revised Common Lectionary: Sunday citations](https://lectionary.library.vanderbilt.edu/sunday-citations/)).
- `7` [Rösel, Luther und die Apokryphen](https://www.die-bibel.de/luther-und-die-apokryphen) (Deutsche Bibelgesellschaft): **Lutheran churches.** Luther kept Jesus Sirach in his Bible among the Apocrypha, under a heading that calls them books not held equal to holy Scripture but useful and good to read (*so nicht der heiligen Schrift gleich gehalten: und doch nützlich und gut zu lesen sind*). Martin Rösel names the rules for living of Jesus Sirach (*die Lebensregeln des Jesus Sirach*) among the apocryphal writings that were much loved in the church, and notes that scholars are sure Luther himself translated only some chapters of Sirach and the Wisdom of Solomon. The hymn "Now thank we all our God", written around 1636 by Martin Rinkart, a pastor in Eilenburg in Saxony, "alludes to the apocryphal book of Ecclesiasticus 50:22-24 (part of the Lutheran Bible)" ([Hymnary.org, Psalter Hymnal 454: Now Thank We All Our God](https://hymnary.org/hymn/PsH/454); [Sirach 50:22–24](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/SIR50.htm#V22)).
- `6` [USCCB, Sirach, chapter 28, with notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/sirach/28) (New American Bible, Revised Edition): **Sirach and the New Testament.** The New American Bible's cross-references link Sirach's teaching on forgiveness to Jesus' words in [Matthew 6:14](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MAT06.htm#V14) and the parable in [Matthew 18:23–35](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MAT18.htm#V23), its warning about the tongue to [James 3:5–12](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JAS03.htm#V5), and its closing invitation to learn wisdom to "Cf. Mt 11:28" ([USCCB, Sirach, chapter 51, with notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/sirach/51); [Matthew 11:28](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MAT11.htm#V28)). The Jewish Encyclopedia records that the book is cited "in the Epistle of James", the Didache and the Epistle of Barnabas, and that Clement of Alexandria and Origen "quote from it repeatedly" as a holy book ([Jewish Encyclopedia, Sirach, The Wisdom of Jesus the Son of (1906)](https://www.jewishencyclopedia.com/articles/8618-jesus-sirach)).

## Open questions

Questions on which the churches or scholars disagree, for example about the book's text or the meaning of a passage. These guidelines don't pick a side.

- `7` [NETS, Sirach, translated by Benjamin G. Wright](https://ccat.sas.upenn.edu/nets/edition/30-sirach-nets.pdf): **which text: the Hebrew or the Greek, the shorter or the longer?** Hebrew survives for "about two-thirds of the book", in manuscripts with "numerous mistakes, additions and corruptions"; the Greek manuscripts reverse two blocks of text in chapters 30–36; and "a number of proverbs were added to the book" in a later Greek form, which the NRSV mostly puts in footnotes. The USCCB introduction says the Hebrew texts "agree substantially with the Greek", and that the New American Bible "provides a critical translation based on the evidence of all the ancient texts" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Wisdom of Ben Sira](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/sirach/0)). The German Lutheran Bible of 2017, by contrast, translates the longer Greek text and puts its additions in square brackets, so that readers can see how the text grew ([Rösel, Luther und die Apokryphen](https://www.die-bibel.de/luther-und-die-apokryphen)); the WEBBE leaves out some of these lines as "omitted by the best authorities" ([Sirach 1](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/SIR01.htm)).
- `6` [USCCB, Sirach, chapter 3, with notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/sirach/3) (New American Bible, Revised Edition): **can almsgiving atone for sins?** Sirach says that "almsgiving will make atonement for sins", and that a son who honours his father "will make atonement for sins" ([Sirach 3:30](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/SIR03.htm#V30), [Sirach 3:3](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/SIR03.htm#V3)); the New American Bible's note explains that by honouring old parents "the sins of children are pardoned", and links 3:30 to [Tobit 12:9](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/TOB12.htm#V9) and [Daniel 4:27](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/DAN04.htm#V27) (Daniel 4:24 in its numbering), as the Tobit page discusses too. The **Catholic** Catechism counts "the practice of charity 'which covers a multitude of sins'" among the "means of obtaining forgiveness of sins" ([Catechism of the Catholic Church, 1434–1439](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P4B.HTM)). The **Reformed** theologian John Calvin, reading the parallel in Daniel, denied that "righteousness and mercy are able to propitiate God and redeem from punishment", adding, "far be it from us to suppose that there ever was any other ἀπολύτροσις (ransom) than the blood of Christ" ([John Calvin, Institutes of the Christian Religion, Book III, Chapter 4](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/calvin/institutes.v.v.html)).
- `6` [USCCB, Sirach, chapter 25, with notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/sirach/25) (New American Bible, Revised Edition): **how should readers take Ben Sira's sayings about women?** Besides its praise of a good wife, the book has harsh words about wives and daughters, and says, "The beginning of sin came from a woman. Because of her, we all die" ([Sirach 25:24](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/SIR25.htm#V24), [Sirach 42:9–14](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/SIR42.htm#V9)). The New American Bible's note says these statements "reflect the kind of instruction young Jewish males were exposed to in the early second century B.C. His patriarchal perspective is as unfair as it is one-sided", and that on the first sin "St. Paul, however, singles out Adam" ([Romans 5:12–19](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ROM05.htm#V12)). The scholar Claudia V. Camp argues that in Ben Sira's "gender anxiety" the woman "he fears most is his own wife", and links this to the rise of a sense of sacred books ([Claudia V. Camp, Why Is There a Bible and What Do Women Have To Do With It? Gender, Ben Sira, and the Canon, The Bible and Interpretation](https://bibleinterp.arizona.edu/articles/2015/01/cam398027)); the Jewish Encyclopedia spoke of his "unconquerable distrust of women" ([Jewish Encyclopedia, Sirach, The Wisdom of Jesus the Son of (1906)](https://www.jewishencyclopedia.com/articles/8618-jesus-sirach)). The Talmud asks whether the passage on daughters could be the reason the book was banned, and answers that the sages said the same; and Rav Yosef still taught from the book that "A good wife is a good gift" ([Sefaria, Sanhedrin 100b:5](https://www.sefaria.org/Sanhedrin.100b.5); [Sefaria, Sanhedrin 100b:9](https://www.sefaria.org/Sanhedrin.100b.9)).
- `6` [Claudia V. Camp, Why Is There a Bible and What Do Women Have To Do With It? Gender, Ben Sira, and the Canon](https://bibleinterp.arizona.edu/articles/2015/01/cam398027) (The Bible and Interpretation, 2015): **what the book shows about the Bible of its time, and why it was left out of the Jewish Bible.** The prologue speaks of "the law, the prophets, and the other books of our fathers", and the praise of the ancestors runs through the Law and the Prophets, naming "the twelve prophets" ([Sirach 1](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/SIR01.htm), [Sirach 49:10](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/SIR49.htm#V10)). Camp calls Sirach "the earliest source in which we find some recognition of components of what we would today call the Hebrew Bible". Why the book itself was left out is debated: the USCCB introduction says only that it was "not included in the Jewish Bible after the first century A.D." ([USCCB, Introduction to the Wisdom of Ben Sira](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/sirach/0)); the Jewish Encyclopedia put it down "in part to this imitation of the Greeks and these literary affectations" ([Jewish Encyclopedia, Sirach, The Wisdom of Jesus the Son of (1906)](https://www.jewishencyclopedia.com/articles/8618-jesus-sirach)); and in the Talmud, a rabbi asks which of its sayings could be the reason for the ban, and finds most of them already in Scripture or in the sages ([Sefaria, Sanhedrin 100b:3](https://www.sefaria.org/Sanhedrin.100b.3)).
