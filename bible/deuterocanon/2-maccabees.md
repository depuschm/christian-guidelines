# 2 Maccabees

The Second Book of Maccabees tells of the Jewish resistance to the Seleucid kings in the second century BC, from the attempt of Heliodorus on the temple treasury to Judas Maccabeus's victory over the general Nicanor. The New American Bible's introduction stresses that "it is not a sequel to 1 Maccabees": it covers "the events in Jewish history from the time of the high priest Onias III and King Seleucus IV (c. 180 B.C.) to the defeat of Nicanor's army (161 B.C.)". The author says that his book abridges "five books" by Jason of Cyrene ([2 Maccabees 2:23](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2MA02.htm#V23)); "both works were written in Greek, which explains why the Second Book of Maccabees was not included in the canon of the Hebrew Bible", and the book "would probably not have been produced much before the end of the second century B.C." ([Vatican, New American Bible, 2 Maccabees: Introduction](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0839/__PE4.HTM)). Two letters from the Jews of Jerusalem to the Jews of Egypt open the book ([2 Maccabees 1:1–2:18](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2MA01.htm#V1)). The NETS translator Joachim Schaper describes the main body as "a literary creation in its own right without a Hebrew parent text" ([NETS, 2 Makkabees, translated by Joachim Schaper](https://ccat.sas.upenn.edu/nets/edition/21-2makk-nets.pdf)). The two books of Maccabees are, in the words of the USCCB introduction, "independent accounts of events (in part identical)" ([USCCB, Introduction to the First Book of Maccabees](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/1maccabees/0)); the HarperCollins Bible Dictionary adds that 2 Maccabees "focuses upon the insult to the Temple and its cult, for which it holds the Jewish Hellenizers primarily responsible" ([Bible Odyssey, Maccabees, The Second Book of the (HarperCollins Bible Dictionary)](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/dictionary/maccabees-the-second-book-of-the/)).

The New American Bible's introduction holds that the book's purpose is "to give a theological interpretation to the history of the period", with "less interest… in the actual exploits of Judas Maccabeus than in God's marvelous interventions". It calls the author "the earliest known composer of stories that glorify God's holy martyrs", and names three teachings "Of theological importance": "the resurrection of the just on the last day", "the intercession of the saints in heaven for people living on earth", and "the power of the living to offer prayers and sacrifices for the dead" ([Vatican, New American Bible, 2 Maccabees: Introduction](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0839/__PE4.HTM)). Two of these passages, the martyrs of chapter 7 and Judas's offering for the dead in chapter 12, are read very differently by the churches, as the sections below set out.

The book is not part of the Jewish Bible, but some of its stories live on in Jewish tradition. The purification of the temple in [2 Maccabees 10:1–8](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2MA10.htm#V1) "is the origin of the Jewish feast of Hanukkah" ([USCCB, Introduction to the First Book of Maccabees](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/1maccabees/0)). Chris Seeman notes that "The new celebration was not originally called Hanukkah but 'Tabernacles of the month of Kislev'" ([Bible Odyssey, Chris Seeman, Hanukkah](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/hanukkah/); [2 Maccabees 1:9](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2MA01.htm#V9)). The Gospel of John sets a visit of Jesus to the temple at the same feast ([John 10:22](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JHN10.htm#V22)), which the New American Bible explains as "an eight-day festival of lights (Hebrew, Hanukkah)… to celebrate the Maccabees' rededication of the altar and reconsecration of the temple", with a reference to this book ([Vatican, New American Bible, John, chapter 10, with notes](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0839/__PXI.HTM)). The Talmud tells its own version of the story of "the woman and her seven sons who died as martyrs for the sake of the sanctification of God's name" ([Sefaria, Gittin 57b:14](https://www.sefaria.org/Gittin.57b.14)).

The churches disagree about the book's place in their Bibles ([which books each church reads](../README.md#which-books-each-church-reads)). The **Catholic Church** counts "1 and 2 Maccabees" among the 46 books of its Old Testament ([Catechism of the Catholic Church, 120](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__PR.HTM)), and the Council of Trent's list of sacred and canonical books in 1546 named "two books of the Machabees, the first and the second" ([Council of Trent, Session IV](https://history.hanover.edu/texts/trent/ct04.html)). The church father Augustine wrote that the books of Maccabees "are held as canonical, not by the Jews, but by the Church, on account of the extreme and wonderful sufferings of certain martyrs" ([Augustine, City of God, Book XVIII](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/120118.htm), chapter 36), while Athanasius of Alexandria's list names them neither among the canonical books nor among the books "to be read" ([Athanasius, Letter 39](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/2806039.htm)). The **Orthodox** churches read the book as one of the *anaginoskómena*, the "books to be read" ([Munteanu, Canoniques, non-canoniques ou bons à lire ?](https://www.diacronia.ro/ro/indexing/details/A25808/pdf)). The **Church of England** names "The Second Book of Maccabees" among the books that "the Church doth read for example of life and instruction of manners; but yet doth it not apply them to establish any doctrine" ([Church of England, Articles of Religion, VI](https://www.churchofengland.org/liturgy/book-of-common-prayer/articles-religion#VI)). **Luther**'s Bible printed 1 and 2 Maccabees among the Apocrypha, books "useful and good to read" ([Rösel, Luther und die Apokryphen](https://www.die-bibel.de/luther-und-die-apokryphen)). The **Reformed** Westminster Confession holds that the books called Apocrypha "are no part of the canon of the Scripture" and are not to be used otherwise "than other human writings" ([Westminster Confession of Faith, 1.3](https://www.opc.org/wcf.html)).

## How the churches read it

Where the churches, or their interpreters, read the book differently, or where it has played a part in their history. Each entry starts with its source's score.

- `7` [Catechism of the Catholic Church, 1030–1032: The Final Purification, or Purgatory](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P2N.HTM): **the Catholic Church and prayer for the dead.** The Catechism teaches that the doctrine of purgatory "is also based on the practice of prayer for the dead, already mentioned in Sacred Scripture", and quotes the end of Judas's story, which it numbers 12:46: Judas "made atonement for the dead, that they might be delivered from their sin" ([2 Maccabees 12:45](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2MA12.htm#V45)). Under the communion of saints it adds that "'because it is a holy and a wholesome thought to pray for the dead that they may be loosed from their sins' she offers her suffrages for them" ([Catechism of the Catholic Church, 946–962](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P2B.HTM), 958). The Council of Trent taught "that there is a Purgatory, and that the souls there detained are helped by the suffrages of the faithful", "but principally by the acceptable sacrifice of the altar" ([Council of Trent, Session XXV: Decree concerning Purgatory](https://history.hanover.edu/texts/trent/ct25.html)), and [2 Maccabees 12:43–45](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2MA12.htm#V43) is the first of the Old Testament readings for Catholic Masses for the Dead ([USCCB, Masses for the Dead: Readings from the Old Testament](https://www.usccb.org/prayer-and-worship/sacraments-and-sacramentals/bereavement-and-funerals/old-testament-readings)).
- `7` [The Longer Catechism of the Orthodox, Catholic, Eastern Church, Part One](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/schaff/creeds2.vi.iii.ii.html) (Schaff, Creeds of Christendom): **the Orthodox Church and prayer for the departed.** The catechism, answers 376–377, teaches that souls who "departed with faith, but without having had time to bring forth fruits worthy of repentance" may be "aided towards the attainment of a blessed resurrection by prayers offered in their behalf", and grounds this "On the constant tradition of the Catholic Church; the sources of which may be seen even in the Church of the Old Testament. Judas Maccabæus offered sacrifice for his men that had fallen." The Orthodox Church in America describes the memorial services, or *Panikhida*, held on the third, ninth and fortieth days after a death, on anniversaries and on "Memorial Saturdays" ([Orthodox Church in America, Questions and Answers: Prayers for the Departed](https://www.oca.org/questions/deathfunerals/prayers-for-the-departed)), and states that "the Orthodox Church does not believe in purgatory as a 'third' or 'alternate' state to heaven and hell" ([Orthodox Church in America, Questions and Answers: Indulgences](https://www.oca.org/questions/romancatholicism/indulgences)).
- `7` [Orthodox Church in America, 7 Holy Maccabee Martyrs](https://www.oca.org/saints/lives/2026/08/01/102162-7-holy-maccabee-martyrs): **the martyrs as saints.** The **Orthodox** Church commemorates the seven brothers, "their mother Solomonia and their teacher Eleazar" on 1 August, and notes that "Various Fathers of the Church preached sermons on the seven Maccabees, including Saint Cyprian of Carthage, Saint Ambrose of Milan, Saint Gregory Nazianzus and Saint John Chrysostom." Cyprian held up the brothers to Christians facing persecution, "Seven brethren were thus associating in martyrdom", and read their mother as a figure of the Church ([Cyprian of Carthage, Treatise 11: Exhortation to Martyrdom, Addressed to Fortunatus](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/050711.htm), 11). The New American Bible notes that these stories, "among the earliest models of 'martyrology,' were understandably popular among the Christians of the early centuries" ([Vatican, New American Bible, 2 Maccabees, chapter 6, with notes](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0839/__PEA.HTM)), and that [Hebrews 11:32–38](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/HEB11.htm#V32), with its "Others were tortured, not accepting their deliverance, that they might obtain a better resurrection", draws on "the history of the judges, the prophets, and the Maccabean martyrs" ([Vatican, New American Bible, Hebrews, chapter 11, with notes](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0839/__P11Q.HTM); see the [Hebrews](../new-testament/hebrews.md) page).
- `7` [Catechism of the Catholic Church, 279–324](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P19.HTM): **creation out of nothing and the resurrection.** The **Catholic** Catechism quotes the mother's words to her sons ([2 Maccabees 7:22–23](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2MA07.htm#V22), [2 Maccabees 7:28](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2MA07.htm#V28)) as Scripture's witness "to faith in creation 'out of nothing' as a truth full of promise and hope" (297). The Catholic lectionary reads [2 Maccabees 7:1–2](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2MA07.htm#V1) and [2 Maccabees 7:9–14](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2MA07.htm#V9) on the Thirty-second Sunday in Ordinary Time in Year C, with the Sadducees' question to Jesus about the resurrection ([USCCB, Readings for the Thirty-second Sunday in Ordinary Time (6 November 2022)](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/readings/110622.cfm); [Luke 20:27–38](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/LUK20.htm#V27)).
- `7` [Catechism of the Catholic Church, 946–962: The communion of saints](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P2B.HTM): **do the saints in heaven pray for us?** Judas's dream shows Jeremiah as "he who prays much for the people and the holy city" ([2 Maccabees 15:14](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2MA15.htm#V14)). The New American Bible sees here "a clear belief in the intercession of the saints" ([Vatican, New American Bible, 2 Maccabees, chapter 15, with notes](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0839/__PEJ.HTM)), and the **Catholic** Catechism teaches that the saints "do not cease to intercede with the Father for us" (956). The **Lutheran** Apology of the Augsburg Confession grants that the saints in heaven "pray for the Church in general, albeit no testimony concerning the praying of the dead is extant in the Scriptures, except the dream taken from the Second Book of Maccabees, 15:14", but holds that "it does not follow that they are to be invoked" ([Apology of the Augsburg Confession, Article XXI: Of the Invocation of Saints](https://bookofconcord.org/defense/of-the-invocation-of-saints/)). Article XXII of the **Church of England** counts the "invocation of Saints" among things "grounded upon no warranty of Scripture" ([Church of England, Articles of Religion, XXII](https://www.churchofengland.org/liturgy/book-of-common-prayer/articles-religion#XXII)).
- `7` [John Calvin, Institutes of the Christian Religion, Book III, Chapter 5](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/calvin/institutes.v.vi.html) (Christian Classics Ethereal Library): **the book's authority in the Reformation.** Calvin, the **Reformed** reformer, declined to answer the argument for purgatory from Judas's offering, "lest I should seem to include that work among the canonical books", and took the epilogue's apology ([2 Maccabees 15:38](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2MA15.htm#V38)) as a sign of its rank: "He who confesses that his writings stand in need of pardon, certainly proclaims that they are not oracles of the Holy Spirit." He also quoted Augustine's view that the book "has been received by the Church not uselessly, if it be read or heard with soberness" (section 8). **Luther** is recorded in his *Table Talk* as saying: "I am so great an enemy to the second book of the Maccabees, and to Esther, that I wish they had not come to us at all, for they have too many heathen unnaturalities" ([Martin Luther, Table Talk: Of God's Word](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/luther/tabletalk.v.i.html)); his Bible nonetheless printed the book among the Apocrypha ([Rösel, Luther und die Apokryphen](https://www.die-bibel.de/luther-und-die-apokryphen)).

## Open questions

Questions on which the churches or scholars disagree, for example about the book's authorship or the meaning of a passage. These guidelines don't pick a side.

- `7` [Vatican, New American Bible, 2 Maccabees, chapter 12, with notes](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0839/__PEG.HTM): **does Judas's offering support prayer for the dead, and purgatory?** The New American Bible calls [2 Maccabees 12:42–45](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2MA12.htm#V42) "the earliest statement of the doctrine that prayers… and sacrifices… for the dead are efficacious", made "only for the purpose of proving that Judas believed in the resurrection of the just", and judges that "His belief was similar to, but not quite the same as, the Catholic doctrine of purgatory." The **Catholic** Church cites the passage for prayer for the dead and purgatory, and the **Orthodox** catechism for prayer for the departed without the Latin doctrine of purgatory (see above). The **Lutheran** Apology answers that "we know that the ancients speak of prayer for the dead, which we do not prohibit; but we disapprove of the application ex opere operato of the Lord's Supper on behalf of the dead" ([Apology of the Augsburg Confession, Article XXIV: Of the Mass](https://bookofconcord.org/defense/of-the-mass/)). Article XXII of the **Church of England** calls "The Romish Doctrine concerning Purgatory" "a fond thing vainly invented" ([Church of England, Articles of Religion, XXII](https://www.churchofengland.org/liturgy/book-of-common-prayer/articles-religion#XXII)), and Article VI does not apply this book "to establish any doctrine". The **Reformed** Westminster Confession teaches that prayer is to be made for the living, "but not for the dead" ([Westminster Confession of Faith, 21.4](https://www.opc.org/wcf.html)), and Calvin read the passage as praising only Judas's "firm hope of the final resurrection", adding that "The act, indeed, was not free from superstition and misguided zeal" ([John Calvin, Institutes of the Christian Religion, Book III, Chapter 5](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/calvin/institutes.v.vi.html), section 8). The wider questions are set out in [Prayer](../../general/prayer.md#open-questions) and [Repentance and forgiveness](../../general/repentance.md#open-questions).
- `7` [Vatican, New American Bible, 2 Maccabees, chapter 7, with notes](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0839/__PEB.HTM): **what does chapter 7 teach about the resurrection, and for whom?** The New American Bible finds that in the brothers' words "belief in the future resurrection of the body, at least for the just, is clearly stated", and compares [Daniel 12:2](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/DAN12.htm#V2), where "some to everlasting life, and some to shame and everlasting contempt" (see the [Daniel](../old-testament/daniel.md) page). The fourth brother tells the king, "For as for you, you will have no resurrection to life" ([2 Maccabees 7:14](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2MA07.htm#V14)). The USCCB introduction contrasts 1 Maccabees, which has "no doctrine of individual immortality except in the survival of one's name and fame" ([USCCB, Introduction to the First Book of Maccabees](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/1maccabees/0)). Naomi Janowitz reads the martyrs' deaths also as atonement for the people's sins: "humans replace animals as a means to atonement" ([Bible Odyssey, Naomi Janowitz, Martyrdom in Maccabees](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/martyrdom-in-maccabees/); [2 Maccabees 7:36–38](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2MA07.htm#V36)).
- `7` [NETS, 2 Makkabees, translated by Joachim Schaper](https://ccat.sas.upenn.edu/nets/edition/21-2makk-nets.pdf) (International Organization for Septuagint and Cognate Studies): **how far is the book history, and how was it put together?** Schaper holds that the main body "goes back to Jason of Cyrene", that some passages are the epitomator's own, and that "The whole of chapter 7, 12.43–45 and 14.37–46 also seem alien in the context of Jason's history". The New American Bible's introduction judges the book "not without genuine historical value in supplementing I Maccabees", but notes that the author sometimes transfers "events from their proper chronological order", gives "exaggerated figures", and "inclines to elaborate descriptions of celestial apparitions" ([Vatican, New American Bible, 2 Maccabees: Introduction](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0839/__PE4.HTM)). The 1910 Catholic Encyclopedia reported that the book "is much less thought of as a historical document by non-Catholic scholars than the First", granted that the story of the sacred fire in the second letter "is a pure legend", and defended the history of the book itself ([New Advent, Catholic Encyclopedia (1910), The Books of Machabees](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/09495a.htm)).
- `7` [Thomas Aquinas, Summa Theologiae, II-II, Question 64, Article 5: Whether it is lawful to kill oneself](https://www.newadvent.org/summa/3064.htm#article5) (New Advent): **how should the death of Razis be judged?** The book presents Razis as "choosing rather to die nobly than to fall into the hands of the wicked wretches" ([2 Maccabees 14:42](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2MA14.htm#V42)), and the New American Bible notes that his story "belongs to the 'martyrology' class of literature" ([Vatican, New American Bible, 2 Maccabees, chapter 14, with notes](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0839/__PEI.HTM)). Thomas Aquinas answered the argument that Razis's act was lawful by holding that those who, "among whom was Razias, have killed themselves thinking to act from fortitude", show "not true fortitude, but rather a weakness of soul unable to bear penal evils". For what the churches teach about suicide and care for those at risk today, see `HOPE-6`.
