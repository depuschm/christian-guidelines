# 3 Maccabees

3 Maccabees tells how the Jews of Egypt were saved from a king who meant to destroy them. The HarperCollins Bible Dictionary describes it as "A book that, despite its name, recounts the persecution of the Jews in the era prior to the Maccabees. It concerns attempts by Ptolemy IV Philopator (221–203 BCE) to persecute Jews throughout his realm" ([Bible Odyssey, Maccabees, The Third Book of the (HarperCollins Bible Dictionary)](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/dictionary/maccabees-the-third-book-of-the/)). After his victory over Antiochus at Raphia, the king visits Jerusalem and insists on entering the sanctuary, where only the high priest may go; at the high priest Simon's prayer he is struck down ([3 Maccabees 1:1–7](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/3MA01.htm#V1), [3 Maccabees 1:8–29](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/3MA01.htm#V8), [3 Maccabees 2:1–24](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/3MA02.htm#V1)). Back in Egypt he orders the Jews to be registered as slaves, has them brought in chains to the hippodrome of Alexandria, and three times prepares to have them trampled by elephants drugged with wine and frankincense. At the old priest Eleazar's prayer, "two angels, dreadful of form, came down", the elephants turn on the king's own forces, and the king releases the Jews and gives them a festival of deliverance ([3 Maccabees 5:2](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/3MA05.htm#V2), [3 Maccabees 6:1–15](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/3MA06.htm#V1), [3 Maccabees 6:16–29](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/3MA06.htm#V16), [3 Maccabees 6:30–41](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/3MA06.htm#V30)). NETS points to the "strong parallel drawn by the author between these two moments of national crisis and deliverance", Simon's prayer at the temple and Eleazar's at the hippodrome, as "fundamental to the dramatic structure of 3 Makkabees" ([NETS, 3 Makkabees, translated by Cameron Boyd-Taylor](https://ccat.sas.upenn.edu/nets/edition/22-3makk-nets.pdf)). The NRSVUE's cross-references link the king at the temple to Heliodorus in [2 Maccabees](2-maccabees.md) and the decree and the feast to [Esther](../old-testament/esther.md) ([NRSVUE, 3 Maccabees 1–7](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=3%20Maccabees%201-7&version=NRSVUE); [2 Maccabees 3:1–40](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2MA03.htm#V1), [Esther 3:8–9](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/EST03.htm#V8), [Esther 9:17–22](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/EST09.htm#V17)).

NETS describes the book as "an original composition", "composed in Greek by a Jewish author steeped in his native tradition, yet one who had attained a high level of Greek literary education", writing in a classicizing style that "came into vogue in the late Hellenistic period". The Jewish Encyclopedia holds that the author "was certainly an Alexandrian Jew who wrote in Greek", notes that "The work begins abruptly and is thought to be but a fragment of a once larger whole", and dates it between the end of the first century BC and the first century AD ([Jewish Encyclopedia, Maccabees, Books of](https://www.jewishencyclopedia.com/articles/10237-maccabees-books-of)). The Catholic Encyclopedia reasons that "Since the book shows acquaintance with the Greek additions to Daniel, it cannot be earlier than the first century B.C." ([Catholic Encyclopedia (1907), Apocrypha: Legendary apocrypha of Jewish origin](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/01601a.htm#I2)). NETS notes that "Of the great uncials, it occurs only in Alexandrinus", that "The book is extant in a Syriac and Armenian version, though it does not appear in the Vulgate", and that "There are no references to 3 Makkabees in the extant Jewish sources, and what few quotations of the book that come down to us from antiquity are to be found almost exclusively in Christian writers of the East."

3 Maccabees is not part of the Jewish Bible, and the churches disagree about its place in theirs ([which books each church reads](../README.md#which-books-each-church-reads)). The **Orthodox** churches read it among the *anaginoskómena*, the "books to be read" ([Munteanu, Canoniques, non-canoniques ou bons à lire ?](https://www.diacronia.ro/ro/indexing/details/A25808/pdf)). The WEBBE's own heading notes that it "is recognised as Deuterocanonical Scripture by the Greek Orthodox and Russian Orthodox Churches. It is considered to be apocrypha by most other church traditions" ([3 Maccabees 1](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/3MA01.htm)), and the HarperCollins Bible Dictionary explains that the NRSV prints it "because it is considered canonical by Eastern Orthodox churches". The **Catholic Church** counts "1 and 2 Maccabees" among the 46 books of its Old Testament ([Catechism of the Catholic Church, 120](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__PR.HTM)), as the Council of Trent listed "two books of the Machabees, the first and the second" in 1546 ([Council of Trent, Session IV](https://history.hanover.edu/texts/trent/ct04.html)). The **Church of England** names only "The First Book of Maccabees" and "The Second Book of Maccabees" among the books that "the Church doth read for example of life and instruction of manners; but yet doth it not apply them to establish any doctrine" ([Church of England, Articles of Religion, VI](https://www.churchofengland.org/liturgy/book-of-common-prayer/articles-religion#VI)). **Luther** didn't take it into his Bible, though the Zurich Bible did ([Rösel, Luther und die Apokryphen](https://www.die-bibel.de/luther-und-die-apokryphen)). The **Reformed** Westminster Confession holds that the books called Apocrypha "are no part of the canon of the Scripture" and are not to be used otherwise "than other human writings" ([Westminster Confession of Faith, 1.3](https://www.opc.org/wcf.html)).

## How the churches read it

Where the churches, or their interpreters, read the book differently, or where it has played a part in their history. Each entry starts with its source's score.

- `7` [New Advent, The Apostolic Canons](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/3820.htm): **three books of Maccabees.** Canon 85 of the Apostolic Canons lists the books to be "counted venerable and sacred by all of you, both clergy and laity", and among those of the Old Testament counts "of the Maccabees, three". The Catholic Encyclopedia judges that "The Syrian Church was the first to give it a friendly reception, presumably on the strength of its mention in the Apostolic Constitutions. Later, Third Machabees was admitted into the canon of the Greek Church, but seems never to have been known to the Latins" ([Catholic Encyclopedia (1907), Apocrypha: Legendary apocrypha of Jewish origin](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/01601a.htm#I2)).
- `7` [Munteanu, Canoniques, non-canoniques ou bons à lire ?](https://www.diacronia.ro/ro/indexing/details/A25808/pdf) (in French): **in the Orthodox Bible.** Munteanu reports that the early ninth-century Patriarch Nicephorus of Constantinople listed 1, 2 and 3 Maccabees among the Old Testament books whose place was disputed, and that 3 Maccabees was first translated into Slavonic for the Ostrog Bible of 1581. Today's Russian and Bulgarian Bibles end their Old Testament with 1–3 Maccabees and 3 Esdras; the Greek Bible of 1928 places 1–3 Maccabees among the canonical books rather than apart from them; and the first Romanian Bible, of 1688, printed 1–4 Maccabees with the other such books at the end of the Old Testament, as Protestant Bibles did.
- `7` [Rösel, Luther und die Apokryphen](https://www.die-bibel.de/luther-und-die-apokryphen) (Deutsche Bibelgesellschaft, in German): **outside the Luther Bible, inside the Zurich Bible.** Martin Rösel writes that Luther took 1 and 2 Maccabees into his Bible, but that in Zurich the translators also included 3 and 4 Esdras and 3 Maccabees (*in Zürich nahm man auch das 3.+4. Esrabuch und das 3. Makkabäerbuch auf*). He adds that this decision also influenced the Lutherans, so that already in the sixteenth century there were enlarged Luther Bibles and separate editions of these three books.
- `7` [Church of England, Articles of Religion, VI](https://www.churchofengland.org/liturgy/book-of-common-prayer/articles-religion#VI): **not among the books read in the Church of England.** The **Church of England**'s list of the books it reads "for example of life and instruction of manners" ends with "The First Book of Maccabees" and "The Second Book of Maccabees", and doesn't name a third.
- `6` [Catholic Encyclopedia (1910), The Books of Machabees](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/09495a.htm) (New Advent): **no claim to the Catholic canon.** For the Catholic Encyclopedia, 3 Maccabees "is the story of a persecution of the Jews in Egypt under Ptolemy IV Philopator (222-205 B. C.), and therefore has no right to its title", and of 3 and 4 Maccabees "Neither book has any claim to canonicity, though the first for a while received favourable consideration in some Churches." NETS confirms that the book "does not appear in the Vulgate", the Latin Bible of the West ([NETS, 3 Makkabees](https://ccat.sas.upenn.edu/nets/edition/22-3makk-nets.pdf)).

## Open questions

Questions on which the churches or scholars disagree, for example about the book's authorship or the meaning of a passage. These guidelines don't pick a side.

- `7` [Munteanu, Canoniques, non-canoniques ou bons à lire ?](https://www.diacronia.ro/ro/indexing/details/A25808/pdf) (in French): **does it belong in the Bible?** The churches' answers differ, as set out above and in the table of [which books each church reads](../README.md#which-books-each-church-reads): the Orthodox read it among the *anaginoskómena*, though Munteanu notes that Patriarch Nicephorus counted it among the disputed books, and the Apostolic Canons count "of the Maccabees, three" ([New Advent, The Apostolic Canons](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/3820.htm)); the Catholic canon has "1 and 2 Maccabees" ([Catechism of the Catholic Church, 120](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__PR.HTM)); the Church of England's Article VI doesn't name it ([Church of England, Articles of Religion, VI](https://www.churchofengland.org/liturgy/book-of-common-prayer/articles-religion#VI)); and the Westminster Confession counts the Apocrypha "no part of the canon" ([Westminster Confession of Faith, 1.3](https://www.opc.org/wcf.html)).
- `6` [Jewish Encyclopedia, Maccabees, Books of](https://www.jewishencyclopedia.com/articles/10237-maccabees-books-of) (1906): **history or story?** The encyclopedia calls the book "this fiction" and doubts even its opening: "Whether there is any foundation for the story concerning Philopator with which the writer begins there is no means of knowing. If true, it is one of a very few grains of fact in the whole account." It points to Josephus, who tells a similar story of a later king, Ptolemy Physcon, and concludes that the author "has transferred it to an earlier Ptolemy and given it an entirely unhistorical setting". In Josephus, the king "exposed them naked, and in bonds, to his elephants", but "these elephants left the Jews, who were exposed to them; and fell violently upon Physco’s friends", and "the Alexandrian Jews do, with good reason, celebrate this day" ([Josephus, Against Apion, Book II, 5, translated by William Whiston](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/josephus/apion-2.html#S5)). A second article in the same entry, "treating the subject from a Jewish standpoint", suggests instead that "the book refers to a persecution in the Fayum". The Catholic Encyclopedia of 1907 judges that "It rests on no ascertainable historical fact", calling it "a very weak piece of fiction written in Greek by an Alexandrian Jew, and probably designed to encourage its countrymen in the midst of persecutions" ([Catholic Encyclopedia (1907), Apocrypha: Legendary apocrypha of Jewish origin](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/01601a.htm#I2)), while its 1910 article on the Maccabees judges: "Though the work contains much that is historical, the story is a fiction." ([Catholic Encyclopedia (1910), The Books of Machabees](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/09495a.htm)).
- `6` [Bible Odyssey, Maccabees, The Third Book of the (HarperCollins Bible Dictionary)](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/dictionary/maccabees-the-third-book-of-the/): **why is it called "Maccabees"?** The dictionary notes that the book, "despite its name, recounts the persecution of the Jews in the era prior to the Maccabees". The Jewish Encyclopedia agrees that it "has in reality nothing to do either with the Maccabees or with their times", and suggests that "It received its name probably because it is a fiction concerning the persecution of the Jews by a foreign king"; it also holds that the book "was written later than II Maccabees, for its author made use of that book" ([Jewish Encyclopedia, Maccabees, Books of](https://www.jewishencyclopedia.com/articles/10237-maccabees-books-of)). The Catholic Encyclopedia concludes that it "has no right to its title" ([Catholic Encyclopedia (1910), The Books of Machabees](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/09495a.htm)).
- `6` [Jewish Encyclopedia, Maccabees, Books of](https://www.jewishencyclopedia.com/articles/10237-maccabees-books-of) (1906): **when was it written, and against what threat?** The encyclopedia dates the book after 2 Maccabees and before the end of the first century AD, and reports that "Ewald regarded this work as a polemic against Caligula" and dated it around AD 40, a view it says later writers abandoned; its second article reports that Willrich "supports the view that the book is best explained as referring to Caligula". The Catholic Encyclopedia argues that "Since the book shows acquaintance with the Greek additions to Daniel, it cannot be earlier than the first century B.C., and could scarcely have found such favour among Christians if composed later than the first century after Christ" ([Catholic Encyclopedia (1907), Apocrypha: Legendary apocrypha of Jewish origin](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/01601a.htm#I2)). NETS places its style in the "late Hellenistic period" ([NETS, 3 Makkabees](https://ccat.sas.upenn.edu/nets/edition/22-3makk-nets.pdf)).
