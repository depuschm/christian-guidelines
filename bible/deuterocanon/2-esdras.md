# 2 Esdras

2 Esdras is an apocalypse, a book of visions in which an angel reveals what is hidden, written in the name of Ezra the scribe ([Ezra 7:6](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/EZR07.htm#V6)). The WEBBE introduces it as a book that "is included in the Slavonic Bible as 3 Esdras, but is not found in the Greek Septuagint. It is included in the Appendix to the Latin Vulgate Bible as 4 Esdras. It is considered to be Apocrypha by most church traditions" ([2 Esdras](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2ES.htm)). The NRSVUE describes it as "Comprising what is sometimes called 5 Ezra (chapters 1–2), 4 Ezra (chapters 3–14), and 6 Ezra (chapters 15–16)" ([NRSVUE, 2 Esdras 1](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=2%20Esdras%201&version=NRSVUE)). The heart of the book, chapters 3–14, opens: "In the thirtieth year after the ruin of the city, I Salathiel, also called Esdras, was in Babylon, and lay troubled upon my bed" ([2 Esdras 3:1](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2ES03.htm#V1)). In seven visions, Ezra argues with the angel Uriel about God's justice: the Jewish Encyclopedia sums up the first as "Esdras asks God how the misery of Israel can be in keeping with divine justice. The answer is given by Uriel: God's ways are unsearchable and the human mind can not grasp them" ([Jewish Encyclopedia, Esdras, Books of](https://www.jewishencyclopedia.com/articles/5852-esdras-books-of)). Scholars count "4 Ezra" among the ancient apocalypses, with Daniel and 1 Enoch ([Bible Odyssey, Jacqueline Hidalgo, What Is an Apocalypse?](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/what-is-an-apocalypse/)). The Catholic Encyclopedia says that it "is reckoned among the most beautiful productions of Jewish literature" ([Catholic Encyclopedia, Esdras](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/05535a.htm)).

Scholars generally date the Jewish apocalypse to the end of the first century AD, after the Romans destroyed Jerusalem in AD 70. The Jewish Encyclopedia reads the eagle of chapters 11–12 as the Roman empire and concludes that "the book must date from the last quarter of the first century—probably between 90 and 96" ([Jewish Encyclopedia, Esdras, Books of](https://www.jewishencyclopedia.com/articles/5852-esdras-books-of)); the HarperCollins Bible Dictionary calls it "A Jewish apocalypse dating from the very end of the first century CE" ([Bible Odyssey, Esdras, The Second Book of (HarperCollins Bible Dictionary)](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/dictionary/esdras-the-second-book-of/)), and the Catholic Encyclopedia notes that scholars "seem to rally more and more around the year A.D. 97" ([Catholic Encyclopedia, Esdras](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/05535a.htm)). The Jewish Encyclopedia holds that "The original was written in Hebrew, and then translated into Greek", but "neither the Hebrew nor the Greek text is extant"; the book survives in Latin, Syriac, Ethiopic, Arabic, Armenian and Georgian. Chapters 1–2 and 15–16 are later additions. The Jewish Encyclopedia judges chapters 1–2 "of Christian origin (probably second century)", describing "the rejection of the Jews in favor of the Christians", and chapters 15–16 possibly Jewish, from the third century ([Jewish Encyclopedia, Esdras, Books of](https://www.jewishencyclopedia.com/articles/5852-esdras-books-of)); the Catholic Encyclopedia assigns both to the third century, as chapters "containing evident traces of Christianity" ([Catholic Encyclopedia, Esdras](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/05535a.htm)). The book's names are confusing (see the [Ezra](../old-testament/ezra.md) page): it is "IV Esdras" in the Latin Bible and most modern scholarship, "II Esdras" in the English Bible, "I Esdras" in the Ethiopic Bible and 3 Esdras in Slavonic Bibles ([Jewish Encyclopedia, Esdras, Books of](https://www.jewishencyclopedia.com/articles/5852-esdras-books-of); [NRSVUE, 2 Esdras 1](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=2%20Esdras%201&version=NRSVUE)).

The book is not part of the Jewish Bible or of the Greek Septuagint, and the churches disagree about it ([which books each church reads](../README.md#which-books-each-church-reads)). Among the **Orthodox** churches it is found only in the Slavonic Bibles, translated from the Latin Vulgate ([Munteanu, Canoniques, non-canoniques ou bons à lire ?](https://www.diacronia.ro/ro/indexing/details/A25808/pdf)). The **Catholic Church** doesn't count it among the 46 books of its Old Testament ([Catechism of the Catholic Church, 120](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__PR.HTM)), and the Council of Trent's list of 1546 doesn't name it ([Council of Trent, Session IV](https://history.hanover.edu/texts/trent/ct04.html)); the Latin text "is usually printed in the appendix to the editions of the Vulgate" ([Catholic Encyclopedia, Esdras](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/05535a.htm)). The **Church of England** names "The Fourth Book of Esdras" among the books that "the Church doth read for example of life and instruction of manners; but yet doth it not apply them to establish any doctrine" ([Church of England, Articles of Religion, VI](https://www.churchofengland.org/liturgy/book-of-common-prayer/articles-religion#VI)). **Luther** left it out of his Bible ([Rösel, Luther und die Apokryphen](https://www.die-bibel.de/luther-und-die-apokryphen)). The **Reformed** Westminster Confession holds that the books called Apocrypha "are no part of the canon of the Scripture" ([Westminster Confession of Faith, 1.3](https://www.opc.org/wcf.html)). The **Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church** lists "Ezra (2nd) and Ezra Sutuel" among the books of its Old Testament ([Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church, The Canonical Books](https://www.ethiopianorthodox.org/english/canonical/books.html)).

## How the churches read it

Where the churches, or their interpreters, read the book differently, or where it has played a part in their history. Each entry starts with its source's score.

- `7` [Munteanu, Canoniques, non-canoniques ou bons à lire ?](https://www.diacronia.ro/ro/indexing/details/A25808/pdf) (in French): **in the Slavonic Bible.** Munteanu writes that, besides the books of the Septuagint, the Slavonic editions alone add 4 Esdras, translated from the Vulgate, which Slavonic Bibles call 3 Esdras. It entered the first complete Slavonic Bible, made at Novgorod in 1499, when a Dominican priest translated from the Vulgate the books then missing from the Slavonic manuscripts; the first printed Slavonic Bible, published at Ostrog in 1581, kept it; and the **Russian** Synod's Bible of 1876 again translated it from the Vulgate. Munteanu notes that Russian and Bulgarian Bibles still place it at the end of the Old Testament, after 3 Maccabees, while the Serbian Bible has 4 Maccabees in its place. The **Greek** Orthodox Bible, which follows the Septuagint, doesn't include it: the NRSVUE notes that it is "not found in the Greek" ([NRSVUE, 2 Esdras 1](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=2%20Esdras%201&version=NRSVUE)).
- `7` [Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church, The Canonical Books](https://www.ethiopianorthodox.org/english/canonical/books.html): **in the Ethiopian canon.** The **Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church** counts "Ezra (2nd) and Ezra Sutuel" among the books of its Old Testament, and says that its Bible, translated from the Septuagint, "includes the book of Enoch, Baruch, and the third and fourth Esdras". The Jewish Encyclopedia notes that the apocalypse "is called 'I Esdras' in the Ethiopic" ([Jewish Encyclopedia, Esdras, Books of](https://www.jewishencyclopedia.com/articles/5852-esdras-books-of)).
- `7` [Jerome, Against Vigilantius](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/3010.htm) (New Advent): **a book "the Church does not receive".** In 406 Vigilantius, a priest who had returned to Gaul, attacked as superstitious the honour paid to relics and "the prayers offered to the dead", as the translators' introduction explains. Defending the saints' prayers for the living, Jerome answers him: "you bring before me an apocryphal book which, under the name of Esdras, is read by you and those of your feather, and in this book it is written that after death no one dares pray for others. I have never read the book: for what need is there to take up what the Church does not receive?" The passage he refers to says that on the day of judgement "no one will ever pray for another" ([2 Esdras 7:102–115](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2ES07.htm#V102)).
- `7` [Church of England, Articles of Religion, VI](https://www.churchofengland.org/liturgy/book-of-common-prayer/articles-religion#VI): **among the Apocrypha of the Reformation churches.** The **Church of England** names "The Third Book of Esdras" and "The Fourth Book of Esdras" first among the books it reads "for example of life and instruction of manners". The Catholic Encyclopedia explains that "Protestant writers, after the Geneva Bible, call… III and IV Esdras of the Vulgate respectively I and II Esdras" ([Catholic Encyclopedia, Esdras](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/05535a.htm)). Martin Rösel writes that **Luther** didn't take the third and fourth books of Ezra into his Bible (*das 3. und 4. Buch Esra wurden nicht aufgenommen*), but that the **Reformed** Bible of Zurich included them with 3 Maccabees, and that this led to enlarged Luther Bibles and separate editions of these three books as early as the 16th century ([Rösel, Luther und die Apokryphen](https://www.die-bibel.de/luther-und-die-apokryphen)).
- `6` [Catholic Encyclopedia, Requiem Masses](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/12776d.htm) (New Advent): **The Requiem Mass.** In the **Catholic** Church's Latin liturgy, Masses for the dead "derive their name from the first word of the Introit, which may be traced to the Fourth Book of Esdras, one of the Apocrypha", at 2:34–35, where the WEBBE reads "He will give you everlasting rest… the everlasting light will shine on you forevermore" ([2 Esdras 2:34–37](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2ES02.htm#V34)). The Catholic Encyclopedia adds that the second chapter also gave the liturgy "the introit Accipite jucunditatem for Whit-Tuesday (36-37)", and that the book, "frequently quoted by the Fathers (especially St. Ambrose), … may be said to have framed the popular belief of the Middle Ages concerning the last things" ([Catholic Encyclopedia, Esdras](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/05535a.htm)).

## Open questions

Questions on which the churches or scholars disagree, for example about the book's authorship or the meaning of a passage. These guidelines don't pick a side.

- `7` [NRSVUE, 2 Esdras 7](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=2%20Esdras%207&version=NRSVUE): **who is the Messiah who dies?** The WEBBE, following the Latin, reads "my son Jesus will be revealed… After these years my son Christ will die, along with all of those who have the breath of life" ([2 Esdras 7:28–29](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2ES07.htm#V28)). The NRSVUE reads "my son the Messiah shall be revealed" and "my son the Messiah shall die", following the Syriac and one Arabic version, and its note gives the others: the Ethiopic reads "my Messiah", another Arabic version "the Messiah", the Armenian "the Messiah of God", and the Latin "my son Jesus". The Catholic Encyclopedia summarises: "'My son' shall come in his glory… they shall reign 400 years, then 'my son' and all the living shall die" ([Catholic Encyclopedia, Esdras](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/05535a.htm)). The Jewish Encyclopedia, which treats chapters 3–14 as a Jewish work, identifies the Messiah in the visions of the lion and of the man from the sea ([Jewish Encyclopedia, Esdras, Books of](https://www.jewishencyclopedia.com/articles/5852-esdras-books-of); [2 Esdras 12:31–34](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2ES12.htm#V31), [2 Esdras 13:32](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2ES13.htm#V32)).
- `7` [Jerome, Against Vigilantius](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/3010.htm) (New Advent): **can anyone pray for others at the judgement?** The book has Ezra list those who prayed for others, Abraham for Sodom and Moses for the people, and Uriel answers that "This present world is not the end… Therefore those who were able prayed for the weak", but that on the day of judgement "no one will be able to have mercy on him who is condemned" ([2 Esdras 7:102–115](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2ES07.htm#V102), [Genesis 18:23–32](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/GEN18.htm#V23), [Exodus 32:11–14](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/EXO32.htm#V11)). The Jewish Encyclopedia reads the author as holding that "the end must be unrelenting justice and no mercy nor any intercession of saints" ([Jewish Encyclopedia, Esdras, Books of](https://www.jewishencyclopedia.com/articles/5852-esdras-books-of)). When Vigilantius cited the book against praying to the martyrs, Jerome refused to argue from a book "the Church does not receive". The churches' positions on prayer for the dead and the prayers of the saints are set out on the [2 Maccabees](2-maccabees.md#open-questions) page.
- `6` [Jewish Encyclopedia, Esdras, Books of](https://www.jewishencyclopedia.com/articles/5852-esdras-books-of) (1906): **how does the book answer the problem of evil?** The Jewish Encyclopedia writes that the author "struggles with the deepest religious problems: What is the origin of suffering and evil in the world?", that "Confidence in God's justice underlies all his thoughts", and that the angel's answer is that "God's ways are unsearchable" and "everything will be clear after the end of this world". The Catholic Encyclopedia sums up the first vision as Uriel chiding Ezra "for inquiring into things beyond his understanding" ([Catholic Encyclopedia, Esdras](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/05535a.htm)). The angel also tells Ezra that "Many have been created, but few will be saved" ([2 Esdras 8:1–3](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2ES08.htm#V1)). The churches' answers to why God allows suffering, and to whether everyone will be saved, are set out in the open questions of [Suffering and hope](../../general/suffering.md#open-questions).
- `6` [Bible Odyssey, Esdras, The Second Book of (HarperCollins Bible Dictionary)](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/dictionary/esdras-the-second-book-of/): **does it belong in the Bible?** The dictionary notes that "It is included among the Apocrypha by Protestants" and that "It is designated as 4 Esdras in the Vulgate". The WEBBE notes that "It is considered to be Apocrypha by most church traditions" ([2 Esdras](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2ES.htm)). The churches' own answers differ, as set out above and in the table of [which books each church reads](../README.md#which-books-each-church-reads): the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church counts it in its Old Testament ([Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church, The Canonical Books](https://www.ethiopianorthodox.org/english/canonical/books.html)); the Slavonic Bibles print it, but not the Greek ([Munteanu, Canoniques, non-canoniques ou bons à lire ?](https://www.diacronia.ro/ro/indexing/details/A25808/pdf)); the Catholic canon doesn't include it ([Catechism of the Catholic Church, 120](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__PR.HTM)); the Church of England reads it "for example of life" but not for doctrine ([Church of England, Articles of Religion, VI](https://www.churchofengland.org/liturgy/book-of-common-prayer/articles-religion#VI)); and the Westminster Confession counts the Apocrypha "no part of the canon" ([Westminster Confession of Faith, 1.3](https://www.opc.org/wcf.html)).
