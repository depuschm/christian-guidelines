# Psalm 151

Psalm 151 is a short psalm of seven verses in the voice of David, which the WEBBE heads "This Psalm is a genuine one of David, though extra" (with the footnote "or, supernumerary"), "composed when he fought in single combat with Goliath" ([Psalm 151](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/PS2.htm)). David tells how he was the youngest of his brothers and tended his father's sheep, how he made music, how God chose and anointed him rather than his tall and handsome brothers, and how he killed the Philistine and "removed reproach from the children of Israel" ([Psalm 151:1–7](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/PS201.htm#V1)). James A. Sanders explains that its "first five verses reflect chapter 16 of 1 Samuel, and the last two chapter 17" ([Bible Odyssey, James A. Sanders, Psalm 151](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/psalm-151/)), the stories of David's anointing and of his victory over Goliath ([1 Samuel 16:1–13](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1SA16.htm#V1), [1 Samuel 17:38–51](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1SA17.htm#V38); see the [1 Samuel](../old-testament/1-samuel.md) page).

Greek Bibles print it after the 150 psalms. Its Greek heading, in the NETS translation, reads "This Psalm is autographical. Regarding Dauid and outside the number" ([NETS, Psalms (and Prayer of Manasses), translated by Albert Pietersma](https://ccat.sas.upenn.edu/nets/edition/24-ps-nets.pdf)), and the NRSVUE renders it "This psalm is ascribed to David as his own composition (though it is outside the number)" ([NRSVUE, Psalm 151](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Psalm%20151%201&version=NRSVUE)). Sanders notes that the psalm "has been known for centuries in translation—in Greek, Old Latin, and Syriac (Christian) Bibles", and that "The Hebrew originals were discovered among the Dead Sea Scrolls": in the last column of the large Psalms Scroll from Cave 11 at Qumran, "Psalm 151A appears in its entirety and celebrates in poetry David's selection as king", and "The fragmentary Psalm 151B celebrated the young David's victory over Goliath". In his view, "The early Greek translation combined the two psalms into one" ([Bible Odyssey, James A. Sanders, Psalm 151](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/psalm-151/)). How the Hebrew and the Greek forms are related, and which is older, is one of the open questions below. The [Psalms](../old-testament/psalms.md) page describes the 150 psalms and the two ways of numbering them.

The psalm is not part of the Jewish Bible, and the churches disagree about its place in theirs ([which books each church reads](../README.md#which-books-each-church-reads)). The **Orthodox** churches read it at the end of the Psalter, among the *anaginoskómena*, the "books to be read" ([Munteanu, Canoniques, non-canoniques ou bons à lire ?](https://www.diacronia.ro/ro/indexing/details/A25808/pdf)), and the WEBBE's own heading notes that it "is recognised as Deuterocanonical Scripture by the Greek Orthodox and Russian Orthodox Churches" ([Psalm 151](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/PS2.htm)). The **Catholic Church** counts the Psalms among the 46 books of its Old Testament ([Catechism of the Catholic Church, 120](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__PR.HTM)), and the Council of Trent's list of 1546 names "the Davidical Psalter, consisting of a hundred and fifty psalms" ([Council of Trent, Session IV](https://history.hanover.edu/texts/trent/ct04.html)). The **Church of England**'s list of the books that "the Church doth read for example of life and instruction of manners" doesn't name it ([Church of England, Articles of Religion, VI](https://www.churchofengland.org/liturgy/book-of-common-prayer/articles-religion#VI)), nor does Martin Rösel's list of the books **Luther** took into his Bible as "useful and good to read" ([Rösel, Luther und die Apokryphen](https://www.die-bibel.de/luther-und-die-apokryphen)). The **Reformed** Westminster Confession holds that the books called Apocrypha "are no part of the canon of the Scripture" ([Westminster Confession of Faith, 1.3](https://www.opc.org/wcf.html)). Sanders sums up that the psalm "is not in Jewish, Roman Catholic, or Protestant Bibles, though it is in some Eastern Orthodox Bibles" ([Bible Odyssey, James A. Sanders, Psalm 151](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/psalm-151/)).

## How the churches read it

Where the churches, or their interpreters, read the psalm differently, or where it has played a part in their history. Each entry starts with its source's score.

- `7` [Munteanu, Canoniques, non-canoniques ou bons à lire ?](https://www.diacronia.ro/ro/indexing/details/A25808/pdf) (in French): **the last psalm of the Orthodox Psalter.** Ştefan Munteanu notes that in the Bible of the **Orthodox** churches the Psalter ends with Psalm 151, and that Athanasius of Alexandria, in his Letter to Marcellinus (sections 14 and 25), is the first to speak of it. In the Bibles he describes, the Greek Orthodox Bibles include it with the Psalms, as does the Orthodox Study Bible of 2008, and the Russian Bible of 2005 prints it after Psalm 150.
- `6` [Heritage of the Coptic Orthodox Church, Saturday of Light](https://copticheritage.org/17-saturday-of-light/): **a hymn of Joyous Saturday.** In the **Coptic Orthodox** Church, one of the [Oriental Orthodox](../../traditions/oriental-orthodox.md) churches, Joyous Saturday is "the night between Good Friday and the Sunday of Resurrection", and its rite "begins with the chanting of the hymn, Anok pe pikouji. This is Psalm 151". The page reads the psalm of Christ: it "tells us how God chose the young David from amongst his older brothers to defeat Goliath, thus saving the Israelites. Similarly, Christ came to deliver us from Satan, the spiritual Goliath."
- `6` [Catholic Encyclopedia, Apocrypha: Apocryphal psalms and prayers](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/01601a.htm#I3) (New Advent, 1907): **"outside the number".** The **Catholic** encyclopedia lists the psalm among the apocrypha: "The manuscripts of the Septuagint contain at the end of the canonical Psalter a short psalm (cli), which, however, is 'outside the number', i.e. of the Psalms", and "It is based on various passages in the Old Testament". Written before the Qumran scroll was found, it adds that "there is no evidence that it was ever written in Hebrew"; see the first open question below.
- `5` [Orthodox Christianity Then and Now, On the Interpretation of the Psalms (A Letter of St. Athanasius of Alexandria to Marcellinus)](https://www.johnsanidopoulos.com/2020/01/on-interpretation-of-psalms-letter-of.html) (John Sanidopoulos's blog, which reposts an English translation of the letter without naming the translator): **a psalm for those chosen to lead.** In his letter on how to pray each psalm, Athanasius advises: "If, weak as you are, you yet are chosen for some position of authority among the brethren", then "glorify the Lord Who chose you and sing Psalm 151, which is especially the Psalm of David." Munteanu cites the same passages of the letter as the first witness to the psalm ([Munteanu, Canoniques, non-canoniques ou bons à lire ?](https://www.diacronia.ro/ro/indexing/details/A25808/pdf)).

## Open questions

Questions on which the churches or scholars disagree, for example about the book's authorship or the meaning of a passage. These guidelines don't pick a side.

- `7` [Orion Center for the Study of the Dead Sea Scrolls, Menahem Haran, The Two Text-Forms of Psalm 151 (Tarbiz, 1985)](https://orion-bibliography.huji.ac.il/node/70568) (Hebrew University of Jerusalem, abstract in English): **which came first, the Hebrew psalms of the Qumran scroll or the Greek psalm?** James A. Sanders, who edited the scroll, holds that the Hebrew Psalms 151A and 151B are the originals and that the Greek "was not a simple combination of the two psalms we now see in the scroll but an edited version", which "omits six phrases from the original Hebrew". In his reading the omitted lines, in which "The mountains do not witness to him, nor do the hills proclaim", "depict David as being as gifted and talented a musician as the Greek god, Orpheus", and were left out because they might have offended Jewish communities "where Orpheus was still worshipped" ([Bible Odyssey, James A. Sanders, Psalm 151](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/psalm-151/)). Menahem Haran argues the opposite: that "the Qumran version is expanded and derivative, whereas the Hebrew text underlying LXX reflects the original scope of this psalm", that the scroll's longer version shows "late language" and "forced and artificial features", and that the scroll's last lines "do not constitute a separate psalm (Ps. 151B)" but "are the second part of one psalm, as in LXX". The NRSVUE's notes show where the Qumran manuscript differs from the Greek, which it translates: "prophet" for "messenger" in verse 4, and "Philistine" for "foreigner" in verse 6 ([NRSVUE, Psalm 151](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Psalm%20151%201&version=NRSVUE)). Writing before the scroll was found, the Catholic Encyclopedia judged that "there is no evidence that it was ever written in Hebrew" ([Catholic Encyclopedia, Apocrypha: Apocryphal psalms and prayers](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/01601a.htm#I3)).
- `6` [Bible Odyssey, Psalm 151 (HarperCollins Bible Dictionary)](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/dictionary/psalm-151/): **why does the psalm stand "outside the number", and is it Scripture?** The dictionary sums up: "In modern Bibles, it is sometimes included as an item in the Apocrypha; it is accepted as canonical scripture by Eastern Orthodox churches." Sanders suggests that the psalm "comes last in the various Psalters in which it appears", and that "Perhaps it gained its place at the climax of the Psalter because of the growing belief at the time that David was responsible for the entire Psalter" ([Bible Odyssey, James A. Sanders, Psalm 151](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/psalm-151/)). Haran reads "Its uncertain position at the borderline of the canon" as a sign that "it is one of the late outgrowths of biblical literature" ([Orion Center, Menahem Haran, The Two Text-Forms of Psalm 151](https://orion-bibliography.huji.ac.il/node/70568)). The churches' own answers differ, as set out above and in the table of [which books each church reads](../README.md#which-books-each-church-reads): the Orthodox Bible ends the Psalter with it ([Munteanu, Canoniques, non-canoniques ou bons à lire ?](https://www.diacronia.ro/ro/indexing/details/A25808/pdf)), the Catholic canon counts "a hundred and fifty psalms" ([Council of Trent, Session IV](https://history.hanover.edu/texts/trent/ct04.html)), and the Westminster Confession counts the Apocrypha "no part of the canon" ([Westminster Confession of Faith, 1.3](https://www.opc.org/wcf.html)).
