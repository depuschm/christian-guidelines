# Wisdom of Solomon

The Wisdom of Solomon, also called the Book of Wisdom, is a book of Jewish wisdom written in Greek. It opens with a call to rulers: "Love righteousness, all you who are judges of the earth" ([Wisdom 1:1](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/WIS01.htm#V1)). The USCCB introduction holds that it "was written about fifty years before the coming of Christ", that "Its author, whose name is not known to us, was probably a member of the Jewish community at Alexandria, in Egypt", and that "At times he speaks in the person of Solomon, placing his teachings on the lips of the wise king of Hebrew tradition in order to emphasize their value" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Wisdom](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/wisdom/0)). The NETS translator Michael A. Knibb notes "widespread recognition" that the book was composed in Greek, calls it "an exhortation to adopt a particular philosophy", and finds that it "is written in a good Greek style and shows none of the characteristics of translation Greek" ([NETS, Wisdom of Salomon, translated by Michael A. Knibb](https://ccat.sas.upenn.edu/nets/edition/29-wissal-nets.pdf)). The Orthodox Church in America sees in it, as in Sirach, "hellenistic spirit and influences of Greek philosophy", from "the very eve of New Testament times" ([Orthodox Church in America, The Orthodox Faith: Old Testament, Wisdom](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/doctrine-scripture/old-testament/wisdom)). Who wrote it, and when, is one of the open questions below.

The USCCB introduction reads the book as written for "the edification of his co-religionists in a time when they had experienced suffering and oppression, in part at least at the hands of apostate fellow Jews". It divides the book into three parts: the reward of righteousness (1:1–6:21), Solomon's praise of Wisdom (6:22–11:1), and God's care for Israel during the exodus (11:2–19:22), broken by two digressions, on God's mercy and on the folly of idolatry. It adds that "The first ten chapters in particular provide background for the teaching of Jesus and for some New Testament theology about Jesus" and that "Many passages from this section of the book, notably 3:1–8, are used by the church in the liturgy" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Wisdom](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/wisdom/0)). The book never names the figures of Israel's history it retells: the New American Bible notes that "The author omits throughout the book the proper names of the characters in sacred history of whom he speaks" ([USCCB, Wisdom, chapter 7, with notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/wisdom/7); [Wisdom 7:1](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/WIS07.htm#V1)). With Proverbs, Job, Ecclesiastes and Sirach it is one of the wisdom books, and David Penchansky notes that "The Septuagint and the Bibles of the Eastern Churches and the Roman Catholic Church include all five books" ([Bible Odyssey, David Penchansky, What Is Wisdom Literature?](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/what-is-wisdom-literature/); see the [Proverbs](../old-testament/proverbs.md), [Job](../old-testament/job.md) and [Ecclesiastes](../old-testament/ecclesiastes.md) pages).

The book is not part of the Jewish Bible, and the churches disagree about its place in theirs ([which books each church reads](../README.md#which-books-each-church-reads)). The **Catholic Church** counts "the Wisdom of Solomon" among the 46 books of its Old Testament ([Catechism of the Catholic Church, 120](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__PR.HTM)), as the Council of Trent's list of sacred and canonical books did in 1546 ([Council of Trent, Session IV](https://history.hanover.edu/texts/trent/ct04.html)). The **Orthodox** churches read it as one of the *anaginoskómena*, the "books to be read" ([Munteanu, Canoniques, non-canoniques ou bons à lire ?](https://www.diacronia.ro/ro/indexing/details/A25808/pdf)), and use it in their services. The **Church of England** names "The Book of Wisdom" among the books that "the Church doth read for example of life and instruction of manners; but yet doth it not apply them to establish any doctrine" ([Church of England, Articles of Religion, VI](https://www.churchofengland.org/liturgy/book-of-common-prayer/articles-religion#VI)). **Luther**'s Bible printed it among the Apocrypha, books "useful and good to read", and Martin Rösel notes that it is one of the few apocryphal books that Luther is known to have translated himself ([Rösel, Luther und die Apokryphen](https://www.die-bibel.de/luther-und-die-apokryphen)). The **Reformed** Westminster Confession holds that the books called Apocrypha "are no part of the canon of the Scripture" and are not to be used otherwise "than other human writings" ([Westminster Confession of Faith, 1.3](https://www.opc.org/wcf.html)).

## How the churches read it

Where the churches, or their interpreters, read the book differently, or where it has played a part in their history. Each entry starts with its source's score.

- `7` [USCCB, Masses for the Dead: Readings from the Old Testament](https://www.usccb.org/prayer-and-worship/sacraments-and-sacramentals/bereavement-and-funerals/old-testament-readings): **at funerals and on the feasts of saints.** The **Catholic** readings for funerals include [Wisdom 3:1–9](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/WIS03.htm#V1), "The souls of the just are in the hand of God", and [Wisdom 4:7–15](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/WIS04.htm#V7), "The just man, though he die early, shall be at rest". The **Church of England**'s Common Worship funeral service lists readings from Wisdom 3 and 4 among those from the "Old Testament and Apocrypha", and turns Wisdom 3 into a canticle, "A Song of the Righteous", with the refrain "God has found the righteous worthy and their hope is of immortality" ([Church of England, Common Worship: Funeral](https://www.churchofengland.org/liturgy/common-worship/funeral)). The Revised Common Lectionary appoints Wisdom 3:1–9 for All Saints' Day in Year B, among several readings from the book ([Vanderbilt Divinity Library, Revised Common Lectionary: Sunday citations](https://lectionary.library.vanderbilt.edu/sunday-citations/)). In the **Orthodox** Church, the Orthodox Church in America notes that of Ecclesiastes, Sirach and Wisdom "only the Wisdom of Solomon… is read liturgically" ([Orthodox Church in America, The Orthodox Faith: Old Testament, Wisdom](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/doctrine-scripture/old-testament/wisdom)); Wisdom 3:1–9 is among the readings for the feast of the Three Holy Hierarchs, Basil the Great, Gregory the Theologian and John Chrysostom ([Orthodox Church in America, Scripture Readings for 30 January 2026](https://www.oca.org/readings/daily/2026/01/30)).
- `7` [Catechism of the Catholic Church, 279–324](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P19.HTM): **creation, providence and the virtues in Catholic teaching.** The **Catholic** Catechism quotes the book often. It teaches that "Because God creates through wisdom, his creation is ordered: 'You have arranged all things by measure and number and weight'" (paragraph 299, [Wisdom 11:20](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/WIS11.htm#V20)), and quotes 11:24–26 on God who sustains what he made (paragraph 301). It quotes 13:5, that "from the greatness and beauty of created things comes a corresponding perception of their Creator", on how we can speak about God ([Catechism of the Catholic Church, 39–43](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__PC.HTM)), and 7:25–26 on beauty, since God "reveals himself to him through the universal language of creation" ([Catechism of the Catholic Church, 2500–2503](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P8N.HTM)). It quotes 1:13 and 2:24 on death and the devil (paragraph 413, [Catechism of the Catholic Church, 385–421](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P1C.HTM)), and 8:7 on the four virtues that "play a pivotal role and accordingly are called 'cardinal'" (paragraph 1805, [Catechism of the Catholic Church, 1804–1811](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P65.HTM)).
- `7` [Origen, De Principiis, Book I](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/04121.htm) (New Advent): **Wisdom and Christ.** Christian readers have long heard the book's portrait of Wisdom behind the New Testament's words about the Son, who "is the radiance of his glory, the very image of his substance" ([Hebrews 1:3](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/HEB01.htm#V3); see the [Hebrews](../new-testament/hebrews.md) and [Colossians](../new-testament/colossians.md) pages). The church father Origen sets the letter to the Hebrews beside it: "Now, we find in the treatise called the Wisdom of Solomon the following description of the wisdom of God: For she is the breath of the power of God, and the purest efflux of the glory of the Almighty", and reads it of the Son. The 1912 Catholic Encyclopedia holds that passages of the book "find their fulfilment in Christ, the Incarnate 'Word', and 'the Wisdom of God'" ([New Advent, Catholic Encyclopedia (1912), Book of Wisdom](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/15666a.htm)).
- `7` [USCCB, Wisdom, chapter 2, with notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/wisdom/2) (New American Bible, Revised Edition): **the righteous man and the passion of Christ.** The New American Bible notes that "The description of the 'righteous one' in 2:12–20 seems to undergird the New Testament passion narrative", and links 2:13 with the mockers at the cross: "He trusts in God. Let God deliver him now, if he wants him; for he said, 'I am the Son of God'" ([Matthew 27:43](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MAT27.htm#V43)). The **Catholic** Church reads Wisdom 2:1a, 12–22 at Mass on Friday of the fourth week of Lent, with the gospel of the plot against Jesus in John 7 ([USCCB, Readings for Friday of the Fourth Week of Lent (20 March 2026)](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/readings/032026.cfm)), and the Revised Common Lectionary appoints Wisdom 1:16–2:1, 12–22 at Proper 20 in Year B ([Vanderbilt Divinity Library, Revised Common Lectionary: Sunday citations](https://lectionary.library.vanderbilt.edu/sunday-citations/)).
- `7` [Athanasius, Letter 39](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/2806039.htm) (New Advent): **the book in the early church.** The church father Athanasius of Alexandria listed "The Wisdom of Solomon" first among the "other books besides these not indeed included in the Canon, but appointed by the Fathers to be read by those who newly join us, and who wish for instruction in the word of godliness". The Muratorian fragment, an early list of the books of the New Testament, includes "the book of Wisdom, written by the friends of Solomon in his honour" ([New Advent, Fragments of Caius (the Muratorian Canon)](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0510.htm)). Jerome wrote of Wisdom and Sirach: "As, then, the Church reads Judith, Tobit, and the books of Maccabees, but does not admit them among the canonical Scriptures, so let it read these two volumes for the edification of the people, not to give authority to doctrines of the Church" ([Jerome, Prefaces to the Vulgate Old Testament: Proverbs, Ecclesiastes, and the Song of Songs](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/schaff/npnf206.vii.iii.x.html)). The Church of England's Article VI takes up Jerome's distinction, "(as Hierome saith)" ([Church of England, Articles of Religion, VI](https://www.churchofengland.org/liturgy/book-of-common-prayer/articles-religion#VI)), while the Catholic Church holds the book to be canonical ([which books each church reads](../README.md#which-books-each-church-reads)).

## Open questions

Questions on which the churches or scholars disagree, for example about the book's authorship or the meaning of a passage. These guidelines don't pick a side.

- `7` [USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Wisdom](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/wisdom/0) (New American Bible, Revised Edition): **who wrote it, and when?** The book speaks in Solomon's voice: "You chose me to be king of your people" ([Wisdom 9:7–8](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/WIS09.htm#V7)). The Muratorian fragment already ascribed it to "the friends of Solomon in his honour" ([New Advent, Fragments of Caius (the Muratorian Canon)](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0510.htm)). The 1912 Catholic Encyclopedia reports that early Christian writers "commonly assumed this authorship on the basis of the title of the book", but that "it is freely admitted that Solomon is not the writer"; that "the writer to whom the authorship of the work has been oftenest ascribed is Philo", the Jewish philosopher of Alexandria, though it judges that differences in doctrine "preclude a common authorship"; and that the book was written under Ptolemy IV (221–204 BC) or Ptolemy VII (145–117 BC) ([New Advent, Catholic Encyclopedia (1912), Book of Wisdom](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/15666a.htm)). The USCCB introduction dates it "about fifty years before the coming of Christ", and David L. Eastman holds that it "was probably written by a Hellenistic Jew in Alexandria" ([Bible Odyssey, David L. Eastman, Alexandria](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/alexandria/)).
- `7` [USCCB, Wisdom, chapter 8, with notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/wisdom/8) (New American Bible, Revised Edition): **an immortal soul, or the resurrection of the dead?** The book's hope for the righteous is that "their hope is full of immortality" ([Wisdom 3:1–9](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/WIS03.htm#V1)), and it says that "a corruptible body weighs down the soul" ([Wisdom 9:15](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/WIS09.htm#V15)). The HarperCollins Bible Dictionary notes that the only passages of the Greek Old Testament that use the Greek words for immortality "are in books of the Apocrypha", Wisdom and 4 Maccabees, and that "The notion of immortality is a Hellenistic idea", while the hope of individual resurrection "appears first in Dan 12:2-3" ([Bible Odyssey, Immortality (HarperCollins Bible Dictionary)](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/dictionary/immortality/); see the [Daniel](../old-testament/daniel.md) page). Readers differ on how far the book takes over Greek ideas of the soul. On "being good, I came into an undefiled body" ([Wisdom 8:19–20](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/WIS08.htm#V19)), the New American Bible notes that "The Platonic doctrine of the pre-existence of the soul is often read into these lines, but such an anthropology does not seem to be the intent of the author"; the Catholic Encyclopedia records that "many Protestants have claimed" that the passage teaches "the error of the pre-existence of the human soul", and answers that in context it "yields a perfectly orthodox sense" ([New Advent, Catholic Encyclopedia (1912), Book of Wisdom](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/15666a.htm)).
- `6` [USCCB, Romans, chapter 1, with notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/romans/1) (New American Bible, Revised Edition): **did Paul know the Wisdom of Solomon?** Wisdom 13 and Paul's letter to the Romans both hold that God can be known from creation and that idolatry leads to every vice ([Wisdom 13:1–9](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/WIS13.htm#V1), [Romans 1:18–23](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ROM01.htm#V18); see the [Romans](../new-testament/romans.md) page). The New American Bible notes that in Romans 1 "Paul uses themes and rhetoric common in Jewish-Hellenistic mission proclamation", pointing to Wisdom 13–14 as an example, which leaves open whether Paul knew the book itself or the tradition behind it. The 1912 Catholic Encyclopedia holds that "The New Testament writers appear perfectly familiar with this deutero-canonical writing", comparing, for example, [Romans 11:34](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ROM11.htm#V34) with [Wisdom 9:13](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/WIS09.htm#V13) and Hebrews 1:3 with Wisdom 7:26 ([New Advent, Catholic Encyclopedia (1912), Book of Wisdom](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/15666a.htm)).
