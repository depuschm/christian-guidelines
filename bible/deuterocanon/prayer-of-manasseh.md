# Prayer of Manasseh

The Prayer of Manasseh is a short penitential prayer of fifteen verses, which the WEBBE heads "The Prayer of Manasses King of Judah, When He Was Held Captive in Babylon" ([Prayer of Manasseh](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MAN.htm)). Manasseh reigned in Judah for fifty-five years. The Second Book of Kings tells of his idolatry and violence, and blames the fall of Judah on his sins ([2 Kings 21:1–18](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2KI21.htm#V1), [2 Kings 23:26–27](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2KI23.htm#V26)). The Second Book of Chronicles adds that, taken in chains to Babylon, "he begged the LORD his God, and humbled himself greatly", and that God heard him and brought him back to Jerusalem ([2 Chronicles 33:11–13](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2CH33.htm#V11)). It says that "his prayer to his God" was written in the records of the kings, but it doesn't give the words ([2 Chronicles 33:18–19](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2CH33.htm#V18); see the [2 Kings](../old-testament/2-kings.md) and [2 Chronicles](../old-testament/2-chronicles.md) pages). The Catholic Encyclopedia calls the prayer "A beautiful Penitential prayer put in the mouth of Manasses, King of Juda", which "is based on II Paralipomenon, xxxiii, 11-13" ([Catholic Encyclopedia, Apocrypha: Apocryphal psalms and prayers](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/01601a.htm#I3)). The Jewish Encyclopedia judges it "most probably, the attempt of some pious Jew of later times to reproduce" the prayer that Chronicles mentions ([Jewish Encyclopedia, Manasseh, Prayer of](https://www.jewishencyclopedia.com/articles/10344-manasseh-prayer-of)).

The HarperCollins Bible Dictionary dates the prayer "sometime during the second or first century BCE" and finds it "typical of Jewish prayer during the intertestamental period" ([Bible Odyssey, Prayer of Manasseh, The (HarperCollins Bible Dictionary)](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/dictionary/prayer-of-manasseh-the/)); whether it was first written in Greek or in Hebrew is one of the open questions below. It has come down to us in Christian books. The Jewish Encyclopedia finds it "among the Canticles which, in some of the Septuagint manuscripts, are appended to the Book of Psalms", at the end of 2 Chronicles in some Latin manuscripts, and in the "Didascalia" and the "Apostolic Constitutions" ([Jewish Encyclopedia, Manasseh, Prayer of](https://www.jewishencyclopedia.com/articles/10344-manasseh-prayer-of)). The NETS translation of the Psalms, which follows Alfred Rahlfs's edition of the Greek Psalms with the Odes, prints it after Psalm 151 as "Rahlfs Ode 12" ([NETS, Psalms (and Prayer of Manasses), translated by Albert Pietersma](https://ccat.sas.upenn.edu/nets/edition/24-ps-nets.pdf)).

The prayer is not part of the Jewish Bible, and the churches disagree about its place in theirs ([which books each church reads](../README.md#which-books-each-church-reads)). The **Orthodox** churches read it among the *anaginoskómena*, the "books to be read" ([Munteanu, Canoniques, non-canoniques ou bons à lire ?](https://www.diacronia.ro/ro/indexing/details/A25808/pdf)), and the WEBBE's own heading notes that it "is recognised as Deuterocanonical Scripture by the Greek Orthodox and Russian Orthodox Churches" ([Prayer of Manasseh](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MAN.htm)). The **Catholic Church** doesn't count it among the 46 books of its Old Testament ([Catechism of the Catholic Church, 120](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__PR.HTM)), and the Council of Trent's list of 1546 doesn't name it ([Council of Trent, Session IV](https://history.hanover.edu/texts/trent/ct04.html)); the Clementine Vulgate printed it in an appendix ([Catholic Encyclopedia, Apocrypha: Apocryphal psalms and prayers](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/01601a.htm#I3)). The **Church of England** names "The Prayer of Manasses" among the books that "the Church doth read for example of life and instruction of manners; but yet doth it not apply them to establish any doctrine" ([Church of England, Articles of Religion, VI](https://www.churchofengland.org/liturgy/book-of-common-prayer/articles-religion#VI)). **Luther**'s Bible printed it with the Apocrypha, books "useful and good to read" ([Rösel, Luther und die Apokryphen](https://www.die-bibel.de/luther-und-die-apokryphen)). The **Reformed** Westminster Confession holds that the books called Apocrypha "are no part of the canon of the Scripture" and are not to be used otherwise "than other human writings" ([Westminster Confession of Faith, 1.3](https://www.opc.org/wcf.html)).

## Outline

The prayer in its own order. The NRSVUE divides it into an ascription of praise (verses 1–8), a confession of sins (9–10) and a supplication for pardon (11–15) ([NRSVUE, Prayer of Manasseh 1](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Prayer%20of%20Manasseh%201&version=NRSVUE)).

| Passage | Contents |
| --- | --- |
| [Prayer of Manasseh 1–4](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MAN01.htm#V1) | The prayer calls on the Lord Almighty, God of Abraham, Isaac and Jacob, who made heaven and earth and bound the sea. |
| [Prayer of Manasseh 5–8](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MAN01.htm#V5) | God's anger at sinners is unbearable, but his mercy is without measure: he has appointed repentance for sinners, and for the one who prays. |
| [Prayer of Manasseh 9–10](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MAN01.htm#V9) | The king confesses sins more than the sand of the sea, bowed down "with many iron bands". |
| [Prayer of Manasseh 11–13](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MAN01.htm#V11) | He bows "the knee of my heart", confesses again and asks to be forgiven. |
| [Prayer of Manasseh 14–15](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MAN01.htm#V14) | He trusts that God will save him, unworthy as he is, and promises to praise him for ever. |

Verse 8 in the WEBBE opens with a sentence ("You, O Lord, according to your great goodness have promised repentance and forgiveness to those who have sinned against you… that they may be saved") that the NRSVUE prints at the end of verse 7, with the note that "Other ancient authorities lack" it ([NRSVUE, Prayer of Manasseh 1](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Prayer%20of%20Manasseh%201&version=NRSVUE)). The Jewish Encyclopedia notes that this passage is "not found in the Greek MSS. of the LXX." ([Jewish Encyclopedia, Manasseh, Prayer of](https://www.jewishencyclopedia.com/articles/10344-manasseh-prayer-of)), and the NETS translation of the Greek Ode doesn't have it ([NETS, Psalms (and Prayer of Manasses)](https://ccat.sas.upenn.edu/nets/edition/24-ps-nets.pdf)).

## How the churches read it

Where the churches, or their interpreters, read the prayer differently, or where it has played a part in their history. Each entry starts with its source's score. The [2 Chronicles](../old-testament/2-chronicles.md) page describes the prayer's place in Anglican, Orthodox and Catholic use alongside the story of Manasseh.

- `7` [Apostolic Constitutions, Book II](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/07152.htm) (New Advent): **an example of repentance for bishops.** Chapter 22 of Book II, under the heading "That David, the Ninevites, Hezekiah, and His Son Manasseh, are Eminent Examples of Repentance", tells the bishop "to have before your eyes the examples of those that have gone before". It retells Manasseh's sins from Kings and Chronicles, quotes the whole prayer, and adds that "there appeared a flame of fire about him, and all the iron shackles and chains which were about him fell off". It concludes that God "not only forgives the penitent, but reinstates them in their former dignity". The Jewish Encyclopedia notes that the prayer also appears in the "Didascalia", "where it follows a reference to II Chron. xxxiii." ([Jewish Encyclopedia, Manasseh, Prayer of](https://www.jewishencyclopedia.com/articles/10344-manasseh-prayer-of)).
- `7` [Munteanu, Canoniques, non-canoniques ou bons à lire ?](https://www.diacronia.ro/ro/indexing/details/A25808/pdf) (in French): **in the Orthodox Bible and at Great Compline.** Munteanu counts the Prayer of Manasseh among the books that Orthodox Bibles add to the 39, and notes that the first printed Slavonic Bible, published at Ostrog in 1581, added it at the end of 2 Chronicles. In the **Orthodox** Lenten service of Great Compline with the Canon of St Andrew of Crete, the priest reads the prayer in the plural: "But now, Lord, we fall to our knees in our hearts, entreating You to pour forth Your goodness upon us", "for You are the God of repentant sinners!" ([Orthodox Church in America, Archdiocese of Canada, Great Compline with the Canon of St Andrew of Crete](https://www.archdiocese.ca/sites/default/files/liturgical-music/6-lent/02%20Compline%20with%20Canon%20of%20St.Andrew%20of%20Crete/Great%20Compline%20served%20with%20the%20Canon%20of%20St%20Andrew.pdf)).
- `7` [The Episcopal Church, Book of Common Prayer (1979), Morning Prayer: Rite Two, Canticle 14](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Page:Book_of_common_prayer_%28TEC%2C_1979%29.pdf/90) (Wikisource): **a canticle for Lent.** The **Episcopal Church**'s Prayer Book sets verses 1–2, 4, 6–7 and 11–15 as Canticle 14, "A Song of Penitence", *Kyrie Pantokrator*, "Especially suitable in Lent, and on other penitential occasions", and renders verse 11 "And now, O Lord, I bend the knee of my heart". The **Church of England** prays a shortened form as Canticle 52, "The Song of Manasseh", in Common Worship's daily prayer ([Church of England, Common Worship: Canticle 52, The Song of Manasseh](https://www.churchofengland.org/liturgy/common-worship/daily-prayer/canticles-daily-prayer/52-song-manasseh)).
- `7` [Rösel, Luther und die Apokryphen](https://www.die-bibel.de/luther-und-die-apokryphen) (Deutsche Bibelgesellschaft, in German): **a prayer to close the Old Testament.** Martin Rösel writes that Luther took the Prayer of Manasseh into his Bible, but not as a book: it has no place in the table of contents of the Apocrypha and no preface of its own, and it serves as a prayer to close the Old Testament (*es dient als Gebet zum Abschluss des Alten Testaments*). The 2017 revision of the Luther Bible translated it afresh from the Greek of the Göttingen Septuagint. The Jewish Encyclopedia notes that the prayer "has been deservedly retained by Luther, and is included in the authorized English version, among the Apocrypha" ([Jewish Encyclopedia, Manasseh, Prayer of](https://www.jewishencyclopedia.com/articles/10344-manasseh-prayer-of)).
- `6` [Catholic Encyclopedia, Apocrypha: Apocryphal psalms and prayers](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/01601a.htm#I3) (New Advent, 1907): **in the appendix of the Latin Bible.** For the **Catholic** Church, the prayer is outside the canon, but it has stayed in the Latin Bible: "In editions of the Vulgate antedating the Council of Trent it was placed after the books of Paralipomenon. The Clementine Vulgate relegated it to the appendix, where it is still to be found in reprints of the standard text." The Jewish Encyclopedia adds that the Latin translation "is not by Jerome" ([Jewish Encyclopedia, Manasseh, Prayer of](https://www.jewishencyclopedia.com/articles/10344-manasseh-prayer-of)).

## Open questions

Questions on which the churches or scholars disagree, for example about the book's authorship or the meaning of a passage. These guidelines don't pick a side.

- `7` [Sefaria, Mishnah Sanhedrin 10:2](https://www.sefaria.org/Mishnah_Sanhedrin.10.2): **was Manasseh forgiven, and what does his story teach?** The two biblical accounts differ: Kings records no repentance and blames the exile on Manasseh ([2 Kings 23:26–27](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2KI23.htm#V26)), while Chronicles tells of his prayer and return ([2 Chronicles 33:11–13](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2CH33.htm#V11); the [2 Chronicles](../old-testament/2-chronicles.md) page sets out how scholars explain the difference). The Mishnah counts Manasseh among three kings who "have no share in the World-to-Come", but "Rabbi Yehuda says: Manasseh has a share in the World-to-Come", citing his prayer in Chronicles. In the Talmud, Rabbi Yoḥanan holds that "Anyone who says that Manasseh has no share in the World-to-Come discourages penitents" ([Sefaria, Sanhedrin 103a:2](https://www.sefaria.org/Sanhedrin.103a.2)), and teaches that God "crafted for him a type of opening in Heaven in order to accept him in repentance" ([Sefaria, Sanhedrin 103a:3](https://www.sefaria.org/Sanhedrin.103a.3)). The Apostolic Constitutions hold him up to bishops as proof that God "not only forgives the penitent, but reinstates them in their former dignity" ([Apostolic Constitutions, Book II](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/07152.htm)). The Jewish Encyclopedia notes that "There appears to be no trace of the Prayer of Manasseh in Jewish tradition" ([Jewish Encyclopedia, Manasseh, Prayer of](https://www.jewishencyclopedia.com/articles/10344-manasseh-prayer-of)).
- `6` [Jewish Encyclopedia, Manasseh, Prayer of](https://www.jewishencyclopedia.com/articles/10344-manasseh-prayer-of) (1906): **who wrote it, when, and in what language?** The Jewish Encyclopedia reports that some scholars held the Greek to be a translation from a Hebrew original, and answers: "This is not impossible, but it is not supported by sufficient evidence", and that "The author was evidently a Jew". It also reports the view of the scholar Nestle that the Greek text in the Septuagint manuscripts "comes from the 'Apostolic Constitutions,' or from the 'Didascalia'". The Catholic Encyclopedia finds that "Learned opinion differs as to whether the prayer which has come down to us was written in Hebrew or Greek", and that "The prayer breathes a Christian spirit, and it is not entirely certain that it is really of Jewish origin" ([Catholic Encyclopedia, Apocrypha: Apocryphal psalms and prayers](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/01601a.htm#I3)). The HarperCollins Bible Dictionary dates it to the second or first century BCE ([Bible Odyssey, Prayer of Manasseh, The](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/dictionary/prayer-of-manasseh-the/)).
- `6` [Bible Odyssey, Prayer of Manasseh, The (HarperCollins Bible Dictionary)](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/dictionary/prayer-of-manasseh-the/): **does it belong in the Bible?** The dictionary sums up: "Protestants include it among the Apocrypha", and "It is considered canonical in Eastern Orthodoxy". The Jewish Encyclopedia holds that it "was never distinctly recognized as canonical by the Church" ([Jewish Encyclopedia, Manasseh, Prayer of](https://www.jewishencyclopedia.com/articles/10344-manasseh-prayer-of)). The churches' own answers differ, as set out above and in the table of [which books each church reads](../README.md#which-books-each-church-reads): the Orthodox read it among the *anaginoskómena* ([Munteanu, Canoniques, non-canoniques ou bons à lire ?](https://www.diacronia.ro/ro/indexing/details/A25808/pdf)), the Catholic canon doesn't include it ([Catechism of the Catholic Church, 120](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__PR.HTM)), the Church of England reads it "for example of life" but not for doctrine ([Church of England, Articles of Religion, VI](https://www.churchofengland.org/liturgy/book-of-common-prayer/articles-religion#VI)), and the Westminster Confession counts the Apocrypha "no part of the canon" ([Westminster Confession of Faith, 1.3](https://www.opc.org/wcf.html)).
