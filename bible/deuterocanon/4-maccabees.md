# 4 Maccabees

The Fourth Book of Maccabees is not a history but a philosophical discourse. Its author sets out to prove "that religious reasoning is absolute master of the emotions" ([4 Maccabees 1:1](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/4MA01.htm#V1)), and proves it from the deaths of the aged priest Eleazar, the seven brothers and their mother, whose martyrdom under King Antiochus IV is told in [2 Maccabees 6:18–7:42](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2MA06.htm#V18) (see the [2 Maccabees](2-maccabees.md) page). The Jewish Encyclopedia calls it "a semiphilosophic discourse, or sermon, on the 'supremacy of the pious reason'", with a prologue, a part that explains the thesis and a part that illustrates it, and concludes that "the only connection this work has with the Maccabees is in the fact that the author's illustrations are drawn from the Second Book of the Maccabees" ([Jewish Encyclopedia, Maccabees, Books of](https://www.jewishencyclopedia.com/articles/10237-maccabees-books-of)). The HarperCollins Bible Dictionary describes it as "a festival oration in memory of the martyrs", whose theme is "the rule of religious reason and the four cardinal virtues—prudence, temperance, courage, and justice—over the emotions" ([Bible Odyssey, Maccabees, The Fourth Book of the (HarperCollins Bible Dictionary)](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/dictionary/maccabees-the-fourth-book-of-the/)).

Stephen Westerholm, who translated the book for NETS, notes that "4 Makkabees is an original composition in Greek", written "in a highly rhetorical and affected Greek style", "in striking contrast with the Jewish conservatism that marks the book's message": the law of Moses "is to be faithfully observed in spite of pressures to assimilate to the surrounding Hellenistic environment". Of the three manuscripts on which the standard edition of the Septuagint is largely based, Vaticanus, Sinaiticus and Alexandrinus, "only the latter two include 4 Makkabees" ([NETS, 4 Makkabees, translated by Stephen Westerholm](https://ccat.sas.upenn.edu/nets/edition/23-4makk-nets.pdf)). The Jewish Encyclopedia holds that the author "was an Alexandrian Jew" and that the book "was written probably at the close of the last century B.C. or during the first century C.E., and before the time of Caligula" ([Jewish Encyclopedia, Maccabees, Books of](https://www.jewishencyclopedia.com/articles/10237-maccabees-books-of)); the Catholic Encyclopedia finds that it "appears to have been produced before the fall of Jerusalem, but its date is a matter of conjecture" ([Catholic Encyclopedia, Apocrypha: Jewish philosophy](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/01601a.htm#I4)). Who wrote it is one of the open questions below. The Jewish Encyclopedia finds its nearest parallel in the New Testament: "The Hellenic and the Jewish elements in his work both appear at their best and in a combination almost without a parallel; the nearest example is the New Testament Epistle to the Hebrews."

The book is not part of the Jewish Bible, and the churches give it, at most, a place at the edge of theirs ([which books each church reads](../README.md#which-books-each-church-reads)). The WEBBE's own heading notes that it "appears in an appendix to the Greek Septuagint. It is considered to be apocrypha by most church traditions" ([4 Maccabees](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/4MA.htm)). For the **Orthodox** churches, Ştefan Munteanu records that the Greek synodal edition of the Septuagint of 1937 added 4 Maccabees in an appendix, because the Athens editions of 1843 and 1892 had printed it ([Munteanu, Canoniques, non-canoniques ou bons à lire ?](https://www.diacronia.ro/ro/indexing/details/A25808/pdf), in French). The **Catholic Church** counts "1 and 2 Maccabees" among the 46 books of its Old Testament ([Catechism of the Catholic Church, 120](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__PR.HTM)), as the Council of Trent named "two books of the Machabees, the first and the second" ([Council of Trent, Session IV](https://history.hanover.edu/texts/trent/ct04.html)); the Catholic Encyclopedia says of the third and fourth books, "Neither book has any claim to canonicity" ([Catholic Encyclopedia, The Books of Machabees](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/09495a.htm)). The **Church of England**'s list of the books it reads "for example of life and instruction of manners" names the first and second books of Maccabees, but not the fourth ([Church of England, Articles of Religion, VI](https://www.churchofengland.org/liturgy/book-of-common-prayer/articles-religion#VI)). **Luther**'s Bible took in 1 and 2 Maccabees, but not 4 Maccabees ([Rösel, Luther und die Apokryphen](https://www.die-bibel.de/luther-und-die-apokryphen), in German). The **Reformed** Westminster Confession holds that the books called Apocrypha "are no part of the canon of the Scripture" ([Westminster Confession of Faith, 1.3](https://www.opc.org/wcf.html)). The **Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church**'s list of its canonical books doesn't include it ([Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church, The Canonical Books](https://www.ethiopianorthodox.org/english/canonical/books.html)).

## How the churches read it

Where the churches, or their interpreters, read the book differently, or where it has played a part in their history. Each entry starts with its source's score.

- `7` [Orthodox Church in America, 7 Holy Maccabee Martyrs](https://www.oca.org/saints/lives/2026/08/01/102162-7-holy-maccabee-martyrs): **the martyrs as saints.** The **Orthodox** Church commemorates "The seven holy Maccabee martyrs", "their mother Solomonia and their teacher Eleazar" on 1 August, tells their story from 2 Maccabees, and notes that "Various Fathers of the Church preached sermons on the seven Maccabees, including Saint Cyprian of Carthage, Saint Ambrose of Milan, Saint Gregory Nazianzus and Saint John Chrysostom." Jerome lists Gregory's "In praise of the Maccabees" among his works ([Jerome, De Viris Illustribus](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/2708.htm), 117). The [2 Maccabees](2-maccabees.md) page sets out how the churches honour the martyrs.
- `7` [Munteanu, Canoniques, non-canoniques ou bons à lire ?](https://www.diacronia.ro/ro/indexing/details/A25808/pdf) (in French): **in some Orthodox Bibles, but not all.** Munteanu traces the book through the printed Bibles of the Orthodox churches. The Romanian Bible of Bucharest of 1688 included 1, 2, 3 and 4 Maccabees, grouped at the end of the Old Testament as apocryphal books, as Protestant Bibles did; the Greek synodal edition of 1937 printed 4 Maccabees in an appendix; but the modern Greek translation published with the blessing of the Greek synod in 1997 leaves it out, and so does the Orthodox Study Bible of 2008.
- `7` [Catechism of the Catholic Church, 1804–1811](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P65.HTM): **the four cardinal virtues.** The **Catholic** Catechism teaches that "Four virtues play a pivotal role and accordingly are called 'cardinal'; all the others are grouped around them. They are: prudence, justice, fortitude, and temperance", and cites [Wisdom 8:7](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/WIS08.htm#V7) for them. The Catholic Encyclopedia holds that the author of 4 Maccabees "derived his four cardinal virtues" from Stoic philosophy, "and it was through Fourth Machabees that this category was appropriated by early Christian ascetical writers" ([Catholic Encyclopedia, Apocrypha: Jewish philosophy](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/01601a.htm#I4)).
- `7` [Church of England, Articles of Religion, VI](https://www.churchofengland.org/liturgy/book-of-common-prayer/articles-religion#VI): **outside the Protestant Apocrypha.** The **Church of England**'s Article VI lists the books "the Church doth read for example of life and instruction of manners", among them "The First Book of Maccabees" and "The Second Book of Maccabees", but not the third or fourth. Martin Rösel records that **Luther** took 1 and 2 Maccabees into his Bible, and that the Zurich Bible later added 3 and 4 Esdras and 3 Maccabees, but neither took in 4 Maccabees ([Rösel, Luther und die Apokryphen](https://www.die-bibel.de/luther-und-die-apokryphen), in German). The **Reformed** Westminster Confession holds that the Apocrypha may be used no otherwise "than other human writings" ([Westminster Confession of Faith, 1.3](https://www.opc.org/wcf.html)).

## Open questions

Questions on which the churches or scholars disagree, for example about the book's authorship or the meaning of a passage. These guidelines don't pick a side.

- `7` [Eusebius, Church History, Book III](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/250103.htm): **who wrote it, and for what occasion?** Eusebius attributed the book to the Jewish historian Josephus: "Another work of no little merit has been produced by the same writer, On the Supremacy of Reason, which some have called Maccabaicum, because it contains an account of the struggles of those Hebrews who contended manfully for the true religion" (chapter 10). Jerome agreed that Josephus wrote a book "On all ruling wisdom, in which the martyr deaths of the Maccabeans are related", which "is highly esteemed" ([Jerome, De Viris Illustribus](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/2708.htm), 13). The Catholic Encyclopedia judges that the work "was erroneously ascribed to Josephus by Eusebius and others", and that its author "is unknown" ([Catholic Encyclopedia, Apocrypha: Jewish philosophy](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/01601a.htm#I4)); the Jewish Encyclopedia finds that "the language and style of the work differ so radically from those of the writings of Josephus that it is clear that this is a mistaken opinion", and notes that the old attribution "has caused the text of IV Maccabees to be included in many editions of the works of Josephus" ([Jewish Encyclopedia, Maccabees, Books of](https://www.jewishencyclopedia.com/articles/10237-maccabees-books-of)). On the occasion, the book speaks of the martyrs "who died with their mother at this time" ([4 Maccabees 1:10](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/4MA01.htm#V10)), and the HarperCollins Bible Dictionary calls it "a festival oration in memory of the martyrs" ([Bible Odyssey, Maccabees, The Fourth Book of the](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/dictionary/maccabees-the-fourth-book-of-the/)). The Jewish Encyclopedia reports that "Ewald and Freudenthal called it a sermon and held that it is an example of Alexandrian synagogue preaching, but this view is now abandoned", holding instead that "it is an address to a more select circle".
- `6` [Jewish Encyclopedia, Atonement](https://www.jewishencyclopedia.com/articles/2092-atonement) (1906): **does the book shape the New Testament's language of atonement?** The book calls the martyrs "the ransom to the sin of the nation", and their death "the death that appeased wrath" ([4 Maccabees 17:20–22](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/4MA17.htm#V20); Eleazar's prayer in [4 Maccabees 6:26–29](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/4MA06.htm#V26)); the NETS translation renders the last phrase "the propitiatory of their death" ([NETS, 4 Makkabees](https://ccat.sas.upenn.edu/nets/edition/23-4makk-nets.pdf)). Paul writes that God sent Christ "to be an atoning sacrifice through faith in his blood" ([Romans 3:25](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ROM03.htm#V25); see the [Romans](../new-testament/romans.md) page), and Jesus says that he came "to give his life as a ransom for many" ([Mark 10:45](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MRK10.htm#V45)). The Jewish Encyclopedia holds that the idea of "the atoning power of the suffering and death of the righteous finds expression also in IV Macc.", and that it "formed the basis of Paul's doctrine of the atoning blood of Christ (Rom. iii. 25)"; it traces the idea itself back to the suffering servant of Isaiah. The **Catholic** Catechism reads Christ's death in the light of Isaiah: the Scriptures foretold it "as the ransom that would free men from the slavery of sin", and "In particular Jesus' redemptive death fulfils Isaiah's prophecy of the suffering Servant" ([Catechism of the Catholic Church, 595–623](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P1O.HTM), paragraph 601; [Isaiah 53:10–12](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ISA53.htm#V10); see the [Isaiah](../old-testament/isaiah.md) page).
- `6` [Jewish Encyclopedia, Maccabees, Books of](https://www.jewishencyclopedia.com/articles/10237-maccabees-books-of) (1906): **immortal souls, or the resurrection of the body?** The Jewish Encyclopedia finds that the author "is a strong believer in immortality, but he has abandoned the Pharisaic standpoint of II Maccabees, which recognizes a bodily resurrection, and holds to the view that all souls exist forever". The book ends with the martyrs "having received pure and immortal souls from God" ([4 Maccabees 18:23](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/4MA18.htm#V23)), where 2 Maccabees tells the same story with the hope of being raised (see the [2 Maccabees](2-maccabees.md) page and [Daniel 12:2](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/DAN12.htm#V2)). The HarperCollins Bible Dictionary notes that the only passages of the Greek Old Testament that use the Greek words for immortality are in "the Wisdom of Solomon and 4 Maccabees", and that "The notion of immortality is a Hellenistic idea" ([Bible Odyssey, Immortality (HarperCollins Bible Dictionary)](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/dictionary/immortality/)). The **Catholic** Catechism holds the two together: at death "the soul goes to meet God, while awaiting its reunion with its glorified body" ([Catechism of the Catholic Church, 992–1004](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P2H.HTM), paragraph 997).
- `6` [Catholic Encyclopedia, Apocrypha: Jewish philosophy](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/01601a.htm#I4) (New Advent, 1907): **Greek philosophy or Jewish faith?** The Catholic Encyclopedia describes the book as "a short philosophical treatise on the supremacy of pious reason, that is reason regulated by divine law, which for the author is the Mosaic Law", and holds that "In setting up reason as the master of human passion, the author was distinctly influenced by Stoic philosophy", while his "aim" was "to inculcate devotion to the Law". The Jewish Encyclopedia finds "a large philosophic element of the Stoic type, though its author possessed a taste for philosophy rather than real philosophical insight. It contains also a core of Judaism" ([Jewish Encyclopedia, Maccabees, Books of](https://www.jewishencyclopedia.com/articles/10237-maccabees-books-of)). The NETS translator sets the book's "Jewish conservatism" against its Greek style ([NETS, 4 Makkabees](https://ccat.sas.upenn.edu/nets/edition/23-4makk-nets.pdf)). The book itself grounds reason in the law God gave to the mind ([4 Maccabees 2:21–23](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/4MA02.htm#V21)), and holds that reason doesn't root out the emotions but withstands them ([4 Maccabees 3:1–5](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/4MA03.htm#V1)).
- `6` [Bible Odyssey, Maccabees, The Fourth Book of the (HarperCollins Bible Dictionary)](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/dictionary/maccabees-the-fourth-book-of-the/): **does it belong in the Bible?** The dictionary explains that the NRSV includes the book among the Apocrypha "because it is considered canonical by Eastern Orthodox churches". Other sources describe a looser place: the WEBBE notes that it "appears in an appendix to the Greek Septuagint" and "is considered to be apocrypha by most church traditions" ([4 Maccabees](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/4MA.htm)), and Munteanu shows that some Orthodox Bibles print it, in an appendix or among the apocryphal books, while others leave it out ([Munteanu, Canoniques, non-canoniques ou bons à lire ?](https://www.diacronia.ro/ro/indexing/details/A25808/pdf)). The Catholic Encyclopedia holds that it has no "claim to canonicity" ([Catholic Encyclopedia, The Books of Machabees](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/09495a.htm)), and Protestant churches don't count it among their Apocrypha (see [which books each church reads](../README.md#which-books-each-church-reads)).
