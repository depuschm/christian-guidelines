# Daniel (Greek)

The Greek versions of Daniel contain three passages that are not in the Hebrew and Aramaic book. The USCCB introduction describes them: "The added episodes of Susanna, Bel, and the Dragon, found only in the Greek version, are edifying short stories with a didactic purpose (chaps. 13–14). The Greek version also adds a long prayer, numbered in the NAB and the Greek, 3:24–90, between 3:23–24 in the Hebrew text" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Daniel](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/daniel/0)). The Hebrew and Aramaic book, which every church reads, has its own page: [Daniel](../old-testament/daniel.md). This page describes the Greek additions. The WEBBE translates the whole Greek book and notes at each addition that it is "not found in the traditional Hebrew text of Daniel" and is "recognised as Deuterocanonical Scripture by the Roman Catholic, Greek Orthodox, and Russian Orthodox Churches" ([Daniel (Greek) 3:23](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/DAG03.htm#V23), [Daniel (Greek) 13:1](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/DAG13.htm#V1), [Daniel (Greek) 14:1](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/DAG14.htm#V1)). The HarperCollins Bible Dictionary sums them up as "Several stories, a prayer, and a hymn not found in the Hebrew text of Daniel, but present in the LXX, an ancient Greek version of the OT" ([Bible Odyssey, Daniel, Additions to (HarperCollins Bible Dictionary)](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/dictionary/daniel-additions-to/)).

The first addition stands inside the story of the fiery furnace. Azariah, one of the three young men, prays in the fire, confessing his people's sins and asking for mercy; the angel of the Lord drives back the flames; and the three sing a hymn calling on all creation to "bless the Lord", the hymn known as the Benedicite ([New Advent, Catholic Encyclopedia (1908), Daniel, Book of](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/04621b.htm)). The second, Susanna, tells of a faithful wife falsely accused of adultery by two elders whose advances she refused, and saved when the young Daniel cross-examines them. The third, Bel and the Dragon, tells two stories in which Daniel shows that an idol and a living dragon worshipped in Babylon are not gods, and is thrown to the lions, where the prophet Habakkuk is carried from Judea to feed him (see [Habakkuk](../old-testament/habakkuk.md)). In the Old Greek version this story carries the heading "From the prophecy of Habakkuk, son of Joshua, of the tribe of Levi", which the New American Bible notes "may represent an earlier form of the story, before it was attached to the Book of Daniel" ([USCCB, Daniel, chapter 14, with notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/daniel/14)).

The book survives in two Greek versions, the Old Greek and the version attributed to Theodotion. The NETS translator R. Timothy McLay writes that "The (earlier) OG translation, of which few witnesses remain, had been supplanted by the TH version by the first or second century CE" ([NETS, Daniel, translated by R. Timothy McLay](https://ccat.sas.upenn.edu/nets/edition/40-daniel-nets.pdf)), and Jerome, translating Daniel into Latin around 392, wrote that "The Septuagint version of Daniel the prophet is not read by the Churches of our Lord and Saviour. They use Theodotion's version, but how this came to pass I cannot tell" ([Jerome, Prefaces to the Vulgate Old Testament: Daniel](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/schaff/npnf206.vii.iii.xiii.html)). In Theodotion's version Susanna comes before the rest of the book, while in the Old Greek it is the last chapter ([NETS, Sousanna, translated by R. Timothy McLay](https://ccat.sas.upenn.edu/nets/edition/39-sousanna-nets.pdf)); the Latin Vulgate prints it as chapter 13, followed by Bel and the Dragon as chapter 14 ([New Advent, Catholic Encyclopedia (1908), Daniel, Book of](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/04621b.htm)), and so do the New American Bible and the WEBBE. No Hebrew or Aramaic text of the additions survives. The New American Bible's notes hold that they "were probably first composed in Hebrew or Aramaic" ([USCCB, Daniel, chapter 3, with notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/daniel/3)) and that Susanna and Bel "were never part of the Hebrew-Aramaic Book of Daniel, or of the Hebrew Bible" ([USCCB, Daniel, chapter 13, with notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/daniel/13)).

The churches disagree about the additions, though every church reads the Hebrew and Aramaic book ([which books each church reads](../README.md#which-books-each-church-reads)). The **Catholic Church** counts "Daniel" among the 46 books of its Old Testament ([Catechism of the Catholic Church, 120](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__PR.HTM)), and the Council of Trent received the sacred books "entire with all their parts, as they have been used to be read in the Catholic Church, and as they are contained in the old Latin vulgate edition" ([Council of Trent, Session IV](https://history.hanover.edu/texts/trent/ct04.html)); the New American Bible notes that "the Catholic Church has always included them among the inspired writings; they existed in the Septuagint, which was used as its Bible by the early church" ([USCCB, Daniel, chapter 13, with notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/daniel/13)). The **Orthodox** churches read the Greek book, and count its additions among the *anaginoskómena*, the "books to be read" ([Munteanu, Canoniques, non-canoniques ou bons à lire ?](https://www.diacronia.ro/ro/indexing/details/A25808/pdf)). The **Church of England** names "The Song of the Three Children", "The Story of Susanna" and "Of Bel and the Dragon" among the books that "the Church doth read for example of life and instruction of manners; but yet doth it not apply them to establish any doctrine" ([Church of England, Articles of Religion, VI](https://www.churchofengland.org/liturgy/book-of-common-prayer/articles-religion#VI)). **Luther**'s Bible printed the additions to Daniel among the Apocrypha, books "useful and good to read" ([Rösel, Luther und die Apokryphen](https://www.die-bibel.de/luther-und-die-apokryphen)). The **Reformed** Westminster Confession holds that the books called Apocrypha "are no part of the canon of the Scripture" and are not to be used otherwise "than other human writings" ([Westminster Confession of Faith, 1.3](https://www.opc.org/wcf.html)).

## How the churches read it

Where the churches, or their interpreters, read the book differently, or where it has played a part in their history. Each entry starts with its source's score.

- `7` [Vatican, John Paul II, General Audience of 2 May 2001](https://www.vatican.va/content/john-paul-ii/en/audiences/2001/documents/hf_jp-ii_aud_20010502.html): **the song of the three in the churches' prayer.** The song is sung in the morning prayer of the **Catholic**, **Anglican** and **Orthodox** churches (see [Daniel: how the churches read it](../old-testament/daniel.md#how-the-churches-read-it)). John Paul II calls it a "marvellous litany-like prayer" that "is well-suited to the Dies Domini, the Day of the Lord", and notes that "Prayed in the light of Christian revelation, it is addressed to the Trinitarian God, as we are invited to do by the liturgy which adds a Trinitarian formula to the Canticle". The Catholic Mass reads Azariah's prayer, [Daniel (Greek) 3:25](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/DAG03.htm#V25), [Daniel (Greek) 3:34–43](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/DAG03.htm#V34), on Tuesday of the third week of Lent, with [Matthew 18:21–35](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MAT18.htm#V21) ([USCCB, Readings for Tuesday of the Third Week of Lent (10 March 2026)](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/readings/031026.cfm)), and sings the blessings that open the song, Daniel 3:52–56 in its numbering ([Daniel (Greek) 3:52–57](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/DAG03.htm#V52) in the WEBBE), as the psalm on Trinity Sunday in Year A ([USCCB, Readings for the Most Holy Trinity (31 May 2026)](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/readings/053126.cfm)). The **Book of Common Prayer** offers the Benedicite, "O all ye Works of the Lord, bless ye the Lord", as an alternative to the Te Deum at Morning Prayer ([Church of England, Book of Common Prayer: The Order for Morning Prayer](https://www.churchofengland.org/liturgy/book-of-common-prayer/order-morning-prayer)), and Common Worship has two canticles from it: "Bless the Lord", from the Song of the Three 29–34 ([Church of England, Common Worship: Daily Prayer, Canticle 50: Bless the Lord](https://www.churchofengland.org/liturgy/common-worship/daily-prayer/canticles-daily-prayer/50-bless-lord)), and "Benedicite – A Song of Creation", from verses 35–65 ([Church of England, Common Worship: Daily Prayer, Canticle 51a: Benedicite – A Song of Creation](https://www.churchofengland.org/liturgy/common-worship/daily-prayer/canticles-daily-prayer/51a-benedicite-song-creation)). In the **Orthodox** Church, "The Seventh and Eighth Odes of the nine Biblical Odes at the back of the Psalter are taken from The Song of the Three Holy Youths (found in the Septuagint text of the Old Testament used by the Orthodox Church)" ([Orthodox Church in America, The Three Holy Youths: Ananias, Azarias and Misael](https://www.oca.org/saints/lives/2026/12/17/103560-the-three-holy-youths-ananias-azarias-and-misael)), and on Holy Saturday, at Vespers with the Divine Liturgy of Saint Basil, "the Canticles of Moses, and of the Three Youths found in Daniel are chanted" with the fifteen Old Testament readings ([Orthodox Church in America, Holy Saturday](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/worship/the-church-year/holy-saturday)).
- `7` [Hippolytus, Exegetical Fragments](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0502.htm) (New Advent): **Susanna and the song in the early church.** The church father Hippolytus of Rome read Susanna as a figure: "Susannah prefigured the Church; and Joacim, her husband, Christ; and the garden, the calling of the saints, who are planted like fruitful trees in the Church". Her washing in the garden with two maids he read of baptism: "when the Church desires to take the laver according to use, she must of necessity have two handmaids to accompany her", faith in Christ and love of God. On the song of the three he wrote that the young men "enumerated all created things, so that not one of them might be reckoned free and independent in itself", and "showed them to be all the servants of God, who created all things by the Word". In the Catacomb of Priscilla in Rome, the frescoes of the "Greek Chapel", from the time of the emperor Gallienus (253–268), are "decorated with the stories of Susanna, the adoration of the Magi and a well-known representation of a banquet" ([Catacombe di Priscilla, Guided tour](https://catacombepriscilla.com/en/home-en/)).
- `7` [Munteanu, Canoniques, non-canoniques ou bons à lire ?](https://www.diacronia.ro/ro/indexing/details/A25808/pdf) (Diacronia, in French): **the additions in the Orthodox Bible.** Ştefan Munteanu writes that Orthodox Bibles include the Greek additions to Daniel: the Prayer of Azariah and the Song of the Three, the History of Susanna, and Bel and the Dragon. In the ninth century Patriarch Nicephorus of Constantinople still listed Susanna among the Old Testament books whose place was disputed, while in 1672 the Synod of Jerusalem approved the Confession of Patriarch Dositheus, which counts Bel and the Dragon and the History of Susanna, with Wisdom, Judith, Tobit, the Maccabees and Sirach, as genuine parts of Scripture. He notes that the Orthodox Church keeps the feast of the prophet Daniel and his three companions on 17 December. The Orthodox Church in America teaches that the song of the three "forms a genuine part of the Bible in the Orthodox Church, as do the books of Susanna and Bel and the Dragon, also part of Daniel" ([Orthodox Church in America, The Orthodox Faith: Old Testament, Prophets](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/doctrine-scripture/old-testament/prophets)).
- `7` [Jerome, Prefaces to the Vulgate Old Testament: Daniel](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/schaff/npnf206.vii.iii.xiii.html) (Christian Classics Ethereal Library): **Jerome's Latin Daniel.** Jerome explained that the book of Daniel "in Hebrew contains neither the history of Susanna, nor the hymn of the three youths, nor the fables of Bel and the Dragon; because, however, they are to be found everywhere, we have formed them into an appendix, prefixing to them an obelus, and thus making an end of them, so as not to seem to the uninformed to have cut off a large portion of the volume". He reported a Jewish teacher's objections to the stories and left them "for the reader to pronounce upon as he may think fit". The 1908 Catholic Encyclopedia answers that "although the deuterocanonical portions of Daniel seem to contain anachronisms, they should not be treated — as was done by St Jerome — as mere fables" ([New Advent, Catholic Encyclopedia (1908), Daniel, Book of](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/04621b.htm)).
- `7` [Church of England, Articles of Religion, VI](https://www.churchofengland.org/liturgy/book-of-common-prayer/articles-religion#VI): **read, but not for doctrine.** The **Church of England** counts the "Four Prophets the greater", Daniel among them, as canonical, and "The Song of the Three Children", "The Story of Susanna" and "Of Bel and the Dragon" among the books read "for example of life and instruction of manners". The **Reformed** commentator Matthew Henry writes that "The fables of Susannah, and of Bel and the Dragon, in both which Daniel is made a party, are apocryphal stories, which we think we have no reason to give any credit to, they being never found in the Hebrew or Chaldee, but only in the Greek, nor ever admitted by the Jewish church" ([Matthew Henry, Commentary on the Whole Bible, Daniel: Introduction](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/henry/mhc4.Dan.i.html)), and the Westminster Confession holds that the books called Apocrypha "are no part of the canon of the Scripture" ([Westminster Confession of Faith, 1.3](https://www.opc.org/wcf.html)).
- `7` [Rösel, Luther und die Apokryphen](https://www.die-bibel.de/luther-und-die-apokryphen) (Deutsche Bibelgesellschaft, in German): **the additions in the Luther Bible.** Martin Rösel writes that Luther took the additions to Esther and Daniel into his Bible among the Apocrypha, which it printed between the Testaments, and that some of these books were much loved in the church, among them the story of Susanna, an addition to the book of Daniel, as an example of a life pleasing to God.
- `6` [USCCB, Readings for Monday of the Fifth Week of Lent (23 March 2026)](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/readings/032326.cfm): **Susanna and the woman caught in adultery.** The **Catholic** Mass reads the story of Susanna, [Daniel (Greek) 13:1–9](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/DAG13.htm#V1), [Daniel (Greek) 13:15–17](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/DAG13.htm#V15), [Daniel (Greek) 13:19–30](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/DAG13.htm#V19), [Daniel (Greek) 13:33–62](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/DAG13.htm#V33), on Monday of the fifth week of Lent, with the gospel of the woman brought to Jesus for judgement, [John 8:1–11](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JHN08.htm#V1) (see [John](../new-testament/john.md)). The NRSVUE's cross-references link Daniel's cry, "I am clear from the blood of this woman!" ([Daniel (Greek) 13:46](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/DAG13.htm#V46)), to Pilate's words in [Matthew 27:24](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MAT27.htm#V24) ([Bible Gateway, Susanna 1 (NRSVUE)](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Susanna%201&version=NRSVUE); see [Matthew](../new-testament/matthew.md)).

## Open questions

Questions on which the churches or scholars disagree. These guidelines don't pick a side. Questions about the Hebrew and Aramaic book, such as when it was written and who "one like a son of man" is, are on the [Daniel](../old-testament/daniel.md#open-questions) page.

- `7` [Africanus, Letter to Origen](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0413.htm) (New Advent): **were the additions part of Daniel, or later stories?** In the third century Julius Africanus wrote to Origen that Susanna, "although apart from this it is elegantly written, is plainly a more modern forgery": the elders' punishments rest on puns that work in Greek but not in Hebrew, and "this section, along with the other two at the end of it, is not contained in the Daniel received among the Jews". Origen replied that the History of Susanna "is found in every Church of Christ in that Greek copy which the Greeks use, but is not in the Hebrew", that both the Septuagint and Theodotion's version contain it "together with the passages at the end of Daniel", and asked Africanus to consider whether it would not be well to "remember the words, You shall not remove the ancient landmarks which your fathers have set" ([Origen, Letter to Africanus](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0414.htm)). The New American Bible's notes hold that the stories "probably were first composed in Hebrew or Aramaic" but "were never part of the Hebrew-Aramaic Book of Daniel" ([USCCB, Daniel, chapter 13, with notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/daniel/13)); the 1908 Catholic Encyclopedia found "the greater probability" to be "in favour of a Hebrew original no longer extant", and ascribed all three to "some unknown Jewish author who lived long after the Exile" ([New Advent, Catholic Encyclopedia (1908), Daniel, Book of](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/04621b.htm)). For Susanna, R. Timothy McLay finds that "It appears that Sousanna was revised and enlarged in its Semitic form in order to enhance the role of Daniel" ([NETS, Sousanna, translated by R. Timothy McLay](https://ccat.sas.upenn.edu/nets/edition/39-sousanna-nets.pdf)).
- `7` [USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Daniel](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/daniel/0): **history, or stories that teach?** The USCCB introduction calls Susanna and Bel and the Dragon "edifying short stories with a didactic purpose", and its notes find that Bel and the Dragon "preserves the fiction of a successive Median and Persian rule of Babylon" and gives "a different account from chap. 6 as to why Daniel was associated with the lions' den" ([USCCB, Daniel, chapter 14, with notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/daniel/14); [Daniel 6:16–24](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/DAN06.htm#V16)). Origen defended the story of Susanna as one that could be true ([Origen, Letter to Africanus](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0414.htm)); Jerome spoke of "the fables of Bel and the Dragon" ([Jerome, Prefaces to the Vulgate Old Testament: Daniel](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/schaff/npnf206.vii.iii.xiii.html)); the 1908 Catholic Encyclopedia held that they "embody oral or written traditions not altogether devoid of historical value" ([New Advent, Catholic Encyclopedia (1908), Daniel, Book of](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/04621b.htm)); and Matthew Henry that "we have no reason to give any credit to" them ([Matthew Henry, Commentary on the Whole Bible, Daniel: Introduction](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/henry/mhc4.Dan.i.html)).
- `7` [NETS, Daniel, translated by R. Timothy McLay](https://ccat.sas.upenn.edu/nets/edition/40-daniel-nets.pdf) (International Organization for Septuagint and Cognate Studies): **which Greek text?** McLay finds that in chapters 4–6 "it is clear that TH is not a revision of the OG", that in chapters 1–3 "one might better label OG and TH independent translations", and that in chapters 7–12 the passages where the two agree closely "give the appearance that the TH text is a revision of the OG". In Susanna the Old Greek "is significantly shorter than TH", and the two versions "have distinct endings" ([NETS, Sousanna, translated by R. Timothy McLay](https://ccat.sas.upenn.edu/nets/edition/39-sousanna-nets.pdf)); in Bel and the Dragon "there is evidence to suggest that the two Greek versions… are independent versions" ([NETS, Bel and the Dragon, translated by R. Timothy McLay](https://ccat.sas.upenn.edu/nets/edition/41-bel-nets.pdf)). Why the church came to read Theodotion's version is uncertain: Jerome wrote that "how this came to pass I cannot tell" ([Jerome, Prefaces to the Vulgate Old Testament: Daniel](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/schaff/npnf206.vii.iii.xiii.html)), while the 1908 Catholic Encyclopedia held that the Septuagint text of Daniel was thought "so unreliable" that "during the second century of our era, it was discarded by the Church" ([New Advent, Catholic Encyclopedia (1908), Daniel, Book of](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/04621b.htm)).
- `6` [Bible Odyssey, Daniel, Additions to (HarperCollins Bible Dictionary)](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/dictionary/daniel-additions-to/): **which text is Scripture: the Hebrew and Aramaic, the Greek, or both?** "Protestants have relegated the Additions to the Apocrypha, while Catholics retain them as part of Daniel." The Catholic Church receives the whole book, as it was read in the Latin Vulgate ([Council of Trent, Session IV](https://history.hanover.edu/texts/trent/ct04.html)); the Orthodox churches read the Greek book, and the Synod of Jerusalem of 1672 counted Susanna and Bel and the Dragon among the genuine parts of Scripture ([Munteanu, Canoniques, non-canoniques ou bons à lire ?](https://www.diacronia.ro/ro/indexing/details/A25808/pdf)); the Church of England and the Lutheran churches read the additions but do not base doctrine on them; and Reformed churches hold the Hebrew and Aramaic book alone to be canonical (see the entries above and [which books each church reads](../README.md#which-books-each-church-reads)).
