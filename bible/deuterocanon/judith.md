# Judith

The Book of Judith tells how a devout widow of the town of Bethulia saves her people from the army of "Nebuchadnezzar, who reigned over the Assyrians in Nineveh" ([Judith 1:1](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JDT01.htm#V1)). The USCCB introduction holds that "The Book of Judith relates the story of God's deliverance of the Jewish people. This was accomplished 'by the hand of a female'—a constant motif… meant to recall the 'hand' of God in the Exodus narrative", and notes that "Her Hebrew name means 'Jewish woman'" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Judith](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/judith/0)). The first half of the book (chapters 1–7) tells of the Assyrian campaign and the siege of Bethulia, and the second (chapters 8–16) of Judith's deed. The USCCB introduction notes that the story "telescopes five centuries of historical and geographical information with imaginary details": it speaks of Nineveh, "the Assyrian capital destroyed in 612 B.C.", of "Nebuchadnezzar, the ruler not of Assyria but of Babylon", and of a people "newly come up from the captivity" ([Judith 4:3](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JDT04.htm#V3)), and concludes that "the work is historical fiction, written to exalt God as Israel's deliverer from foreign might, not by an army, but by means of a simple widow". Whether the book is history or a story is one of the open questions below.

The USCCB introduction holds that "The work may have been written around 100 B.C.", and that "Despite Jerome's claim to have translated an Aramaic text, no ancient Aramaic or Hebrew manuscripts have been found. The oldest extant text of Judith is the preservation of 15:1–7 inscribed on a third-century A.D. potsherd." The NETS translator Cameron Boyd-Taylor holds that "the book of Ioudith is demonstrably the translation of a Semitic work, most likely Hebrew", whose "parent text vanished sometime in late antiquity" ([NETS, Ioudith, translated by Cameron Boyd-Taylor](https://ccat.sas.upenn.edu/nets/edition/18-ioudith-nets.pdf)). The book recalls the stories of Deborah and Jael, who killed the enemy general Sisera ([Judges 4:17–22](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JDG04.htm#V17); see the [Judges](../old-testament/judges.md) page), and the USCCB introduction finds it "filled with double entendres and ironic situations". Judith is not part of the Jewish Bible, but Denise Dombkowski Hopkins notes that "Jewish tradition connects Judith with the Maccabbean revolt and Chanukah" ([Bible Odyssey, Denise Dombkowski Hopkins, Judith](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/judith/)). Erica Brown explains that some commentators tie the Talmud's ruling that women must light the Hanukkah lights, because "They [women] were also in the miracle", to Judith's deed, and that "Women who light Hanukkah candles do so to re-live her courage" ([My Jewish Learning, Erica Brown, Yet Another Hanukkah Miracle](https://www.myjewishlearning.com/article/yet-another-miracle/)).

The churches disagree about the book's place in the Bible ([which books each church reads](../README.md#which-books-each-church-reads)). The **Catholic Church** counts Judith among the 46 books of its Old Testament ([Catechism of the Catholic Church, 120](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__PR.HTM)), as the Council of Trent's list of sacred and canonical books did in 1546 ([Council of Trent, Session IV](https://history.hanover.edu/texts/trent/ct04.html)). The **Orthodox** churches read it as one of the *anaginoskómena*, the "books to be read" ([Munteanu, Canoniques, non-canoniques ou bons à lire ?](https://www.diacronia.ro/ro/indexing/details/A25808/pdf)), and honour "the righteous Judith" among the ancestors of Christ ([Orthodox Church in America, Sunday before the Nativity](https://www.oca.org/saints/lives/2026/12/20/81-sunday-before-the-nativity)). The **Church of England** names "The Book of Judith" among the books that "the Church doth read for example of life and instruction of manners; but yet doth it not apply them to establish any doctrine" ([Church of England, Articles of Religion, VI](https://www.churchofengland.org/liturgy/book-of-common-prayer/articles-religion#VI)). **Luther**'s Bible printed it among the Apocrypha, books "useful and good to read" ([Rösel, Luther und die Apokryphen](https://www.die-bibel.de/luther-und-die-apokryphen)). The **Reformed** Westminster Confession holds that the books called Apocrypha "are no part of the canon of the Scripture" and are not to be used otherwise "than other human writings" ([Westminster Confession of Faith, 1.3](https://www.opc.org/wcf.html)). The USCCB introduction sums up the history: "the rabbis did not count Judith among their scriptures, and the Reformation adopted that position. The early Church, however, held this book in high honor." See also the [Wisdom of Solomon](wisdom-of-solomon.md) page.

## How the churches read it

Where the churches, or their interpreters, read the book differently, or where it has played a part in their history. Each entry starts with its source's score.

- `7` [Vatican, John Paul II, General Audience of 29 August 2001: The Canticle of Judith](https://www.vatican.va/content/john-paul-ii/en/audiences/2001/documents/hf_jp-ii_aud_20010829.html): **Judith and Mary in Catholic teaching and prayer.** The **Catholic** Church prays part of Judith's song at Lauds, its morning prayer, and John Paul II noted that "The Liturgy of Lauds gives us only a few verses to recite". He taught that some of the book's words "will pass, more or less integrally into Christian tradition which sees in the Jewish heroine a prefiguration of Mary", heard an echo of Judith in the Magnificat ([Luke 1:52](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/LUK01.htm#V52)), and explained why "the liturgical tradition common to Christians of the East and of the West loves to ascribe to Mary the Mother of Jesus, the praise given to Judith". On the feast of Our Lady of Guadalupe the psalm after the first reading is taken from [Judith 13:18–19](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JDT13.htm#V18), with the response "You are the highest honor of our race" from 15:9 ([USCCB, Readings for the Feast of Our Lady of Guadalupe (12 December 2025)](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/readings/121225.cfm)). The Catechism names Judith among the "holy women" who "kept alive the hope of Israel's salvation", and adds: "the purest figure among them is Mary" ([Catechism of the Catholic Church, 54–64](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__PG.HTM)).
- `7` [Clement of Rome, Letter to the Corinthians, 55](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/1010.htm) (New Advent): **the book in the early church.** Clement of Rome, whom the USCCB introduction calls "The first-century Pope", held Judith up as an example of courage ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Judith](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/judith/0)): "The blessed Judith, when her city was besieged… went out for the love which she bare to her country and people then besieged; and the Lord delivered Holofernes into the hands of a woman." Jerome called her "a type of the Church which cuts off the head of the devil" ([Jerome, Letter 79: To Salvina, 10](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/3001079.htm)), wrote that in her story "a woman vanquishes men, and chastity beheads lust", though he introduced the book with "if any one is of opinion that it should be received as canonical" ([Jerome, Letter 54: To Furia, 16](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/3001054.htm)), and, as the USCCB introduction notes, described Mary as a new Judith: when the Son of God came, "chaste Judith once more cut off the head of Holofernes" ([Jerome, Letter 22: To Eustochium, 21](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/3001022.htm)). Athanasius counted Judith among the books "not indeed included in the Canon, but appointed by the Fathers to be read" ([Athanasius, Letter 39](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/2806039.htm)). The editors of Jerome's prefaces report that he translated the book because the Council of Nicaea, "contrary to the Hebrew tradition", had included it in the canon ([Jerome, Prefaces to the Vulgate Old Testament: Tobit and Judith](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/schaff/npnf206.vii.iii.xx.html)), and the 1910 Catholic Encyclopedia held that "St. Paul seems to quote the Greek text of Judith 8:14, in 1 Corinthians 2:10" ([Judith 8:14](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JDT08.htm#V14), [1 Corinthians 2:10](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1CO02.htm#V10); [New Advent, Catholic Encyclopedia (1910), Book of Judith](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/08554a.htm)).
- `7` [Orthodox Church in America, Sunday before the Nativity](https://www.oca.org/saints/lives/2026/12/20/81-sunday-before-the-nativity): **Judith among the ancestors of Christ.** On the Sunday before the Nativity the **Orthodox** Church commemorates the ancestors of Christ, among them "the righteous Judith, slayer of Holofernes", with Ruth, Esther and other holy women. On the Sunday of the Forefathers the Orthodox Church in America lists "Righteous Judith" among the saints of the day, noting that "Information about the Righteous Judith may be found in the Old Testament book bearing her name" ([Orthodox Church in America, Lives of all saints commemorated on December 13 (2026, Sunday of the Forefathers)](https://www.oca.org/saints/all-lives/2026/12/13)).
- `7` [Church of England, Common Worship: Daily Prayer, Canticle 45: A Song of Judith](https://www.churchofengland.org/liturgy/common-worship/daily-prayer/canticles-daily-prayer/45-song-judith): **Judith's song in Anglican prayer.** The **Church of England**, which reads the book "for example of life" but not for doctrine ([Church of England, Articles of Religion, VI](https://www.churchofengland.org/liturgy/book-of-common-prayer/articles-religion#VI)), turns [Judith 16:13–16](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JDT16.htm#V13) into a canticle for daily prayer, with the refrain "You sent forth your Spirit, O God, and you continue to show mercy to all". The Revised Common Lectionary, which many Protestant churches use, appoints no Sunday reading from Judith ([Vanderbilt Divinity Library, Revised Common Lectionary: Sunday citations](https://lectionary.library.vanderbilt.edu/sunday-citations/)).
- `6` [Eva Guldanová, Martin Luther a starozmluvné apokryfy](https://fevth.uniba.sk/fileadmin/ebf/testimonia_theologica/2019/Martin_Luther_a_starozmluvne_apokryfy.pdf) (Testimonia Theologica, 2019, in Slovak): **Luther's preface to Judith.** Guldanová sets out what **Luther** wrote in his 1534 preface to the book, as printed in Luther's Works, volume 35. Luther held that if the events could be proved from reliable histories, Judith would be a noble book that belonged in the Bible, but that it hardly agrees with the historical accounts of Scripture, above all Jeremiah and Ezra. He excused its errors by reading it as a religious fiction or play, whose author preaches through its characters as a holy poet or prophet, saw Judith as a figure of the Jewish people kept pure and holy in God's word, true faith and prayer, and judged it a fine, good, holy and useful book, well worth reading by Christians.
- `6` [Bible Odyssey, Diane Apostolos-Cappadona, Judith in Art](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/judith-in-art/): **Judith in Christian art and devotion.** Apostolos-Cappadona notes that "Medieval Christian artists celebrated her as a symbol of pious widowhood", and that "Following the pattern of early Christian theologians, Rabanus's ninth-century commentary on the book of Judith built up her image as a Marian prototype". After the Reformation, "Judith and 'her book' had no formal place in Lutheran and Reformed theology but fulfilled an ambiguous cultural role as a female worthy or seductress", while painters of southern Europe such as Caravaggio and Artemisia Gentileschi showed her killing Holofernes "as a sign of the Church triumphing over the heresy of the Reformers".

## Open questions

Questions on which the churches or scholars disagree, for example about the book's authorship or the meaning of a passage. These guidelines don't pick a side.

- `7` [Bible Odyssey, Denise Dombkowski Hopkins, Judith](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/judith/): **history or story?** The book places Nebuchadnezzar in Nineveh and has its people "newly come up from the captivity" ([Judith 1:1](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JDT01.htm#V1), [Judith 4:3](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JDT04.htm#V3)). Hopkins holds that "Deliberate historical inaccuracies, common to Jewish novels of the time, signal that Judith's story is fiction", and the USCCB introduction calls the book "historical fiction" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Judith](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/judith/0)). The 1910 Catholic Encyclopedia reported that "Catholics with very few exceptions accept the book of Judith as a narrative of facts, not as an allegory", and set out ways of harmonising its difficulties, for example by blaming them on copyists or by identifying its Nebuchadnezzar with the Assyrian king Ashurbanipal, while noting that "Some few Catholic writers" held only that the book "has a solid historical foundation", and that the story was written down "at a period long subsequent to the facts" ([New Advent, Catholic Encyclopedia (1910), Book of Judith](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/08554a.htm)). Luther, according to Guldanová, held that the book hardly agrees with the history in Jeremiah and Ezra, and read it as a religious fiction or play ([Eva Guldanová, Martin Luther a starozmluvné apokryfy](https://fevth.uniba.sk/fileadmin/ebf/testimonia_theologica/2019/Martin_Luther_a_starozmluvne_apokryfy.pdf)).
- `7` [Thomas Aquinas, Summa Theologiae, II-II, Question 110, Article 3](https://www.newadvent.org/summa/3110.htm#article3) (New Advent): **may Judith's lies and her killing of Holofernes be praised?** Judith asks God to strike the enemy "by the deceit of my lips" and deceives Holofernes ([Judith 9:10](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JDT09.htm#V10), [Judith 11:5](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JDT11.htm#V5)). Thomas Aquinas, who held every lie to be a sin, answered that "Judith is praised, not for lying to Holofernes, but for her desire to save the people, to which end she exposed herself to danger. And yet one might also say that her words contain truth in some mystical sense." Clement of Rome praised her for going out "for the love which she bare to her country" ([Clement of Rome, Letter to the Corinthians, 55](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/1010.htm)). Among modern readers, Hopkins notes that "Critics censure Judith for her lies, for acting like a man, and for using her beauty as a weapon to seduce Holofernes. Supporters see Judith as a brave liberator and independent woman", and that she "joins the ranks of other women who lie for the survival of Israel: Rebekah, Tamar, the midwives Shiphrah and Puah, Rahab, and Esther" ([Bible Odyssey, Denise Dombkowski Hopkins, Judith](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/judith/)).
- `7` [USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Judith](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/judith/0) (New American Bible, Revised Edition): **when was it written, in what language, and why is it not in the Jewish Bible?** The USCCB introduction suggests "around 100 B.C.", and notes that "there seem to be allusions to the second-century Seleucid ruler Antiochus IV Epiphanes". Hopkins suggests the time of the Maccabean revolt "or later during the reign of the Hasmonean Queen Salome Alexandra (76–67 B.C.E.), with whom Judith shares many similarities" ([Bible Odyssey, Denise Dombkowski Hopkins, Judith](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/judith/)). Jerome said he translated the book from Aramaic, but the USCCB introduction notes that no ancient Aramaic or Hebrew manuscript has been found, and the NETS translator holds that its parent was "most likely Hebrew" ([NETS, Ioudith, translated by Cameron Boyd-Taylor](https://ccat.sas.upenn.edu/nets/edition/18-ioudith-nets.pdf)); the 1910 Catholic Encyclopedia describes a short medieval Hebrew version in which the enemy is Seleucus and the city attacked is Jerusalem ([New Advent, Catholic Encyclopedia (1910), Book of Judith](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/08554a.htm)). On why the rabbis did not count the book among their Scriptures, Hopkins suggests that it may have been "produced too late to achieve canonical status", that Achior's conversion and the placing of Bethulia in Samaritan territory may have told against it, and that "Ultimately, Judith may have simply been too radical or sexy for her time."

- `6` [BibleProject, Judith (overview video)](https://bibleproject.com/videos/judith/): an overview video of the book, in BibleProject's series on the deuterocanon, which "tells the story of an Israelite widow whose radical faith topples an empire".
- `6` [New Advent, Catholic Encyclopedia (1910), Book of Judith](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/08554a.htm) (Hugh Pope): an older Catholic introduction to the book's story, its Greek, Latin and Hebrew texts, the debate about its history, and its place in the canon.
