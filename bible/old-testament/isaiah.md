# Isaiah

The Book of Isaiah gathers the prophecies of "Isaiah the son of Amoz", which he saw "concerning Judah and Jerusalem, in the days of Uzziah, Jotham, Ahaz, and Hezekiah, kings of Judah" ([Isaiah 1:1](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ISA01.htm#V1)). The USCCB introduction calls Isaiah "one of the greatest of the prophets", who "appeared at a critical moment in Israel's history", when Assyria destroyed the northern kingdom and besieged Jerusalem. His call came in a vision of the LORD in the temple, in the year King Uzziah died ([Isaiah 6:1–8](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ISA06.htm#V1)), and the introduction holds that "The vision of the Lord enthroned in glory stamps an indelible character on Isaiah's ministry and provides a key to the understanding of his message"; it led him to call his people "back to a reliance on God's promises" and "to insist on the ethical behavior that was required of human beings who wished to live in the presence of such a holy God" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Isaiah](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/isaiah/0)). The book moves from judgement on Judah and the nations to comfort for the exiles in Babylon and visions of a new Jerusalem; it names the Persian king Cyrus, who conquered Babylon, as the LORD's "anointed" ([Isaiah 44:28–45:1](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ISA44.htm#V28)).

Who wrote the book is one of the open questions below. Christian and Jewish tradition read it as the work of the prophet: the Babylonian Talmud records that "Hezekiah and his colleagues wrote" Isaiah, with Proverbs, the Song of Songs and Ecclesiastes ([Sefaria, Bava Batra 15a:2](https://www.sefaria.org/Bava_Batra.15a.2)), and the ESV Global Study Bible dates Isaiah's call "around 740 B.C." and reads chapters 40–55 as "Prophecies for the sixth century B.C." ([Crossway, ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to Isaiah](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-isaiah/)). Today, Orthodox teaching notes, "Most scholars believe that the book of Isaiah is the work of more than one author" ([Orthodox Church in America, The Orthodox Faith: Old Testament, Prophets](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/doctrine-scripture/old-testament/prophets)), and the USCCB introduction calls the book "an anthology of poems composed chiefly by the great prophet, but also by disciples, some of whom came many years after Isaiah": chapters 40–55, Second Isaiah, "are generally attributed to an anonymous poet who prophesied toward the end of the Babylonian exile", and chapters 56–66, Third Isaiah, "contain oracles from the postexilic period" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Isaiah](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/isaiah/0)). The Great Isaiah Scroll from Qumran, "the only one that is almost complete" of the biblical scrolls, contains "all 66 chapters" and dates "from ca. 125 BCE" ([Israel Museum, The Digital Dead Sea Scrolls: The Great Isaiah Scroll](http://dss.collections.imj.org.il/isaiah)).

Isaiah is Jewish Scripture: "Isaiah ('Yeshayahu') is the fifth book of the Prophets and is known for its visions of universal peace and renewal" ([Sefaria, Isaiah](https://www.sefaria.org/Isaiah)), and "more synagogue readings come from Isaiah than any other biblical book outside of the Torah" ([J. Blake Couey, Isaiah, Bible Odyssey](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/isaiah/)). In Christian Bibles it is the first of the major prophets, with [Jeremiah](jeremiah.md), [Lamentations](lamentations.md), [Ezekiel](ezekiel.md) and [Daniel](daniel.md) ([Orthodox Church in America, The Orthodox Faith: Old Testament, Prophets](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/doctrine-scripture/old-testament/prophets)), and it "is among the texts from the Hebrew Bible most frequently quoted or alluded to in the New Testament, especially in association with the birth, life, and death of Jesus" ([J. Blake Couey, Isaiah, Bible Odyssey](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/isaiah/)). Every church reads it.

## How the churches read it

Where the churches, or their interpreters, read the book differently, or where it has played a part in their history. Each entry starts with its source's score.

- `8` [English Language Liturgical Consultation, Sanctus and Benedictus](https://www.englishtexts.org/sanctus-and-benedictus): **"Holy, holy, holy" in the Eucharist.** The seraphim's song ([Isaiah 6:3](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ISA06.htm#V3)) became the Sanctus of the eucharistic prayer. The agreed ecumenical English text reads "Holy, holy, holy Lord, God of power and might, heaven and earth are full of your glory", and its notes explain that the Sanctus "first entered the eucharistic prayer in the East" and "spread rapidly in both the East and the West", from "the thought that the worshipers who had lifted their hearts to the Lord were sharing in the worship of heaven"; it is "based upon Scripture, but not exactly conforming to the texts of Isaiah 6:3". In the **Orthodox** Divine Liturgy the people sing "Holy! Holy! Holy! Lord of Sabaoth! Heaven and earth are full of Thy glory!" ([Orthodox Church in America, The Orthodox Faith: Eucharistic Canon: Anaphora](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/worship/the-divine-liturgy/eucharistic-canon-anaphora)), and *The Orthodox Faith* notes that "The famous vision of the prophet in chapter six is included in the eucharistic prayers of the Orthodox Church" ([Orthodox Church in America, The Orthodox Faith: Old Testament, Prophets](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/doctrine-scripture/old-testament/prophets)). The living creatures of Revelation sing the same words ([Revelation 4:8](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/REV04.htm#V8)).
- `7` [Vatican, Catechism of the Catholic Church, 595–623](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P1O.HTM): **the suffering servant and the passion of Christ.** The New Testament reads the servant of [Isaiah 52:13–53:12](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ISA52.htm#V13) as Jesus: Philip, asked by the Ethiopian official "Who is the prophet talking about?", "beginning from this Scripture, preached to him about Jesus" ([Acts 8:34–35](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ACT08.htm#V34); see [Acts](../new-testament/acts.md)), and 1 Peter writes, "You were healed by his wounds" ([1 Peter 2:22–25](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1PE02.htm#V22)). The **Catholic** Catechism teaches that "Jesus' redemptive death fulfils Isaiah's prophecy of the suffering Servant", and that "Jesus himself explained the meaning of his life and death in the light of God's suffering Servant" (601); by his obedience unto death "Jesus accomplished the substitution of the suffering Servant, who 'makes himself an offering for sin'" (615). The **Reformed** commentator John Calvin reads the servant as Christ throughout, and writes on [Isaiah 53:5](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ISA53.htm#V5) that "all offense is removed when we know that by his death our sins have been expiated, and salvation has been obtained for us" ([John Calvin, Commentary on Isaiah 53:1–12](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/calvin/calcom16.vi.i.html)). **Orthodox** teaching names, as of central importance in Isaiah, "the prophecies at the end of the book, about the salvation of all creation in the suffering servant of the Lord" ([Orthodox Church in America, The Orthodox Faith: Old Testament, Prophets](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/doctrine-scripture/old-testament/prophets)). Whether the servant first meant Israel is one of the open questions below.
- `7` [Vatican, Catechism of the Catholic Church, 484–511](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P1K.HTM): **Immanuel and the virgin birth.** Matthew writes that Jesus' birth fulfilled "Behold, the virgin shall be with child, and shall give birth to a son. They shall call his name Immanuel" ([Matthew 1:22–23](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MAT01.htm#V22); see [Matthew](../new-testament/matthew.md)). The **Catholic** Catechism teaches that in the virginal conception of Jesus "The Church sees here the fulfilment of the divine promise given through the prophet Isaiah: 'Behold, a virgin shall conceive and bear a son'" (497). Calvin holds that "this name Immanuel could not be literally applied to a mere man; and, therefore, there can be no doubt that the Prophet referred to Christ", and asks, "what wonderful thing did the Prophet say, if he spoke of a young woman who conceived through intercourse with a man?" ([John Calvin, Commentary on Isaiah 7:1–25](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/calvin/calcom13.xiv.i.html)). In the **Orthodox** Church, [Isaiah 7:10–16](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ISA07.htm#V10), [Isaiah 9:6–7](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ISA09.htm#V6) and [Isaiah 11:1–10](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ISA11.htm#V1) are among the readings on the eve of the Nativity of Christ ([Orthodox Church in America, Scripture Readings for 24 December 2026 (Eve of the Nativity of Christ)](https://www.oca.org/readings/daily/2026/12/24)). How the verse should be translated is one of the open questions below.
- `7` [Vatican, Catechism of the Catholic Church, 702–716](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P22.HTM): **the Messiah and the Spirit.** The **Catholic** Catechism teaches that "The characteristics of the awaited Messiah begin to appear in the 'Book of Emmanuel'", "especially in the first two verses of Isaiah 11" ([Isaiah 11:1–2](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ISA11.htm#V1)), that "The Messiah's characteristics are revealed above all in the 'Servant songs'" (712–713), and that Christ began his preaching by making his own the words of [Isaiah 61:1–2](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ISA61.htm#V1) (714). With a reference to Isaiah 11, it teaches that "The seven gifts of the Holy Spirit are wisdom, understanding, counsel, fortitude, knowledge, piety, and fear of the Lord", which "belong in their fullness to Christ, Son of David" ([Vatican, Catechism of the Catholic Church, 1830–1832](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P67.HTM)). The ESV Global Study Bible, an evangelical study Bible, likewise holds that in Isaiah "The only hope of the world is in one man": "the promised Davidic king", "the servant of the Lord", "the anointed preacher of the gospel" ([Crossway, ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to Isaiah](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-isaiah/)).
- `7` [Vatican, Catechism of the Catholic Church, 131–133](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__PS.HTM): **an evangelist more than a prophet.** The Catechism takes from Jerome's commentary on Isaiah the sentence "Ignorance of the Scriptures is ignorance of Christ" (133). In the preface to his translation of Isaiah, Jerome speaks of the prophet "as an Evangelist more than a prophet", and the preface to his commentary asserts "that many of the prophecies are directly applicable to Christ" (as summarised in the *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers*: [Christian Classics Ethereal Library, Jerome, Prefaces to the Vulgate Old Testament: Isaiah](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/schaff/npnf206.vii.iii.xi.html) and [Christian Classics Ethereal Library, Jerome, Prefaces to the Commentaries: Isaiah](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/schaff/npnf206.vii.iv.viii.html)). John's gospel says that "Isaiah said these things when he saw his glory, and spoke of him" ([John 12:41](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JHN12.htm#V41)).
- `7` [Orthodox Church in America, The Orthodox Faith: Old Testament, Prophets](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/doctrine-scripture/old-testament/prophets): **Isaiah in the church year.** In the **Orthodox** Church "The entire book of Isaiah is read in the Church during Great Lent, and many selections are read at the vigils of the great feasts of the Church". In the **Catholic** Mass of Good Friday the first reading is the fourth servant song, [Isaiah 52:13–53:12](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ISA52.htm#V13) ([USCCB, Readings for Good Friday of the Lord's Passion (3 April 2026)](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/readings/040326.cfm)), and the Revised Common Lectionary, used by many Protestant churches, reads the four servant songs from Monday of Holy Week to Good Friday ([Vanderbilt Divinity Library, Revised Common Lectionary: Holy Week 2026](https://lectionary.library.vanderbilt.edu/calendar/2025-26/?season=holy_week)). The same lectionary reads Isaiah on the first three Sundays of Advent in 2026 ([Vanderbilt Divinity Library, Revised Common Lectionary: Advent 2026](https://lectionary.library.vanderbilt.edu/calendar/2026-27/?season=advent)) and in each of its three sets of readings for Christmas, the first of which begins with "The people who walked in darkness have seen a great light" ([Isaiah 9:2–7](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ISA09.htm#V2); [Vanderbilt Divinity Library, Revised Common Lectionary: Christmas 2026](https://lectionary.library.vanderbilt.edu/calendar/2026-27/?season=christmas)).

## Open questions

Questions on which the churches or scholars disagree. These guidelines don't pick a side.

- `7` [H. G. M. Williamson, How Many Isaiahs Were There?](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/how-many-isaiahs-were-there/) (Bible Odyssey): **one prophet or several?** Williamson writes that "Readers of the Bible used to think that because the events in the second half of the book of Isaiah were predictions, they were all written down by the prophet Isaiah many years in advance", but that the book "refers to historical events that are at least 200 years apart", and from chapter 40 the writer "speaks as though he is right there with his readers". Some answer "three" Isaiahs, "but it is not as simple as that. There seem to be many more contributors to this great book": "Isaiah is therefore like a tapestry, with many hands contributing to a greater unity". Christine Hayes calls it "a very strong consensus" among scholars that chapters 40–66 are "not the work of Isaiah of Jerusalem" ([Christine Hayes, Introduction to the Old Testament, Lecture 17, Open Yale Courses](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-17)), and the USCCB introduction attributes chapters 40–55 to "an anonymous poet" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Isaiah](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/isaiah/0)). In 1908 the Pontifical Biblical Commission held that "There are no solid arguments … to prove that the book of Isaias is to be attributed not to Isaias himself alone, but to two or rather to many authors", as the 1910 *Catholic Encyclopedia* reported ([Catholic Encyclopedia (1910), Isaias](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/08179b.htm)); the ESV Global Study Bible reads chapters 40–55 as prophecies by Isaiah "for the sixth century B.C." ([Crossway, ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to Isaiah](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-isaiah/)).
- `7` [Christine Hayes, Introduction to the Old Testament, Lecture 19, Open Yale Courses](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-19): **who is the suffering servant?** Hayes notes that "the identity of the servant has been a puzzle to biblical interpreters for centuries": "Sometimes the servant is referred to as a collective figure, sometimes the servant is referred to as an individual figure", and the servant is named "Israel" in [Isaiah 49:3](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ISA49.htm#V3) but sent to Israel in [Isaiah 49:5–6](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ISA49.htm#V5). In its original context, she holds, "it appears most likely that the servant is Israel herself described metaphorically as an individual", though "This has never been solved satisfactorily". Jewish tradition reads the servant as Israel: Rashi comments that "he mentions all Israel as one man", and that "Behold My servant shall prosper" was "said concerning the house of Jacob" ([Sefaria, Rashi on Isaiah 53:3](https://www.sefaria.org/Rashi_on_Isaiah.53.3)). The New American Bible notes that "The servant is thus both identified with Israel and distinguished from it", and that "this chapter helped the followers of Jesus to interpret his suffering, death, and resurrection" ([USCCB, Isaiah, chapter 52, with notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/isaiah/52)). Elizabeth Shively notes that "Christians traditionally claim that the suffering, death, and resurrection of Jesus is prefigured in Isaiah's Suffering Servant", an interpretation that "originates in the New Testament" and is "based on a tradition of interpretation stretching back to Isaiah itself" ([Elizabeth E. Shively, The Suffering Servant and the New Testament, Bible Odyssey](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/the-suffering-servant-and-the-new-testament/)); the churches read the servant as Christ (see above). The Ethiopian official in Acts asks the same question: "Who is the prophet talking about? About himself, or about someone else?" ([Acts 8:34–35](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ACT08.htm#V34)).
- `7` [USCCB, Isaiah, chapter 7, with notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/isaiah/7) (New American Bible, Revised Edition): **"virgin" or "young woman" in 7:14?** The WEBBE translates "Behold, the virgin will conceive" ([Isaiah 7:14](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ISA07.htm#V14)); the New American Bible translates "the young woman, pregnant and about to bear a son, shall name him Emmanuel", and notes that "Hebrew 'almah designates a young woman of marriageable age without specific reference to virginity. The Septuagint translated the Hebrew term as parthenos, which normally does mean virgin, and this translation underlies Mt 1:23", and that "Since for the Christian the incarnation is the ultimate expression of God's willingness to 'be with us,' it is understandable that this text was interpreted to refer to the birth of Christ". Joel Hoffman holds that "The original Hebrew text of Isa 7:14 is not about a virgin" ([Joel M. Hoffman, Was There Really a Virgin Birth in the Bible?, Bible Odyssey](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/was-there-really-a-virgin-birth-in-the-bible/)). Who the mother was "is a matter of some dispute": "some scholars take the verse as a reference to Isaiah's own wife", others to the king's wife and the birth of Hezekiah ([Christine Hayes, Introduction to the Old Testament, Lecture 17, Open Yale Courses](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-17)); the Jewish commentator Rashi reads "the young woman" as "My wife will conceive this year" ([Sefaria, Rashi on Isaiah 7:14](https://www.sefaria.org/Rashi_on_Isaiah.7.14); compare [Isaiah 8:3](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ISA08.htm#V3)). The churches read the verse as fulfilled in the virginal conception of Jesus, as Matthew does, and Calvin argues that a sign must be something wonderful (see above).

## Tutorials and further reading

- `7` [Christine Hayes, Literary Prophecy: Hosea and Isaiah](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-17) (Open Yale Courses, Introduction to the Old Testament, lecture 17): a university lecture, with a transcript, whose second half reads First Isaiah: Isaiah's call, the sign of Immanuel and the hope for a king from David's line.
- `7` [Christine Hayes, Literary Prophecy: Perspectives on the Exile (Jeremiah, Ezekiel and 2nd Isaiah)](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-19) (Open Yale Courses, Introduction to the Old Testament, lecture 19): the last part of the lecture reads Second Isaiah, its message of comfort and its servant songs.
- `7` [J. Blake Couey, Isaiah](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/isaiah/) (Bible Odyssey): the prophet in his times, the debate on whether he was "primarily a prophet of judgment or of hope", and the book's legacy in Judaism and Christianity.
- `7` [Israel Museum, The Digital Dead Sea Scrolls: The Great Isaiah Scroll](http://dss.collections.imj.org.il/isaiah): photographs of the complete scroll of Isaiah found at Qumran in 1947, with an introduction.
- `6` [BibleProject, Isaiah 1–39](https://bibleproject.com/videos/isaiah-1-39/): an animated overview of the first part of the book, its warnings of judgement and its hope of "a suffering servant, a Messiah".
- `6` [BibleProject, Isaiah 40–66](https://bibleproject.com/videos/isaiah-40-66/): an animated overview of the second part of the book.
