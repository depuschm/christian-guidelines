# 1 Samuel

1 Samuel tells how Israel passed from the rule of judges to the rule of kings. The introduction to the New American Bible says that the books of Samuel "describe the rise and development of kingship in Israel", and that Samuel "bridges the gap between the period of the Judges and the monarchy, and guides Israel's transition to kingship". It divides the story around its main figures: Samuel, "the reluctant king maker" (chapters 1–12), and Saul, "the king whom the Lord rejects" (chapters 13–31), whose story is interwoven with the rise of David, "the king after the Lord's own heart", whose reign fills 2 Samuel. The book's editor "presents both positive and negative traditions about the monarchy", portraying it "both as evidence of Israel's rejection of the Lord as their sovereign" and "as part of God's plan to deliver the people" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Books of Samuel](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/1samuel/0)). Its first chapters tell of Hannah, who prays for a son and sings a song of praise ([1 Samuel 2:1–10](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1SA02.htm#V1)) that Mary's Magnificat echoes ([Luke 1:46–55](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/LUK01.htm#V46)).

In the Hebrew Bible, 1 and 2 Samuel are one book among the Former Prophets, after Joshua and Judges. The Jewish Encyclopedia explains that "Originally the two books of Samuel formed a single book, as did the two books of Kings", and that the Greek Septuagint divided Samuel and Kings into four "Books of Kingdoms", a division that Jerome kept in the Latin Vulgate under the name "Books of Kings" ([Jewish Encyclopedia, Samuel, Books of](https://jewishencyclopedia.com/articles/13080-samuel-books-of)). In the biblical listing of the Orthodox Church, "which is generally that of the Septuagint", "1 and 2 Samuel are called 1 and 2 Kings, and 1 and 2 Kings are called 3 and 4 Kings" ([Orthodox Church in America, The Orthodox Faith: Old Testament, History](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/doctrine-scripture/old-testament/history)). The story continues in [2 Samuel](2-samuel.md).

The book names no author. A Jewish tradition recorded in the Talmud holds that "Samuel wrote his own book", and that, since the book reports his death, "Gad the seer and Nathan the prophet finished it" ([Sefaria, Bava Batra 14b](https://www.sefaria.org/Bava_Batra.14b.12), [Sefaria, Bava Batra 15a](https://www.sefaria.org/Bava_Batra.15a.8)). Many modern scholars read the book instead as part of the Deuteronomistic History, a history of Israel from Deuteronomy to Kings; in Thomas Römer's reconstruction, which Steven McKenzie says has achieved "a sort of consensus", its scrolls were revised "under Josiah, in the exile, and then in the Persian period" ([Bible Odyssey, Steven L. McKenzie, Deuteronomistic History](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/deuteronomistic-history/)); the USCCB introduction speaks of "A Deuteronomistic editor" who shaped earlier blocks of tradition, such as the Ark Narrative and the story of "David's ascendancy over Saul", into the book ([USCCB, Introduction to the Books of Samuel](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/1samuel/0)).

1 Samuel is also Jewish Scripture: Sefaria describes it as "the third book of the Prophets, which begins with the birth and leadership of the prophet Samuel" ([Sefaria, I Samuel](https://www.sefaria.org/I_Samuel)). Hannah's silent prayer, in which "Only her lips moved, but her voice was not heard" ([1 Samuel 1:13](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1SA01.htm#V13)), "was later espoused by Talmud sages" as a way of praying ([Bible Odyssey, Ruth Fidler, Hannah](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/hannah/)). In Christian Bibles, 1 Samuel stands among the historical books that follow the Law ([Orthodox Church in America, The Orthodox Faith: Old Testament, History](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/doctrine-scripture/old-testament/history)).

## How the churches read it

Where the churches, or their interpreters, read the book differently, or where it has played a part in their history. Each entry starts with its source's score.

- `7` [Pope John Paul II, General Audience of 20 March 2002: The canticle of Anna, the mother of Samuel](https://www.vatican.va/content/john-paul-ii/en/audiences/2002/documents/hf_jp-ii_aud_20020320.html): on **Hannah's song** ([1 Samuel 2:1–10](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1SA02.htm#V1)), **Catholic** teaching hears it echoed in Mary's Magnificat ([Luke 1:46–55](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/LUK01.htm#V46)): the Pope calls it "the Magnificat of the Old Testament", and notes that in Hebrew the last word of the song, "his anointed", "is precisely 'messiah'", which turns "this royal prayer into a song of messianic hope". In the **Orthodox** Church the song is the third of the biblical odes sung at Matins, "The Prayer of Hannah, the mother of the Prophet Samuel" ([Orthodox Church in America, The Lenten Office of Daily Matins](https://www.oca.org/files/PDF/Music/Rubrics/Horologion/lenten-office-daily-matins.pdf)), and commemorates Hannah as a prophetess on 9 December ([Orthodox Church in America, Prophetess Hannah the mother of the Prophet Samuel](https://www.oca.org/saints/lives/2026/12/09/103507-prophetess-hannah-the-mother-of-the-prophet-samuel)). The **Church of England** prays it as "A Song of Hannah" in Common Worship, with the refrain "Blessed are those who believe, for what God has promised will be fulfilled" ([Church of England, Common Worship: Daily Prayer, 21 A Song of Hannah](https://www.churchofengland.org/liturgy/common-worship/daily-prayer/canticles-daily-prayer/21-song-hannah)), which echoes Elizabeth's words to Mary ([Luke 1:45](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/LUK01.htm#V45)). How the churches pray the Magnificat itself is set out on the [Luke](../new-testament/luke.md) page.
- `7` [Catechism of the Catholic Church, 436](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P1F.HTM): on **the anointing of kings** ([1 Samuel 10:1](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1SA10.htm#V1), [1 Samuel 16:13](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1SA16.htm#V13)), the **Catholic** Catechism explains that "Christ" comes from the Greek translation of "the Hebrew Messiah, which means 'anointed'", that "in Israel those consecrated to God for a mission that he gave were anointed in his name", "for kings, for priests and, in rare instances, for prophets", citing the anointing of Saul and David, and that Jesus fulfilled Israel's hope "in his threefold office of priest, prophet and king". The **Reformed** Heidelberg Catechism likewise asks "Why is he called 'Christ,' meaning 'anointed'?" and answers that he is anointed as "our chief prophet and teacher", "our only high priest" and "our eternal king"; Christians, it adds, "share in his anointing" ([Heidelberg Catechism, Q&A 31–32](https://www.crcna.org/welcome/beliefs/confessions/heidelberg-catechism)).
- `7` [John Calvin, Institutes of the Christian Religion, IV.20.28–31](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/calvin/institutes.vi.xxi.html): on **David and "the LORD's anointed"** ([1 Samuel 24:6](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1SA24.htm#V6), [1 Samuel 26:9–11](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1SA26.htm#V9)), David's refusal to harm Saul shaped Protestant teaching on obedience to rulers. The **Reformed** reformer Calvin cites it to teach that reverence is owed "to all our rulers, be their characters what they may", while adding that "popular magistrates" appointed "to curb the tyranny of kings" may check "the undue license of kings", and betray the people's liberty if they fail to. The **Church of England**'s homily "Against Rebellion", one of the homilies its [Articles of Religion](https://www.churchofengland.org/liturgy/book-of-common-prayer/articles-religion) judge should be read in churches, holds up the same example "for the better instruction of all subjects in their bounden duty of obedience" ([An Homily Against Disobedience and Willful Rebellion, second part](https://internetshakespeare.uvic.ca/doc/Homilies_2-21_M/section/The%20second%20part%20of%20the%20Homily%20Against%20Disobedience%20and%20Willful%20Rebellion./index.html), Internet Shakespeare Editions). What the churches teach today about obeying rulers, and about when to obey God rather than people, is set out in [Public life](../../general/public-life.md), `CIV-1` and `CIV-3`.
- `7` [Catechism of the Catholic Church, 2116](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P7E.HTM): on **Saul and the medium at Endor** ([1 Samuel 28:3–25](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1SA28.htm#V3)), the Chronicler says that Saul died partly "because he asked counsel of one who had a familiar spirit" ([1 Chronicles 10:13–14](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1CH10.htm#V13)), and the Law forbids it ([Leviticus 19:31](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/LEV19.htm#V31), [Deuteronomy 18:10–12](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/DEU18.htm#V10)). The **Catholic** Catechism teaches that "All forms of divination are to be rejected", among them "conjuring up the dead" and "recourse to mediums"; the **Reformed** Heidelberg Catechism, citing the same laws, teaches that the first commandment requires us to "avoid and shun all idolatry, sorcery, superstitious rites" ([Heidelberg Catechism, Q&A 94](https://www.crcna.org/welcome/beliefs/confessions/heidelberg-catechism)). Whether it was really Samuel who appeared is an open question below.

## Open questions

Questions on which the churches or scholars disagree. These guidelines don't pick a side.

- `7` [USCCB, Introduction to the Books of Samuel](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/1samuel/0) (New American Bible, Revised Edition): **who wrote 1 Samuel, and when?** Jewish tradition holds that "Samuel wrote his own book" and that "Gad the seer and Nathan the prophet finished it" ([Sefaria, Bava Batra 14b](https://www.sefaria.org/Bava_Batra.14b.12), [Sefaria, Bava Batra 15a](https://www.sefaria.org/Bava_Batra.15a.8)); the Jewish Encyclopedia traces the second part of this to [1 Chronicles 29:29](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1CH29.htm#V29), which names the histories of "Samuel the seer", "Nathan the prophet" and "Gad the seer" ([Jewish Encyclopedia, Samuel, Books of](https://jewishencyclopedia.com/articles/13080-samuel-books-of)). The USCCB introduction holds that "this material does not present either a continuous history or a systematic account of this period": an editor built "a narrative timeline around freely composed speeches" and shaped independent blocks, such as the Ark Narrative ([1 Samuel 4:1–7:1](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1SA04.htm#V1)), Saul's rise ([1 Samuel 9:1–11:15](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1SA09.htm#V1)) and David's ascendancy over Saul ([1 Samuel 16:1–31:13](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1SA16.htm#V1)), into "an object lesson for biblical Israel" after the destruction of Jerusalem. Scholars debate how and when the wider Deuteronomistic History was edited ([Bible Odyssey, Steven L. McKenzie, Deuteronomistic History](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/deuteronomistic-history/)).
- `7` [Augustine, On Care to Be Had for the Dead, 18](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/1316.htm) (New Advent): **was it really Samuel who appeared at Endor?** The book says that the woman "saw Samuel" and that "Samuel said to Saul…" ([1 Samuel 28:11–19](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1SA28.htm#V11)). Justin Martyr took it as proof that souls survive death: "the soul of Samuel was called up by the witch, as Saul demanded" ([Justin Martyr, Dialogue with Trypho, 105](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/01287.htm)). Augustine notes that "some think it was not Samuel himself", but "that some spirit, meet for so evil works, did figure his semblance", and recalls that Sirach praises Samuel because "even when dead" he "did prophesy" ([Sirach 46:20](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/SIR46.htm#V20)). Tertullian held that a demon was permitted "to represent the soul of Samuel": "God forbid, however, that we should suppose that the soul of any saint, much less of a prophet, can be dragged out of (its resting-place in Hades) by a demon" ([Tertullian, A Treatise on the Soul, 57](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0310.htm)). The Protestant commentator Matthew Henry likewise held that "God permitted the devil" "to put on Samuel's shape" ([Matthew Henry, Commentary on the Whole Bible, 1 Samuel 28](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/henry/mhc2.iSam.xxix.html)).
- `6` [Bible Odyssey, Keith Bodner, Goliath](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/goliath/): **who killed Goliath, and how tall was he?** Bodner notes that "Scholars note a tension between this story (1Sam 17) and the one in 2Sam 21:19-22, which reports that a certain Elhanan son of Jaare-oregim killed Goliath", and that "The Greek Septuagint lists Goliath's height approximately three feet shorter" than the "six cubits and a span" of the Hebrew text, which the WEBBE follows ([1 Samuel 17:4](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1SA17.htm#V4)). The parallel in Chronicles says that Elhanan "killed Lahmi the brother of Goliath" ([1 Chronicles 20:5](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1CH20.htm#V5)), and some translations, the WEBBE among them, read the same in 2 Samuel: "Goliath the Gittite's brother" ([2 Samuel 21:19](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2SA21.htm#V19)).
