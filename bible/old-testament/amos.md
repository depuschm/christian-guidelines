# Amos

The Book of Amos collects the oracles of "a sheepbreeder of Tekoa in Judah, who delivered his oracles in the Northern Kingdom during the prosperous reign of Jeroboam II (786–746 B.C.)", at the royal sanctuary of Bethel, "from which he was finally expelled by the priest in charge" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Amos](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/amos/0); [Amos 1:1](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/AMO01.htm#V1), [2 Kings 14:23–29](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2KI14.htm#V23)). The book opens with judgements on Israel's neighbours and ends the series with Israel itself; then come oracles of judgement, five visions, the clash with the priest Amaziah, and a closing promise of restoration ([Amos 7:10–17](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/AMO07.htm#V10), [Amos 9:11–15](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/AMO09.htm#V11)). The USCCB introduction calls Amos's message "one of the most powerful voices ever to challenge hypocrisy and injustice": "Religion without justice is an affront to the God of Israel and, far from appeasing God, can only provoke divine wrath", and "The Lord is not some petty national god but the sovereign creator of the cosmos". Samuel Thomas writes that "All prophets in the Hebrew Bible exhibit moral outrage, but Amos seems to have been downright hopping mad" ([Samuel Thomas, Let Justice Roll Down Like Waters, Bible Odyssey](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/let-justice-roll-down-like-waters/)).

Amos is usually counted the first of the prophets whose words were written down as a book. Christine Hayes begins the "literary prophets" with him, "even though it is not the first in the order of the Bible", and dates his preaching to "around 750 under the reign of Jeroboam the Second" ([Christine Hayes, Introduction to the Old Testament, Lecture 16, Open Yale Courses](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-16)); the Orthodox Church in America notes that "Although Amos is ranked third, his prophecy was the first to be recorded" ([Orthodox Church in America, Lives of all saints commemorated on June 15: Prophet Amos](https://www.oca.org/saints/all-lives/2026/06/15)). The USCCB introduction holds that the book "is an anthology of his oracles and was compiled either by the prophet or by some of his disciples", and Luther Seminary's Enter the Bible that the speeches "were once spoken by Amos, but they were most likely collected by other people–probably followers or disciples of Amos–and gathered into a book" ([Enter the Bible, Introductory Issues in Amos](https://enterthebible.org/courses/amos/lessons/introductory-issues-in-amos/)). The ESV Global Study Bible dates his ministry "sometime between 793–739 B.C., probably nearer the end of that period" ([ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to Amos](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-amos/)). Whether the hopeful ending comes from Amos himself is one of the open questions below.

Amos is Jewish Scripture: "Amos is the third of 12 books of Minor Prophets ('Trei Asar'), marked by their shortness" ([Sefaria, Amos](https://www.sefaria.org/Amos)). In the Hebrew Bible the twelve form one book: the Talmud explains that Hosea's prophecy "is written together with those of Haggai, Zechariah, and Malachi in one book of the Twelve Prophets", because "Were it written separately, since it is small it would be lost" ([Sefaria, Bava Batra 14b:9](https://www.sefaria.org/Bava_Batra.14b.9)), and Sirach already speaks of "the twelve prophets" ([Sirach 49:10](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/SIR49.htm#V10); [deuterocanon](../README.md#deuterocanon)). In the Greek Septuagint, the Old Testament of the Orthodox churches, Amos comes second, after Hosea and before Micah and Joel ([NETS, The Twelve Prophets, translated by George E. Howard](https://ccat.sas.upenn.edu/nets/edition/32-twelve-nets.pdf)). Every church reads Amos among the minor prophets, a name that refers "to the length of their books, not to their individual prominence" ([Orthodox Church in America, Lives of all saints commemorated on June 15: Prophet Amos](https://www.oca.org/saints/all-lives/2026/06/15)).

## How the churches read it

Where the churches, or their interpreters, read the book differently, or where it has played a part in their history. Each entry starts with its source's score.

- `8` [Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith, Instruction on Certain Aspects of the "Theology of Liberation", IV.6 and IX.10](https://www.vatican.va/roman_curia/congregations/cfaith/documents/rc_con_cfaith_doc_19840806_theology-liberation_en.html) (1984): **Amos in Catholic social teaching.** The **Catholic** Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith teaches that "the prophets after Amos keep affirming with particular vigor the requirements of justice and solidarity and the need to pronounce a very severe judgment on the rich who oppress the poor", and that "Justice as regards God and justice as regards mankind are inseparable". It credits the "theologies of liberation" with "restoring to a place of honor the great texts of the prophets and of the Gospel in defense of the poor", but holds that they "go on to a disastrous confusion between the 'poor' of the Scripture and the 'proletariat' of Marx" (the instruction's reading of the exodus is set out on the [Exodus](exodus.md) page). The Catechism of the Catholic Church quotes the oracle against "buying the poor for silver and the needy for a pair of sandals" ([Amos 8:6](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/AMO08.htm#V6)): Jesus "does not soften the vehemence of former oracles", "but invites us to recognize his own presence in the poor who are his brethren" ([Catechism of the Catholic Church, 2443–2449](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P8F.HTM)).
- `8` [The Belhar Confession, 4](https://www.rca.org/about/theology/creeds-and-confessions/the-belhar-confession/) (Dutch Reformed Mission Church in South Africa, 1986, on the site of the Reformed Church in America): **"so that justice may roll down like waters".** The confession, which "has its roots in the struggle against apartheid in southern Africa" and is now a standard of the Uniting Reformed Church in Southern Africa and the Reformed Church in America, cites Amos 5 to confess that "the church must witness against and strive against any form of injustice, so that justice may roll down like waters, and righteousness like an ever-flowing stream" ([Amos 5:24](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/AMO05.htm#V24)), and that "the church as the possession of God must stand where the Lord stands, namely against injustice and with the wronged". It is a **Reformed** confession.
- `7` [Dennis R. Edwards, The Bible in the Civil Rights Movement](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/the-bible-in-the-civil-rights-movement/) (Bible Odyssey): **Amos and the civil rights movement.** Among the quotations engraved beside the statue of the **Baptist** preacher Martin Luther King Jr. at his memorial in Washington is "part of an oracle from the Hebrew prophet Amos" ([Amos 5:24](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/AMO05.htm#V24)). Edwards notes that "It's not surprising that a Baptist preacher would quote the Bible", but that the Bible "took on special significance for many involved in the Civil Rights Movement", and that "There were many other clergymen along with Dr. King who relied upon the Bible to guide their involvement in the movement". In his Letter from a Birmingham Jail (1963), King asked: "Was not Amos an extremist for justice: 'Let justice roll down like waters and righteousness like an ever flowing stream'" ([Martin Luther King Jr., Letter from a Birmingham Jail, University of Pennsylvania African Studies Center](https://www.africa.upenn.edu/Articles_Gen/Letter_Birmingham.html)); two months before the March on Washington, at a rally in Detroit, he declared that "justice will roll down like waters, and righteousness like a mighty stream" ([The Martin Luther King, Jr. Research and Education Institute, "I Have a Dream"](https://kinginstitute.stanford.edu/i-have-dream)), and his "I Have a Dream" speech quotes the verse too ([Marc Zvi Brettler, The Prophets and the Temple, Bible Odyssey](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/the-prophets-and-the-temple/)).
- `7` [Augustine, City of God, Book XVIII, chapter 28](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/120118.htm) (New Advent): **the tent of David and the church of the nations.** At the meeting in Jerusalem, James quotes [Amos 9:11–12](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/AMO09.htm#V11) as agreeing with the conversion of the Gentiles ([Acts 15:15–18](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ACT15.htm#V15); see the [Acts](../new-testament/acts.md) page). Augustine quotes the same verses in his chapter on what Hosea and Amos prophesied "Pertaining to the Gospel of Christ", together with [Amos 4:13](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/AMO04.htm#V13), which he quotes as "announcing to men their Christ", where the WEBBE has "declares to man what is his thought". The **Reformed** commentator Matthew Henry holds that "To him to whom all the prophets bear witness this prophet, here in the close, bears his testimony", and that "In him also God's covenant with Israel had its accomplishment" ([Matthew Henry, Commentary on Amos 9](https://www.ccel.org/h/henry/mhc2/MHC30009.HTM)). The **Catholic** New American Bible notes that "In Acts 15:15–17, James interprets this passage in a messianic sense" ([USCCB, Amos, chapter 9, with notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/amos/9)).
- `7` [Orthodox Church in America, Lives of all saints commemorated on June 15: Prophet Amos](https://www.oca.org/saints/all-lives/2026/06/15): **the Orthodox commemoration.** The **Orthodox** Church keeps the feast of the Prophet Amos on 15 June. Its life calls him "Simple and uneducated, yet fervent in his faith and zealous for the glory of God", a "former shepherd of sheep and goats, and dresser of sycamore trees" who "was chosen by the Lord for prophetic service", and records the tradition that he was struck down and "died there after two days (circa 787 B.C.)". In icons "His scroll reads: It is he who builds his ascent up to Heaven" ([Amos 9:6](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/AMO09.htm#V6)).
- `6` [USCCB, Readings for the Twenty-fifth Sunday in Ordinary Time (21 September 2025)](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/readings/092125.cfm): **Amos and the rich in the lectionaries.** In the **Catholic** lectionary, Amos is read on two Sundays in Ordinary Time beside Jesus' parables about money in Luke 16: the oracle against those who "trample upon the needy" ([Amos 8:4–7](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/AMO08.htm#V4)) with the dishonest steward ([Luke 16:1–13](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/LUK16.htm#V1)), and the woe to "the complacent in Zion" ([Amos 6:1–7](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/AMO06.htm#V1)) with the rich man and Lazarus ([Luke 16:19–31](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/LUK16.htm#V19); [USCCB, Readings for the Twenty-sixth Sunday in Ordinary Time (28 September 2025)](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/readings/092825.cfm); see the [Luke](../new-testament/luke.md) page). The Revised Common Lectionary, used by most mainline **Protestant** churches in Canada and the United States ([Vanderbilt Divinity Library, Revised Common Lectionary: FAQ](https://lectionary.library.vanderbilt.edu/faq/)), reads the same two passages in Year C, and Amos 5:18–24, 5:6–7 and 10–15, and Amaziah's challenge in chapter 7 on other Sundays after Pentecost ([Vanderbilt Divinity Library, Revised Common Lectionary: Sunday citations](https://lectionary.library.vanderbilt.edu/sunday-citations/)).

## Open questions

Questions on which the churches or scholars disagree. These guidelines don't pick a side.

- `7` [USCCB, Amos, chapter 7, with notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/amos/7) (New American Bible, Revised Edition): **what did Amos mean by "I was no prophet"?** The translations differ on the tense: the WEBBE has "I was no prophet, neither was I a prophet's son, but I was a herdsman" ([Amos 7:14–15](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/AMO07.htm#V14)), the NRSVUE "I am no prophet nor a prophet's son, but I am a herdsman", with the note "Or was" ([Bible Gateway, Amos 1–9 (NRSVUE)](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Amos%201-9&version=NRSVUE)), and the New American Bible "I am not a prophet, nor do I belong to a company of prophets". Its note reads the reply as a rejection of the "prophet-for-hire" who "earns [his] bread" by oracles: "To disassociate himself from this kind of 'professional' prophet, Amos rejects outright the title of nabi' ('prophet')". Matthew Henry reads it as "neither born nor bred to the office", "not educated in the schools of the prophets, as many others were" ([Matthew Henry, Commentary on Amos 7](https://www.ccel.org/h/henry/mhc2/MHC30007.HTM)); the ESV Global Study Bible as "not a prophet by profession" ([ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to Amos](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-amos/)); and Samuel Thomas as a sign that "Amos did not really consider himself a prophet" ([Samuel Thomas, Let Justice Roll Down Like Waters, Bible Odyssey](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/let-justice-roll-down-like-waters/)).
- `7` [Christine Hayes, Introduction to the Old Testament, Lecture 16, Open Yale Courses](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-16): **is the hopeful ending from Amos?** Hayes notes that because the book's "overriding theme" is that "punishment is inevitable", "most scholars believe that the final verses of the book, verses halfway through [chapter 9 verse] 8 down to 15, are a later addition by an editor", "likely added in order to relieve the gloom and the pessimism and the fatalism of the prophet's message"; many scholars also see the short oracle against Judah ([Amos 2:4–5](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/AMO02.htm#V4)) as an addition made "after Judah's fall". The **Catholic** New American Bible calls 9:11–15 "A later appendix" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Amos](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/amos/0)) and "most likely an editorial supplement", in which the editors "express the view that destruction was not the Lord's final word for Israel" ([USCCB, Amos, chapter 9, with notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/amos/9)). Matthew Henry reads the promise as Amos's own: "this prophet, here in the close, bears his testimony" ([Matthew Henry, Commentary on Amos 9](https://www.ccel.org/h/henry/mhc2/MHC30009.HTM)), and the ESV Global Study Bible counts among the book's themes that "A faithful remnant of Israel would be preserved and would someday see glorious restoration and blessing" ([ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to Amos](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-amos/); [Amos 9:8](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/AMO09.htm#V8)).
- `7` [Marc Zvi Brettler, The Prophets and the Temple](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/the-prophets-and-the-temple/) (Bible Odyssey): **does Amos reject worship itself, or worship without justice?** Brettler notes that "Prophetic religion is commonly thought to oppose temple worship and to insist that proper ethical behavior alone is sufficient for God", citing [Amos 5:21–24](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/AMO05.htm#V21); but "It is difficult to know exactly how exaggerated these speeches are", and he holds that "the message of such prophecies was likely that temple worship was not automatically effective and that ritual practice must be accompanied by ethical behavior". The **Catholic** New American Bible holds that "The prophet does not condemn cultic activity as such but rather the people's attempt to offer worship with hands unclean from oppression of their fellow Israelites", and that "Through the Sinai covenant the love of God and the love of neighbor are inextricably bound together" ([USCCB, Amos, chapter 5, with notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/amos/5)). Hayes holds that "for Amos, and for all the prophets, injustice is sacrilege", which "is not to say that they would be rejected were Israel to be upholding the covenant", but that "for some of the prophets rejection of the cult was quite radical", an idea "not yet really fully formed in Amos" ([Christine Hayes, Introduction to the Old Testament, Lecture 16, Open Yale Courses](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-16)). The next verse asks, "Did you bring to me sacrifices and offerings in the wilderness forty years, house of Israel?" ([Amos 5:25–27](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/AMO05.htm#V25)), words Stephen quotes in his speech in Acts ([Acts 7:42–43](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ACT07.htm#V42)).

## Tutorials and further reading

- `7` [Christine Hayes, Literary Prophecy: Amos](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-16) (Open Yale Courses, Introduction to the Old Testament, lecture 16): a university lecture, with a transcript, on the literary prophets and the structure, literary features and themes of Amos.

- `6` [Enter the Bible, Theological Themes in Amos](https://enterthebible.org/courses/amos/lessons/theological-themes-in-amos/) (Luther Seminary): God's anger as part of God's love, God's judgement as "a middle step", and prophetic authority.
