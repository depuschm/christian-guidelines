# 2 Chronicles

2 Chronicles retells the history of the kings in Jerusalem from Solomon to the exile. Its first nine chapters tell how Solomon asked for wisdom, built the temple and dedicated it with prayer. The rest of the book follows only the kings of Judah, from Rehoboam, under whom the northern tribes broke away, to the fall of Jerusalem to the Babylonians. It ends with the decree of Cyrus, king of Persia, that the exiles may go up and build the LORD's house again ([2 Chronicles 36:22–23](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2CH36.htm#V22)). The introduction to the New American Bible explains that "the Chronicler aimed to establish and defend the legitimate claims of the Davidic monarchy in Israel's history, and to underscore the status of Jerusalem and its divinely established Temple worship as the center of religious life for the Jewish people" ([USCCB, Introduction to the First Book of Chronicles](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/1chronicles/0)). The ESV Global Study Bible names as its central theme "God's covenant with David as the basis of Israel's life and hope", and notes that the Chronicler "showed how the nation's unfaithfulness to God had led to disaster but also how its faithful kings and people had experienced God's blessing" ([ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to 1–2 Chronicles](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-1-2-chronicles/)).

1 and 2 Chronicles are one book in the Hebrew Bible, the first half of which is described in [1 Chronicles](1-chronicles.md). Sefaria describes 2 Chronicles, *Divrei Hayamim Bet*, as "the second half of the last book of the Hebrew Bible", which "focuses almost exclusively on kings from Judah" ([Sefaria, II Chronicles](https://www.sefaria.org/II_Chronicles)), so that the Jewish Bible ends with Cyrus's call to go up to Jerusalem. Christian Bibles place Chronicles after Kings among the books "which are called historical"; in the Septuagint, the Greek translation followed by the Orthodox Church, the books are called *Paralipomena*, "that which has been left out", as if they filled in what the earlier histories omit ([Orthodox Church in America, The Orthodox Faith: Old Testament, History](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/doctrine-scripture/old-testament/history)). The USCCB introduction adds that the books "are much more than a supplement to Samuel and Kings".

The book names no author. The ESV Global Study Bible notes that "traditionally he has been called 'the Chronicler'", and describes him as a priest or Levite "employed in the service of the temple during the Persian period (539–332 B.C.)" ([ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to 1–2 Chronicles](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-1-2-chronicles/)). The Talmud says that Ezra wrote "the genealogy of the book of Chronicles until his period" ([Sefaria, Bava Batra 15a:2](https://www.sefaria.org/Bava_Batra.15a.2)). Many scholars date the book later and ask whether the Chronicler also wrote Ezra and Nehemiah; the debate is set out under open questions below.

## How the churches read it

Where the churches, or their interpreters, read the book differently, or where it has played a part in their history. Each entry starts with its source's score.

- `7` [Church of England, Common Worship: Canticle 52, The Song of Manasseh](https://www.churchofengland.org/liturgy/common-worship/daily-prayer/canticles-daily-prayer/52-song-manasseh): on **the prayer of Manasseh**, 2 Chronicles says that Manasseh's prayer is written "amongst the acts of the kings of Israel" but doesn't give its words ([2 Chronicles 33:18–19](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2CH33.htm#V18)). A penitential prayer in his name, the Prayer of Manasseh, is in Orthodox Bibles among the books of the [deuterocanon](../README.md#deuterocanon): "I have sinned, O Lord, I have sinned, and I acknowledge my iniquities" ([Prayer of Manasseh 12](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MAN01.htm#V12)). The **Church of England** prays it among the canticles of Common Worship as "The Song of Manasseh", with the refrain "Full of compassion and mercy and love is God, the Most High, the Almighty", while its Articles of Religion list "The Prayer of Manasses" among the books that "the Church doth read for example of life and instruction of manners; but yet doth it not apply them to establish any doctrine" ([Church of England, Articles of Religion, VI](https://www.churchofengland.org/liturgy/book-of-common-prayer/articles-religion#VI)). In the **Orthodox** Church the priest reads the "Prayer of Manasses" at Great Compline, as in the Lenten service with the Canon of St Andrew of Crete published by the Archdiocese of Canada of the Orthodox Church in America ([Orthodox Church in America, Archdiocese of Canada, Great Compline with the Canon of St Andrew of Crete](https://www.archdiocese.ca/sites/default/files/liturgical-music/6-lent/02%20Compline%20with%20Canon%20of%20St.Andrew%20of%20Crete/Great%20Compline%20served%20with%20the%20Canon%20of%20St%20Andrew.pdf)). For the **Catholic** Church, the Catholic Encyclopedia of 1907 records that the prayer "is based on II Paralipomenon, xxxiii, 11-13", that "In editions of the Vulgate antedating the Council of Trent it was placed after the books of Paralipomenon", and that "The Clementine Vulgate relegated it to the appendix" ([Catholic Encyclopedia, Apocrypha: Apocryphal psalms and prayers](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/01601a.htm#I3)).
- `6` [Matthew Henry, Commentary on the Whole Bible, 2 Chronicles 33](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/henry/mhc2.iiCh.xxxiv.html) (Christian Classics Ethereal Library): on **Manasseh's repentance** ([2 Chronicles 33:10–13](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2CH33.htm#V10)), the **Reformed** commentator Matthew Henry, a Presbyterian minister ([Christian Classics Ethereal Library, Matthew Henry](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/henry)), calls it "a memorable instance … of the riches of God's pardoning mercy and the power of his renewing grace", and draws the lesson that "Sanctified afflictions often prove happy means of conversion".
- `6` [USCCB, Readings for the Fourth Sunday of Lent, Year B](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/readings/031024-YearB.cfm): in the **Catholic** lectionary, the book's account of the people's unfaithfulness, the fall of Jerusalem and Cyrus's decree ([2 Chronicles 36:14–16](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2CH36.htm#V14), [2 Chronicles 36:19–23](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2CH36.htm#V19)) is the first reading on the Fourth Sunday of Lent in Year B. It is followed by Psalm 137, the lament of the exiles in Babylon ([Psalm 137](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/PSA137.htm)), and by the Gospel of Jesus and Nicodemus ([John 3:14–21](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JHN03.htm#V14)).

## Open questions

Questions on which the churches or scholars disagree, for example about the book's authorship or the meaning of a passage. These guidelines don't pick a side.

- `7` [Christine Hayes, The Restoration: 1 and 2 Chronicles, Ezra and Nehemiah](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-22) (Open Yale Courses, Introduction to the Old Testament, lecture 22): **who wrote Chronicles, and when?** The Talmud says that "Ezra wrote his own book and the genealogy of the book of Chronicles until his period" ([Sefaria, Bava Batra 15a:2](https://www.sefaria.org/Bava_Batra.15a.2)), and the Orthodox Church in America's account says that the books "may well have been compiled by Ezra, although scholars consider them as the work of third century authors, perhaps the same who wrote Ezra and Nehemiah" ([Orthodox Church in America, The Orthodox Faith: Old Testament, History](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/doctrine-scripture/old-testament/history)). The ESV Global Study Bible holds that the Chronicler was a priest or Levite of the Persian period (539–332 BC) who "had scribal training and access to the temple records" ([ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to 1–2 Chronicles](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-1-2-chronicles/)), and the USCCB introduction places him at "the end of the fifth century B.C." ([USCCB, Introduction to the First Book of Chronicles](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/1chronicles/0)). Hayes explains that scholars speak of "the Chronicler" as "the hypothetical author of 1 and 2 Chronicles and Ezra and possibly Nehemiah", and that all four books "were probably edited in the late fifth century BCE, maybe close to the fourth century — that's our best guess". The end of 2 Chronicles and the beginning of Ezra share the same decree of Cyrus ([2 Chronicles 36:22–23](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2CH36.htm#V22), [Ezra 1:1–3](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/EZR01.htm#V1)).
- `7` [USCCB, Introduction to the First Book of Chronicles](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/1chronicles/0) (New American Bible, Revised Edition): **how should the differences between Chronicles and Kings be read?** The introduction holds that biblical history "was less concerned with reporting in precise detail all the facts of a situation than with drawing out the meaning of those facts", and notes that the Chronicler's work "contains early preexilic material not found in the Books of Kings". Christine Hayes calls Chronicles "already an interpretation. It's an inner-biblical interpretation", which leaves out much that "sheds a poor light on Israel's kings" ([Christine Hayes, The Restoration: 1 and 2 Chronicles, Ezra and Nehemiah, Open Yale Courses](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-22)). Manasseh is the sharpest case: 2 Kings tells of no repentance and blames his sins for the exile ([2 Kings 23:26–27](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2KI23.htm#V26), [2 Kings 24:3–4](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2KI24.htm#V3)), while 2 Chronicles tells of his captivity in Babylon and his repentance ([2 Chronicles 33:10–13](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2CH33.htm#V10)). Jesse Ainslie and Joseph Lam explain the difference by the time of writing: "Some two centuries after the compilation of Kings, under Persian imperial rule, Chronicles recast the life of Manasseh in more sympathetic terms" ([Bible Odyssey, Jesse Ainslie and Joseph Lam, Manasseh](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/manasseh/)). The Reformed ESV Reformation Study Bible instead reads the captivity as an event, under "Either Esarhaddon (681–669 b.c.) or Ashurbanipal (669–627 b.c.)", and suggests that "Manasseh was involved in the rebellion (652–648 b.c.) of Shamash-Shum-Ukin of Babylon" ([ESV Reformation Study Bible, note on 2 Chronicles 33:11](https://www.biblegateway.com/resources/reformation-study-bible/2Chr.33.11)), and Matthew Henry held that Kings passed over the repentance because it was "personal only and not national" ([Matthew Henry, Commentary on the Whole Bible, 2 Chronicles 33](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/henry/mhc2.iiCh.xxxiv.html)).
- `6` [Steve Bateman, Does God Promise to Bless America? How to Apply 2 Chronicles 7:14](https://www.thegospelcoalition.org/article/god-promise-bless-america/) (The Gospel Coalition, 2024): **does the promise of 7:14 apply to nations today?** In American evangelical preaching the verse ([2 Chronicles 7:14](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2CH07.htm#V14)) has often been read as a call to national repentance; Bateman recalls a 1976 song that "helped a generation of Christians memorize 2 Chronicles 7:14", and that Ronald Reagan was sworn in "with his left hand on his mother's Bible open to 2 Chronicles 7:14". Bateman holds that "The promise is to God's redeemed people in the promised land during the only legitimate theocracy in history", so that "While the verse doesn't directly apply to America, it certainly applies to the American church." Steve Gaines, then president of the Southern Baptist Convention, writes "Is America Israel? No", but asks whether "warnings and promises to God's people in the Old Testament" can "be applied to Christians today" and answers "Absolutely"; he knows no verse "that serves as a better guide for praying for revival" ([Steve Gaines, An Appeal to Rightly Apply 2 Chronicles 7:14, ChurchLeaders](https://churchleaders.com/pastors/pastor-articles/306103-an-appeal-to-rightly-apply-2-chronicles-714-steve-gaines.html)). The pastor Shane Idleman finds in it "principles that lead to national restoration in the midst of catastrophes" ([Shane Idleman, Why 2 Chronicles 7:14 applies to modern America, The Christian Post](https://www.christianpost.com/voices/why-2-chronicles-714-applies-to-modern-america.html)).
