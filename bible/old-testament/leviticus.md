# Leviticus

Leviticus is the third book of the Pentateuch, the five books of the Law. Almost all of it is law that the LORD gives to Moses, from the Tent of Meeting and on Mount Sinai ([Leviticus 1:1](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/LEV01.htm#V1), [Leviticus 27:34](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/LEV27.htm#V34)). The introduction to the New American Bible explains that the ancient Greek translators named it "Leviticus" "because a good part of this book deals with concerns of the priests, who are of the tribe of Levi". It "mainly treats cultic matters", such as sacrifices and offerings, purity and holiness, the priesthood, the sanctuary and the feast days, but is "also interested in various behavioral, ethical, and economic issues"; its laws "cohere as a system and attempt to inculcate a way of life", and the book, "comprising as it does the center of the Pentateuch", carries forward the story of Exodus ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Leviticus](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/leviticus/0)). Its call "You shall be holy; for I, the LORD your God, am holy" ([Leviticus 19:2](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/LEV19.htm#V2)) runs through the book, and its nineteenth chapter gives the commandment "you shall love your neighbour as yourself" ([Leviticus 19:18](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/LEV19.htm#V18)), which Jesus names as the second of the two great commandments ([Matthew 22:37–40](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MAT22.htm#V37)).

The book names no author. The Orthodox Church in America's account of the Law says that scholars hold it "was not written by the personal hand of Moses" but grew out of "a number of oral and written traditions transmitted among the People of Israel, containing material of later periods", and that "in the Tradition of Israel and of the Christian Church, the Law remains essentially connected with Moses" ([Orthodox Church in America, The Orthodox Faith: Old Testament, Law](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/doctrine-scripture/old-testament/law)). The USCCB introduction places Leviticus in "the Priestly tradition (P) of the Pentateuch", with "signs of variant traditions and development" within it ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Leviticus](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/leviticus/0)), and most scholars assign chapters 17–26, which they call the "Holiness code", to a second priestly school ([Christine Hayes, The Priestly Legacy, Open Yale Courses](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-9)).

Leviticus is also Jewish Scripture. Under its Hebrew name *Vayikra* it is "the third book of the Torah", divided into ten Torah portions from *Vayikra* to *Bechukotai*, and Sefaria describes it as "unified by the theme of holiness in people, time, and space" ([Sefaria, Leviticus](https://www.sefaria.org/Leviticus)). Its Day of Atonement ([Leviticus 16:1–34](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/LEV16.htm#V1)) is Yom Kippur, the holiest day, "known as the Sabbath of Sabbaths" ([Christine Hayes, The Priestly Legacy, Open Yale Courses](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-9)). Every church reads Leviticus as one of the five books of the Law in its Old Testament ([Orthodox Church in America, The Orthodox Faith: Old Testament, Law](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/doctrine-scripture/old-testament/law)).

## How the churches read it

Where the churches, or their interpreters, read the book differently, or where it has played a part in their history. Each entry starts with its source's score.

- `8` [Pope Francis, Spes non confundit](https://www.vatican.va/content/francesco/en/bulls/documents/20240509_spes-non-confundit_bolla-giubileo2025.html) (2024): on **the jubilee** ([Leviticus 25:8–12](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/LEV25.htm#V8)), the **Catholic Church** holds a Jubilee or Holy Year that "the Pope proclaims every twenty-five years" (1). Pope Francis's bull for the Jubilee of 2025 quotes [Leviticus 25:10](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/LEV25.htm#V10), "an ancient appeal, one drawn from the word of God", to call for "acts of clemency and liberation that enable new beginnings" for prisoners, and notes that Jesus presented himself as the fulfilment of "the year of the Lord's favour" (10, citing [Luke 4:18–19](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/LUK04.htm#V18)); it quotes Leviticus 25:23 to urge "settling unjust and unpayable debts" (16). The Catechism counts "the jubilee year of forgiveness of debts", the "prohibition of loans at interest", the tithe, the daily payment of the day labourer and "the right to glean vines and fields" among the Old Testament's measures for the poor ([Catechism of the Catholic Church, 2449](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P8F.HTM)).
- `7` [Church of England, Articles of Religion, VII](https://www.churchofengland.org/liturgy/book-of-common-prayer/articles-religion#VII): on **which of the laws of Moses still bind Christians**, the **Anglican** Article VII holds that the law "as touching Ceremonies and Rites" does not bind Christians, "nor the Civil precepts thereof ought of necessity to be received in any commonwealth", yet "no Christian man whatsoever is free from the obedience of the Commandments which are called Moral"; the **Methodist** Articles of Religion repeat this ([The United Methodist Church, Articles of Religion, VI](https://www.umc.org/en/content/articles-of-religion)). The **Reformed** Westminster Confession calls the ceremonial laws, "prefiguring Christ", "now abrogated", and the judicial laws "expired together with the State of that people; not obliging any other now, further than the general equity thereof may require", while "The moral law doth forever bind all" ([Westminster Confession of Faith, 19.3–19.5](https://www.opc.org/wcf.html)). The same three kinds of precept go back to Thomas Aquinas: "moral" precepts "dictated by the natural law", "ceremonial" precepts, "determinations of the Divine worship", and "judicial" precepts, "determinations of the justice to be maintained among men" ([Summa Theologiae, I-II, q. 99, a. 4](https://www.newadvent.org/summa/2099.htm)); the ceremonies "had need to cease" when the New Law came, while the Old Law lasts "for ever" in its moral precepts ([Summa Theologiae, I-II, q. 103, a. 3](https://www.newadvent.org/summa/2103.htm)). The **Catholic** Catechism calls the Old Law "the first stage of revealed Law", whose "moral prescriptions are summed up in the Ten Commandments", "holy, spiritual, and good, yet still imperfect", and "a preparation for the Gospel" ([Catechism of the Catholic Church, 1961–1964](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P6V.HTM)), and the **Orthodox** Longer Catechism teaches that Christians must walk by the Ten Commandments, "for they are in substance the same law" written "in the hearts of all men" ([Longer Catechism of the Eastern Church, Q. 490](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/schaff/creeds2.vi.iii.iv.html)). How the **Lutheran** confessions relate the law to the Christian life is set out on the [Galatians](../new-testament/galatians.md) page.
- `7` [Catechism of the Catholic Church, 433](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P1E.HTM): on **the Day of Atonement and Christ** ([Leviticus 16:2](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/LEV16.htm#V2), [Leviticus 16:15–16](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/LEV16.htm#V15)), Catholic and Reformed teaching alike read the day and its sacrifices as pointing to Christ, as Hebrews does ([Hebrews 9:11–12](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/HEB09.htm#V11)). The **Catholic** Catechism recalls that the high priest invoked God's name "in atonement for the sins of Israel, after he had sprinkled the mercy seat in the Holy of Holies with the sacrificial blood", and then turns to Paul's words on Jesus "whom 'God put forward as an expiation by his blood'" ([Romans 3:25](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ROM03.htm#V25)); the **Reformed** Westminster Confession calls the ceremonial laws "typical ordinances" "prefiguring Christ, his graces, actions, sufferings, and benefits" ([Westminster Confession of Faith, 19.3](https://www.opc.org/wcf.html)). How the churches relate Christ's once-for-all sacrifice to the Lord's Supper is set out on the [Hebrews](../new-testament/hebrews.md) page.
- `7` [Catechism of the Catholic Church, 1539–1553](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P4T.HTM): on **the priesthood of Aaron** ([Leviticus 8:1–13](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/LEV08.htm#V1)), **Catholic** teaching, citing Leviticus 8, holds that "A special rite consecrated the beginnings of the priesthood of the Old Covenant", a priesthood "powerless to bring about salvation, needing to repeat its sacrifices ceaselessly", and that "The liturgy of the Church... sees in the priesthood of Aaron and the service of the Levites... a prefiguring of the ordained ministry of the New Covenant" (1539–1541). The **Baptist** Faith and Message stresses instead "the priesthood of believers" ([Baptist Faith and Message 2000, Preamble](https://bfm.sbc.net/preamble/)); 1 Peter calls all believers "a royal priesthood" ([1 Peter 2:9](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1PE02.htm#V9)). The wider question is set out on the [Hebrews](../new-testament/hebrews.md) page.
- `7` [Catechism of the Catholic Church, 582](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P1N.HTM): on **the food laws** ([Leviticus 11:1–47](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/LEV11.htm#V1)), the New Testament reports that Jesus said nothing from outside defiles a person, "making all foods clean" ([Mark 7:18–19](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MRK07.htm#V18)), and that Peter heard in a vision: "What God has cleansed, you must not call unclean" ([Acts 10:13–15](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ACT10.htm#V13)). The **Catholic** Catechism teaches that "Jesus perfects the dietary law, so important in Jewish daily life, by revealing its pedagogical meaning" (582).

## Open questions

Questions on which the churches or scholars disagree. These guidelines don't pick a side.

- `7` [USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Leviticus](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/leviticus/0) (New American Bible, Revised Edition): **who wrote Leviticus, and when?** The book presents its laws as given by the LORD to Moses on Mount Sinai ([Leviticus 26:46](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/LEV26.htm#V46)), and the tradition of Israel and of the church connects the Law with Moses; modern scholars see in it many traditions, with material from later periods ([Orthodox Church in America, The Orthodox Faith: Old Testament, Law](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/doctrine-scripture/old-testament/law)). The USCCB introduction assigns the book to "the Priestly tradition (P)". Christine Hayes explains that most scholars assign chapters 17–26 to a separate "Holiness" school (H), and that the relative dates of P and H are "much debated", though "the consensus increasingly is that H is later" ([Christine Hayes, The Priestly Legacy, Open Yale Courses](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-9)).
- `7` [Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith, Letter on the Pastoral Care of Homosexual Persons, 6](https://www.vatican.va/roman_curia/congregations/cfaith/documents/rc_con_cfaith_doc_19861001_homosexual-persons_en.html) (1986): **how do the sexual laws of chapters 18 and 20 bear on Christian ethics today?** These chapters forbid incest, adultery, sex between men and other relations, and chapter 20 sets the death penalty for several of them ([Leviticus 18:6–23](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/LEV18.htm#V6), [Leviticus 20:10–21](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/LEV20.htm#V10)). The Catechism of the Catholic Church defines incest with a reference to [Leviticus 18:7–20](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/LEV18.htm#V7) ([Catechism of the Catholic Church, 2388](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P87.HTM)). The Catholic letter reads [Leviticus 18:22](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/LEV18.htm#V22) and [Leviticus 20:13](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/LEV20.htm#V13) as excluding "from the People of God those who behave in a homosexual fashion", and holds that Paul "proposes the same doctrine". The churches now differ on whether they may marry or bless same-sex couples; their positions are set out in [Marriage, family and singleness](../../general/family.md#open-questions).
