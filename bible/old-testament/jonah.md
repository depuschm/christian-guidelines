# Jonah

The Book of Jonah tells a story rather than collecting oracles: "Unlike other prophetic books, this is not a collection of oracles but the story of a disobedient, narrow-minded prophet who is angry at the outcome of the sole message he delivers" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Jonah](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/jonah/0)). The LORD tells Jonah son of Amittai, "Arise, go to Nineveh, that great city, and preach against it", but he flees by sea towards Tarshish ([Jonah 1:1–3](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JON01.htm#V1)). A storm, the sailors and a great fish follow; Jonah prays, preaches in Nineveh, sees the city repent and God relent, and the book ends with God's question to the angry prophet ([Jonah 4:10–11](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JON04.htm#V10)). Nineveh was "the capital of the Assyrian empire which brought the Northern Kingdom of Israel to an end". The New American Bible holds that the story "caricatures a narrow mentality which would see God's interest extending only to Israel, whereas God is presented as concerned with and merciful to even the inhabitants of Nineveh", and that "The book is replete with irony, wherein much of its humor lies": "The name 'Jonah' means 'dove' in Hebrew, but Jonah's character is anything but dove-like" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Jonah](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/jonah/0)). The ESV Global Study Bible names as its primary theme "that God's compassion is boundless, not limited just to 'us' (Jonah and the Israelites) but also available for 'them' (the pagan sailors and the Ninevites)" ([ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to Jonah](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-jonah/)).

A prophet of the same name appears once more in the Old Testament: King Jeroboam II of Israel restored its borders according to the word the LORD "spoke by his servant Jonah the son of Amittai, the prophet, who was from Gath Hepher" ([2 Kings 14:25](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2KI14.htm#V25); [2 Kings](2-kings.md)). The ESV Global Study Bible places him in "the peaceful and prosperous time of Jeroboam II", who ruled "from 782 to 753 B.C." ([ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to Jonah](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-jonah/)). The book does not name its author. Matthew Henry held that "Probably Jonah was himself the penman of this book" ([Matthew Henry, Commentary on Jonah, Introduction](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/henry/mhc4.Jonah.i.html)); the Talmud holds that "The members of the Great Assembly wrote" the Twelve Prophets, with Ezekiel, Daniel and Esther ([Sefaria, Bava Batra 15a:2](https://www.sefaria.org/Bava_Batra.15a.2)); and the 1910 Catholic Encyclopedia notes that "nowhere does the book itself claim to have been written by the Prophet", and that "most modern scholars, for various reasons, assign the date of the composition to a much later epoch" ([Catholic Encyclopedia (1910), Jonah](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/08497b.htm)). When the book was written, and whether it tells history, are open questions below.

Jonah is Jewish Scripture: "Jonah ('Yonah') is one of 12 books of Minor Prophets ('Trei Asar'), marked by their shortness", and "The book is traditionally read publicly on the afternoon of Yom Kippur" ([Sefaria, Jonah](https://www.sefaria.org/Jonah)). The Talmud already names, as the reading from the Prophets at the afternoon service of the Day of Atonement, "the book of Jonah, which mentions the repentance of the people of Nineveh" ([Sefaria, Megillah 31a:11](https://www.sefaria.org/Megillah.31a.11)), and counts the twelve minor prophets as "one book of the Twelve Prophets" ([Sefaria, Bava Batra 14b:9](https://www.sefaria.org/Bava_Batra.14b.9)). Maya Bernstein gives the sages' reasons for the custom: the book "reminds us of God's infinite mercy", and the people of Nineveh "are a paradigm of repentance, a model for us as we struggle through the day" ([Maya Bernstein, Jonah and Yom Kippur, My Jewish Learning](https://www.myjewishlearning.com/article/jonah-yom-kippur/)). In the Hebrew order Jonah is the "fifth of the Minor Prophets" ([Bible Odyssey, Jonah (HarperCollins Bible Dictionary)](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/dictionary/jonah-2/)); in the Greek Septuagint, the Old Testament of the Orthodox churches, it comes sixth, after Hosea, Amos, Micah, Joel and Obadiah, and its translator judges that "Ionas is perhaps closest to the traditional Hebrew among the Twelve" ([NETS, The Twelve Prophets, translated by George E. Howard](https://ccat.sas.upenn.edu/nets/edition/32-twelve-nets.pdf)). Every church reads Jonah.

## Outline

The outline follows the ESV Global Study Bible: "The story of Jonah includes seven episodes, with the first three paralleled by the second three. The final episode stands alone as the climax of the story" ([ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to Jonah](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-jonah/)). In the Hebrew text and the New American Bible, the WEBBE's 1:17 is 2:1, and the WEBBE's 2:1–10 is 2:2–11 ([USCCB, Jonah, chapter 2, with notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/jonah/2)).

## How the churches read it

Where the churches, or their interpreters, read the book differently, or where it has played a part in their history. Each entry starts with its source's score.

- `7` [Catechism of the Catholic Church, 992–1004](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P2H.HTM): **the sign of Jonah.** Asked for a sign, Jesus answers that "no sign will be given to it but the sign of Jonah the prophet. For as Jonah was three days and three nights in the belly of the huge fish, so will the Son of Man be three days and three nights in the heart of the earth", and that "The men of Nineveh will stand up in the judgement with this generation and will condemn it, for they repented at the preaching of Jonah; and behold, someone greater than Jonah is here" ([Matthew 12:38–42](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MAT12.htm#V38), [Luke 11:29–32](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/LUK11.htm#V29); [Matthew](../new-testament/matthew.md), [Luke](../new-testament/luke.md)). The **Catholic** Catechism teaches that Jesus announced his own resurrection, and that "He speaks of this unique event as the 'sign of Jonah'". At Mass the Catholic Church reads [Jonah 3:1–10](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JON03.htm#V1) with [Luke 11:29–32](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/LUK11.htm#V29) on the Wednesday of the first week of Lent ([USCCB, Readings for Wednesday of the First Week in Lent, 25 February 2026](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/readings/022526.cfm)). Pope Francis set "the Jonah syndrome", which "afflicts those without zeal for the conversion of others" and "those who trust only in their personal righteousness, in their works", against the sign of Jonah, which "is the mercy of God in Jesus Crucified and Risen for us" ([Vatican, Pope Francis, The Jonah Syndrome, morning meditation, 14 October 2013](https://www.vatican.va/content/francesco/en/cotidie/2013/documents/papa-francesco-cotidie_20131014_jonah-syndrome.html)).
- `7` [Orthodox Church in America, Holy Saturday](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/worship/the-church-year/holy-saturday): **Jonah on Holy Saturday.** At the **Orthodox** Vespers of Holy Saturday, "fifteen readings from the Old Testament scriptures are read, all of which relate to God's work of creation and salvation which has been summed up and fulfilled in the coming of the predicted Messiah", among them Jonah; the readings for Holy Saturday 2026 give the whole book, [Jonah 1:1–4:11](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JON01.htm#V1) ([Orthodox Church in America, Scripture Readings for 11 April 2026 (Great and Holy Saturday)](https://www.oca.org/readings/daily/2026/04/11)). The Orthodox Church commemorates the prophet on 22 September, and its life of him teaches that from Jesus' words "the Lord shows clearly the symbolic meaning of the Book of the Prophet Jonah in relation to Christ's death on the Cross, descent into Hell, and the Resurrection" ([Orthodox Church in America, Lives of all saints commemorated on September 22 (Prophet Jonah)](https://www.oca.org/saints/all-lives/2026/09/22)).
- `7` [Cyril of Jerusalem, Catechetical Lecture 14](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/310114.htm) (New Advent): **Jonah as a figure of Christ.** Cyril of Jerusalem taught his catechumens that "when we examine the story of Jonas, great is the force of the resemblance. Jesus was sent to preach repentance; Jonas also was sent: but whereas the one fled, not knowing what should come to pass; the other came willingly, to give repentance unto salvation", and that in praying from the fish Jonah "was a type of Christ, who was to descend into Hades". Cyril of Alexandria, in the preface to his commentary, wrote that "the mystery of Christ, therefore, is foreshadowed and somehow represented to us in the story of the divinely inspired Jonah" ([Aron Tillema, Interpretations of Jonah, Bible Odyssey](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/interpretations-of-jonah/)). Enter the Bible notes that "Depictions of Jonah are abundant in early Christian art, particularly in funerary art such as catacomb walls and sarcophagi", where "Jonah's journey into and out of the belly of a big fish serves as representation of Christ's death and resurrection" ([Enter the Bible, Bible in the World – Jonah](https://enterthebible.org/courses/jonah/lessons/bible-in-the-world-jonah/)); the Vatican Museums keep "the sarcophagus of Jonah" among "some of the symbolic masterpieces of early Christian art" ([Vatican Museums, The Good Shepherd and Jonah](https://www.museivaticani.va/content/museivaticani/en/collezioni/musei/museo-pio-cristiano/buon-pastore-e-giona/buon-pastore-e-giona.html)).
- `6` [Syriac Orthodox Archdiocese of the Western United States, The Fast of Nineveh](https://www.soc-wus.org/2011News/21420119111.htm): **the Fast of Nineveh.** "The Syriac Orthodox Church of Antioch tradition attaches great significance to the Fast of Nineveh and three days of Jonah the prophet", kept before Great Lent. The **Malankara Orthodox Syrian Church** explains that "The three-day fast commemorates the three days Jonah spent in the belly of the fish and also the repentance of the city of Nineveh", that its origins in the Syrian Orthodox Church "can be traced back to the fourth century AD", and that the Armenians and the Copts took it up ([Northeast American Diocese, Malankara Orthodox Syrian Church, Nineveh Fast](https://www.neamericandiocese.org/feasts/feasts_details/23)). The **Coptic** Church counts it among its five major fasts ([Oriental Orthodox churches](../../traditions/oriental-orthodox.md)).
- `6` [Concordia Publishing House, Jonah: An Overview](https://blog.cph.org/study/jonah-an-overview) (adapted from the Lutheran Bible Companion): **a Lutheran reading of law and gospel.** The **Lutheran** publisher reads the book as "a story of both personal and national repentance", in which "God's wrath serves His greater purpose of having mercy on Jonah, the sailors, and the Ninevites", and applies the second call to Christians today: "May we never be found guilty of neglecting our mission to make disciples of all nations."
- `6` [John Calvin, Commentary on Jonah, Preface](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/calvin/calcom28.iii.i.html) (Christian Classics Ethereal Library): **a Reformed reading.** Calvin held that "This Book is partly historical and partly didactic". On the sign of Jonah he limited the likeness to the resurrection: "Jonah was not a type of Christ, because he was sent away unto the Gentiles, but because he returned to life again" ([John Calvin, Commentary on Jonah 1:1–2](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/calvin/calcom28.iii.1.ii.html)). The **Reformed** commentator Matthew Henry wrote that the story "is recorded chiefly for the sake of Christ, of whom Jonah was a type", and that it shows "God's mercy both in pardoning repenting sinners, witness Nineveh, and in bearing with repining saints, witness Jonah" ([Matthew Henry, Commentary on Jonah, Introduction](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/henry/mhc4.Jonah.i.html)). In the Revised Common Lectionary, used by many **Protestant** churches, [Jonah 3:1–5](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JON03.htm#V1) and [Jonah 3:10](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JON03.htm#V10) are read on the Third Sunday after the Epiphany in Year B, and [Jonah 3:10–4:11](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JON03.htm#V10) in the season after Pentecost in Year A ([Vanderbilt Divinity Library, Revised Common Lectionary: Sunday citations](https://lectionary.library.vanderbilt.edu/sunday-citations/)).

## Open questions

Questions on which the churches or scholars disagree. These guidelines don't pick a side.

- `7` [USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Jonah](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/jonah/0): **is Jonah history, or a story told to teach?** The New American Bible notes that "As to genre, it has been classified in various ways, such as parable or satire." Early Christian writers read it as history: Augustine told a questioner to "begin by dismissing doubts as to the fact itself; for this did actually occur, and did not occur in vain" ([Augustine, Letter 102, New Advent](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/1102102.htm)), and Cyril of Jerusalem wrote, "I believe that Jonas was preserved, for all things are possible with God" ([Cyril of Jerusalem, Catechetical Lecture 14](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/310114.htm)). The 1910 Catholic Encyclopedia holds that "Catholics have always looked upon the Book of Jonah as a fact-narrative", and argues from Jesus' words that "Christ contrasts fact with fact, not fancy with fancy, nor fancy with fact"; to those who accept that Christ cannot err but deny the story's history, holding that "Christ spoke according to the ideas of the people", it answers that their arguments "are not conclusive" ([Catholic Encyclopedia (1910), Jonah](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/08497b.htm)). Matthew Henry called the book "rather a history than a prophecy" ([Matthew Henry, Commentary on Jonah, Introduction](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/henry/mhc4.Jonah.i.html)), and the Lutheran Bible Companion holds that "The more traditional approach, which is preferable, reads the story of Jonah at face value as an artfully crafted historical narrative that describes real events from the prophet's life" ([Concordia Publishing House, Jonah: An Overview](https://blog.cph.org/study/jonah-an-overview)). Jay Sklar notes that "Throughout the centuries, the most common approach to Jonah was to read it as a historical account. In more recent times, however, many have read Jonah as a parable"; weighing the arguments, he finds that they "neither disprove a parabolic approach nor prove a historical approach", and is "hesitant to take an approach different from that of the majority of readers since ancient times" ([Jay Sklar, Is the Story of Jonah and the Whale a Historical Account or a Parable?, Crossway](https://www.crossway.org/articles/is-the-story-of-jonah-and-the-whale-a-historical-account-or-a-parable-jonah-1-and-2/)). Enter the Bible holds that "It seems the book is not trying to relay a straightforward history but to weave a moral tale", read by some as a parable, by others as satire, and by others as "simply a story that features a prophet" ([Enter the Bible, Introductory Issues in Jonah](https://enterthebible.org/courses/jonah/lessons/introductory-issues-in-jonah/)). Aron Tillema notes that "Many scholars today consider this ancient text to possess humorous qualities", but that "early interpretations of Jonah that read the text as humorous are few and far between" ([Aron Tillema, Humor in the Book of Jonah, Bible Odyssey](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/humor-in-the-book-of-jonah/)). In Jewish tradition the question is old too: Rabbi Yosef Kaspi wrote, "There are those who say that it all happened in reality, and those that say that it all occurred in a dream, and in a prophetic vision" ([Maya Bernstein, Jonah and Yom Kippur, My Jewish Learning](https://www.myjewishlearning.com/article/jonah-yom-kippur/)).
- `7` [Christine Hayes, Introduction to the Old Testament, Lecture 24, Open Yale Courses](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-24): **when was the book written?** Hayes notes that "The date of the book of Jonah really can't be ascertained and you will hear arguments in both directions. Many scholars date it late; others suppose that the story is at least at base an old, old story." The New American Bible holds that the book "almost certainly is postexilic and may reflect the somewhat narrow, nationalistic reforms of Ezra and Nehemiah" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Jonah](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/jonah/0); [Ezra](ezra.md), [Nehemiah](nehemiah.md)), and Julia M. O'Brien writes that "The book of Jonah, likely written over a hundred years after Nineveh's destruction, narrates the rapid repentance of this great city's king in order to make a point about divine forgiveness" ([Julia M. O'Brien, Nineveh, Bible Odyssey](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/nineveh/)). Those who take the book as history place the events in Jonah's own time: the 1910 Catholic Encyclopedia holds that "It is likely that the incidents took place during the period of Assyrian decadence", while noting that most modern scholars date the book "probably the fifth century B.C." ([Catholic Encyclopedia (1910), Jonah](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/08497b.htm)), and Matthew Henry held that Jonah probably wrote it himself ([Matthew Henry, Commentary on Jonah, Introduction](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/henry/mhc4.Jonah.i.html)).
- `7` [USCCB, Jonah, chapter 2, with notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/jonah/2) (New American Bible, Revised Edition): **was the psalm of chapter 2 part of the first story?** The New American Bible notes that the prayer's verses "may have originally been an independent composition", and Christine Hayes that "The prayer or the psalm is not entirely appropriate to the narrative context. It's probably an insertion in the story by a later writer" ([Christine Hayes, Introduction to the Old Testament, Lecture 24, Open Yale Courses](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-24)). Enter the Bible notes that "Jonah 2 seems to thank God for deliverance, but Jonah has not been delivered yet in the prose section of the book", and that "Other scholars consider Jonah 3-4 to be the original story with Jonah 1-2 added later" ([Enter the Bible, Introductory Issues in Jonah](https://enterthebible.org/courses/jonah/lessons/introductory-issues-in-jonah/)). Calvin read the psalm as Jonah's own: he "also writes a Song of Thanksgiving. This last part contains doctrines and is not a mere narrative" ([John Calvin, Commentary on Jonah, Preface](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/calvin/calcom28.iii.i.html)); and Cyril of Jerusalem heard Jonah speak in it "in the person of Christ, prophesying most clearly" ([Cyril of Jerusalem, Catechetical Lecture 14](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/310114.htm); [Jonah 2:1–9](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JON02.htm#V1)).
- `6` [Matthew Henry, Commentary on Jonah 4](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/henry/mhc4.Jonah.v.html) (Christian Classics Ethereal Library): **why did Jonah flee, and does he change?** Jonah gives his own reason: he knew that God would relent ([Jonah 4:2](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JON04.htm#V2)). A Jewish text of the eighth century has him reason, "I know that the nations are [close] to repentance, [and] now they will repent and the Holy One, blessed be He, will direct his anger against Israel", so that the rabbis read him as "interested in his own prophetic reputation and care for the people of Israel" ([Aron Tillema, Interpretations of Jonah, Bible Odyssey](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/interpretations-of-jonah/)). Christine Hayes reads him as "a champion of divine justice", who "believes that sin should be punished" and is "outraged at God's forgiveness" ([Christine Hayes, Introduction to the Old Testament, Lecture 24, Open Yale Courses](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-24)), and the New American Bible sees in the story the caricature of "a narrow mentality" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Jonah](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/jonah/0)). The book ends with God's question and no answer from Jonah ([Jonah 4:11](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JON04.htm#V11)); Maya Bernstein calls it "a book of contradictions, which ends in an unanswered question" ([Maya Bernstein, Jonah and Yom Kippur, My Jewish Learning](https://www.myjewishlearning.com/article/jonah-yom-kippur/)). Matthew Henry held that "though no doubt he did repent, yet, as in Solomon's case, no account is left us of his recovering himself", while Hayes reads the ending as a lesson learned: "Jonah learns that a change of heart is enough to obtain mercy".
- `6` [Enter the Bible, Introductory Issues in Jonah](https://enterthebible.org/courses/jonah/lessons/introductory-issues-in-jonah/) (Luther Seminary): **a fish or a whale?** "The Hebrew of the Book of Jonah actually says he was in the belly of a 'big fish.' It does not provide specificity regarding the kind of fish. The ancient people would not have known of the modern distinction between a fish and an aquatic mammal." The Greek Septuagint calls it "a great sea monster" ([NETS, The Twelve Prophets, translated by George E. Howard](https://ccat.sas.upenn.edu/nets/edition/32-twelve-nets.pdf)), and the WEBBE has "the huge fish" in Jesus' words ([Matthew 12:40](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MAT12.htm#V40)). Calvin held that "either a whale, or a Lamia, or a fish unknown to us, may be able to swallow up a man whole and entire; but he who is thus devoured cannot live in the inside of a fish", so that the book marks Jonah's survival as a miracle ([John Calvin, Commentary on Jonah 1:17, Lecture 76](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/calvin/calcom28.iii.1.xxi.html)). Aron Tillema notes that, "Despite the pressing concerns of modern readers about the feasibility of surviving three days in the digestive system of a fish, this was not a central question for many of Jonah's early interpreters" ([Aron Tillema, Interpretations of Jonah, Bible Odyssey](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/interpretations-of-jonah/)).
