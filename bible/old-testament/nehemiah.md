# Nehemiah

Nehemiah tells how the walls of Jerusalem were rebuilt after the exile, and how the people who had returned renewed their covenant with God. Much of it is told in the first person by Nehemiah, a Jew at the Persian court in Susa: "Now I was cup bearer to the king" ([Nehemiah 1:11](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/NEH01.htm#V11)). When he hears that "The wall of Jerusalem is also broken down", he weeps, fasts and prays, and King Artaxerxes sends him to rebuild the city ([Nehemiah 1:3–4](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/NEH01.htm#V3), [Nehemiah 2:1–8](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/NEH02.htm#V1)). In spite of mockery and threats, the wall is finished "in fifty-two days" ([Nehemiah 6:15](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/NEH06.htm#V15)). Ezra then reads the law to the assembled people, who keep the Feast of Booths, confess their sins and pledge themselves to the law; Jerusalem is resettled and the wall dedicated; and on a second visit Nehemiah enforces reforms on the temple, the Sabbath and marriage. The introduction to the New American Bible calls Ezra "the great religious reformer who succeeded in establishing the Torah as the constitution of the returned community", and Nehemiah, "governor of the province of Judah", "the man of action who rebuilt the walls of Jerusalem and introduced necessary administrative reforms". It dates his first term as governor to 445–432 BC, and judges that "Despite temperamental shortcomings, Nehemiah was a man of good practical sense combined with deep faith in God" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Nehemiah](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/nehemiah/0)).

In the Hebrew Bible Nehemiah stands among the Writings, and it "was originally included as part of the book of Ezra" ([Sefaria, Nehemiah](https://www.sefaria.org/Nehemiah)); "in ancient times the two books were put under the one name, Ezra" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Ezra](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/ezra/0)). Christian Bibles count it among the books "which are called historical", which "tell of the resettlement of the Jews, and of the rebuilding and the reopening of the temple in Jerusalem" ([Orthodox Church in America, The Orthodox Faith: Old Testament, History](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/doctrine-scripture/old-testament/history)). The names under which Ezra and Nehemiah appear in different Bibles, and the story of the return before Nehemiah, are on the [Ezra](ezra.md) page.

The parts told in the first person are known as the "Memoirs of Nehemiah", and "The substantial authenticity of Nehemiah's memoirs is widely accepted" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Nehemiah](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/nehemiah/0)). Who put the book together, and when, is less certain: the Talmud says that "Ezra wrote his own book" ([Sefaria, Bava Batra 15a:2](https://www.sefaria.org/Bava_Batra.15a.2)), while the ESV Global Study Bible holds that Nehemiah "is not the author of the entire book" and that "The same author probably wrote Nehemiah and portions of Ezra" ([ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to Nehemiah](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-nehemiah/)). The questions of authorship and of the order of Ezra's and Nehemiah's missions are set out under open questions below.

## How the churches read it

Where the churches, or their interpreters, read the book differently, or where it has played a part in their history. Each entry starts with its source's score.

- `8` [Pope Francis, Aperuit illis](https://www.vatican.va/content/francesco/en/motu_proprio/documents/papa-francesco-motu-proprio-20190930_aperuit-illis.html) (2019): on **Ezra's reading of the law** ([Nehemiah 8:1–12](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/NEH08.htm#V1)), Pope Francis declared that "the Third Sunday in Ordinary Time is to be devoted to the celebration, study and dissemination of the word of God" (3), and took Nehemiah 8 as the model: the people "had been scattered in exile, but now they found themselves gathered 'as one' around the sacred Scripture". From it he draws the lesson that "The Bible cannot be just the heritage of some, much less a collection of books for the benefit of a privileged few", and that "pastors are primarily responsible for explaining sacred Scripture and helping everyone to understand it" (4–5). On that Sunday in Year C, the **Catholic** lectionary reads Nehemiah 8 with Jesus reading the scroll of Isaiah in the synagogue at Nazareth ([USCCB, Readings for the Third Sunday in Ordinary Time, 26 January 2025](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/readings/012625.cfm), [Luke 4:16–21](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/LUK04.htm#V16)), and the Revised Common Lectionary appoints Nehemiah 8 with the same Gospel passage on the Third Sunday after the Epiphany in Year C ([Vanderbilt Divinity Library, Revised Common Lectionary, Third Sunday after the Epiphany, Year C](https://lectionary.library.vanderbilt.edu/texts/?y=19342&z=e&d=15)). The Presbyterian commentator Matthew Henry, a nonconformist pastor ([Christian Classics Ethereal Library, Matthew Henry](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/henry)), drew this lesson from the same verses: "It is therefore required of those who are teachers by office that they explain the word and give the sense of it." "Reading is good, and preaching good, but expounding brings the reading and the preaching together" ([Matthew Henry, Commentary on Nehemiah 8](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/henry/mhc2.Neh.ix.html)).
- `7` [The Westminster Confession of Faith, 1647, with the American Alterations](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/schaff/creeds3.iv.xvii.ii.html) (Schaff, Creeds of Christendom, Christian Classics Ethereal Library): the **Reformed** Westminster Confession cites Nehemiah 13 among its proof texts. It cites the Sabbath reforms ([Nehemiah 13:15–22](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/NEH13.htm#V15)) for keeping "an holy rest all the day" on the Lord's Day, "the Christian Sabbath" (21.7–21.8); how other churches keep the day is set out in [Worship and the sacraments](../../general/worship.md#open-questions). It cites Nehemiah's rebuke of marriages with foreign women ([Nehemiah 13:25–27](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/NEH13.htm#V25)) for its teaching that "it is the duty of Christians to marry only in the Lord" (24.3). And the text of 1647 cites Nehemiah's reforms in chapter 13 among the proofs that those who spread opinions "contrary to the light of nature, or to the known principles of Christianity" may be "proceeded against by the censures of the Church, and by the power of the Civil Magistrate" (20.4); the American edition "omits and by the power of the Civil Magistrate, also the prooftexts".
- `7` [Orthodox Church in America, Prophet Nehemiah](https://www.oca.org/saints/lives/2026/12/13/609-prophet-nehemiah): the **Orthodox** Church honours Nehemiah as a prophet, and the Orthodox Church in America lists him among the saints commemorated on the Sunday of the Forefathers ([Orthodox Church in America, Lives of all saints commemorated on 13 December 2026](https://www.oca.org/saints/all-lives/2026/12/13)), the Sunday between 11 and 17 December that honours "the ancestors of Christ according to the flesh, who lived before the Law and under the Law" ([Orthodox Church in America, Sunday of the Forefathers](https://www.oca.org/saints/lives/2026/12/13/80-sunday-of-the-forefathers)). Its life of the prophet says that "He and Ezra purified the Jewish people by making known the Law of Moses, and forcing the men to divorce their pagan wives."
- `7` [Catechism of the Catholic Church, 2443–2449](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P8F.HTM): on **debt and interest** ([Nehemiah 5:1–13](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/NEH05.htm#V1)), the New American Bible notes that the nobles' interest was "contrary to the Mosaic law" ([USCCB, Nehemiah, chapter 5](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/nehemiah/5), [Deuteronomy 23:19–20](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/DEU23.htm#V19)), and the **Catholic** Catechism counts the "prohibition of loans at interest and the keeping of collateral" among the Old Testament's "juridical measures" for the poor (2449). Matthew Henry, a **Presbyterian**, concluded from the chapter that "if the poor borrow to maintain their families, and we be able to help them, it is certain we ought either to lend freely what they have occasion for, or (if they be not likely to repay it) to give freely something towards it" ([Matthew Henry, Commentary on Nehemiah 5](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/henry/mhc2.Neh.vi.html)).

## Open questions

Questions on which the churches or scholars disagree, for example about the book's authorship or the meaning of a passage. These guidelines don't pick a side.

- `7` [USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Ezra](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/ezra/0) (New American Bible, Revised Edition): **who put the book together, and when?** The Talmud holds that "Ezra wrote his own book" ([Sefaria, Bava Batra 15a:2](https://www.sefaria.org/Bava_Batra.15a.2)), a book that originally included Nehemiah ([Sefaria, Nehemiah](https://www.sefaria.org/Nehemiah)). The USCCB introduction explains that Ezra and Nehemiah were long held to be the work of "the Chronicler", the author of Chronicles, but that "In recent years, however, the question of the authorship of Ezra and Nehemiah is seen to be more complex": some scholars still maintain one author, others speak of "a 'Chronistic school'". The ESV Global Study Bible holds that "The same author probably wrote Nehemiah and portions of Ezra" ([ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to Nehemiah](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-nehemiah/)). Christine Hayes gives as "our best guess" that Chronicles, Ezra and Nehemiah were all "probably edited in the late fifth century BCE, maybe close to the fourth century" ([Christine Hayes, The Restoration: 1 and 2 Chronicles, Ezra and Nehemiah, Open Yale Courses](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-22)). The New American Bible's notes also find passages out of their original order: chapter 8 "Chronologically ... belongs after Ezr 8:36", and the pact of chapter 10 "is best read after Neh 13:31" ([USCCB, Nehemiah, chapter 8](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/nehemiah/8), [USCCB, Nehemiah, chapter 10](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/nehemiah/10)).
- `7` [Christine Hayes, The Restoration: 1 and 2 Chronicles, Ezra and Nehemiah](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-22) (Open Yale Courses, Introduction to the Old Testament, lecture 22): **who came first, Ezra or Nehemiah, and were they in Jerusalem together?** The text dates Ezra's journey to the seventh year of King Artaxerxes and Nehemiah's to the twentieth, and names them side by side at the reading of the law ([Ezra 7:6–8](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/EZR07.htm#V6), [Nehemiah 2:1](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/NEH02.htm#V1), [Nehemiah 8:9](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/NEH08.htm#V9)). The ESV Global Study Bible follows that order: "Nehemiah arrived in Jerusalem in 445 B.C., 13 years after Ezra arrived" ([ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to Nehemiah](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-nehemiah/)). The USCCB introduction also accepts the order of the text, since "arguments in favor of the chronological priority of Nehemiah to Ezra are indecisive" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Ezra](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/ezra/0)), but its note on chapter 8 finds "no clear evidence of a simultaneous presence of Nehemiah and Ezra in Jerusalem", and treats the mention of Nehemiah in 8:9 as a later gloss ([USCCB, Nehemiah, chapter 8](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/nehemiah/8)). Hayes explains that "It's really not clear who returned first", that there were two kings called Artaxerxes in the fifth century BC and one in the fourth, and that "even the experts cannot agree at all on the sequence of events".
- `7` [Bible Odyssey, Katherine E. Southwood, Intermarriage in Ezra-Nehemiah](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/intermarriage-in-ezra-nehemiah/): **how should Christians read the separation from foreigners and the rejection of mixed marriages?** ([Nehemiah 13:1–3](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/NEH13.htm#V1), [Nehemiah 13:23–27](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/NEH13.htm#V23)) Some Christian readings take the chapter as a warning against marrying outside the faith: Matthew Henry finds in it "a good reason why Christians should not be unequally yoked" ([Matthew Henry, Commentary on Nehemiah 13](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/henry/mhc2.Neh.xiv.html), [2 Corinthians 6:14](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2CO06.htm#V14)), and the ESV Global Study Bible reads the parallel crisis in Ezra as "marriage to idolaters", stressing that "Membership in the covenant community is open to the people of any nation" who accept the covenant ([ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to Ezra](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-ezra/), [Ezra 6:21](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/EZR06.htm#V21)). Southwood instead reads the narrative as one that "betrays increased awareness of ethnic identity among those returning from exile": "In these texts, ethnicity is important", and "a lot of other texts in the Hebrew Bible do not object to intermarriage in the way that Ezra and Nehemiah do". Christine Hayes calls Ezra's ban on marriage with any foreigner "a great innovation" that "was not universally accepted at all", and points to Isaiah 56, where "foreigners who have joined themselves to God are welcome" ([Christine Hayes, The Restoration, Open Yale Courses](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-22), [Isaiah 56:3–7](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ISA56.htm#V3)); the book of [Ruth](ruth.md) tells of a Moabite woman who became an ancestor of David. The dissolution of marriages in Ezra 9–10 is discussed on the [Ezra](ezra.md) page.
