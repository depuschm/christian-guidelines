# Exodus

Exodus is the second of the five books of the Law, which are also called the Pentateuch or the Books of Moses. They open the Old Testament in every church, and they are the Torah of Jewish Scripture ([Thomas Hopko, The Orthodox Faith: Old Testament, Law](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/doctrine-scripture/old-testament/law)). The name Exodus comes from the Greek word for "departure", which the translators of the Septuagint used for the book's central event. Its Hebrew title, Shemoth ("Names"), comes from its opening words, "Now these are the names of the sons of Israel" ([Exodus 1:1](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/EXO01.htm#V1), [USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Exodus](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/exodus/0)). The book continues the story of Genesis. Jacob's descendants are enslaved in Egypt. God calls Moses from a burning bush, reveals his name to him, and brings Israel out of Egypt through the sea. At Mount Sinai God makes a covenant with Israel and gives the Ten Commandments and his law. The people break the covenant by making a golden calf, God renews it, and the people build the tabernacle, where "the LORD's glory filled the tabernacle" ([Exodus 40:34](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/EXO40.htm#V34)). The USCCB introduction says that these events "made Israel a nation", and that many elements of the law given at Sinai "were fundamental to the teaching of Jesus" as well as to Christian moral teaching.

The exodus is told again and again in the rest of the Bible: in the Law, the Prophets and the Psalms, and in the New Testament. Brian M. Britt calls it "a foundational collective memory from ancient Israel", whose later traditions range "from the Jewish observance of Passover to Christian celebrations of Easter, Muslim teachings about the Prophet Musa, and modern liberation theologies" ([Bible Odyssey, The Exodus Tradition in the Bible](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/the-exodus-tradition-in-the-bible/)). Carol Meyers writes that keeping Passover makes all who observe it, "from biblical times to the present", aware of the meaning of the exodus story: "liberation from servitude by a salvific god" ([Bible Odyssey, Passover](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/passover/)).

The book says that Moses wrote down some of what God told him ([Exodus 17:14](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/EXO17.htm#V14), [Exodus 24:4](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/EXO24.htm#V4), [Exodus 34:27](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/EXO34.htm#V27)), and Jesus speaks of "the book of Moses" ([Mark 12:26](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MRK12.htm#V26)). The evangelical ESV Global Study Bible holds that Exodus "is best understood to have been written primarily by Moses", with some details added and some language updated later ([Crossway, ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to Exodus](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-exodus/)). Thomas Hopko's Orthodox introduction reports that "Scholars tell us that the Law was not written by the personal hand of Moses", but grew out of many oral and written traditions, and adds that in the tradition of Israel and of the church "the Law remains essentially connected with Moses" ([Thomas Hopko, The Orthodox Faith: Old Testament, Law](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/doctrine-scripture/old-testament/law)). Many critical scholars, among them Jeffrey Stackert, find four sources combined in the Torah ([Bible Odyssey, Moses](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/moses/)). Who wrote the book, and when and how far its events can be traced in history, are open questions (see below).

## How the churches read it

Where the churches, or their interpreters, read the book differently, or where it has played a part in their history. Each entry starts with its source's score.

- `9` [Baptism, Eucharist and Ministry, Eucharist 1](https://www.oikoumene.org/sites/default/files/Document/FO1982_111_en.pdf) (World Council of Churches, Faith and Order Paper No. 111, 1982): on **the Passover** ([Exodus 12:1–28](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/EXO12.htm#V1)) and **the covenant meal at Sinai** ([Exodus 24:3–11](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/EXO24.htm#V3)), the churches' common text says that "Christians see the eucharist prefigured in the Passover memorial of Israel's deliverance from the land of bondage and in the meal of the Covenant on Mount Sinai", and calls the Eucharist "the new paschal meal of the Church". Paul writes that "Christ, our Passover, has been sacrificed in our place" ([1 Corinthians 5:7](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1CO05.htm#V7)), and the Catechism of the Catholic Church teaches that by celebrating the Last Supper "in the course of the Passover meal, Jesus gave the Jewish Passover its definitive meaning" ([Catechism of the Catholic Church, 1340](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P3Z.HTM)). Carol Meyers notes that the order of the Jewish Passover meal, the seder, was "probably not set until the second century CE", so that the Last Supper was unlikely to have been a seder ([Bible Odyssey, Passover](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/passover/)). See `WOR-3`.
- `9` [Baptism, Eucharist and Ministry, Baptism 2](https://www.oikoumene.org/sites/default/files/Document/FO1982_111_en.pdf) (World Council of Churches, Faith and Order Paper No. 111, 1982): on **the crossing of the sea** ([Exodus 14:21–31](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/EXO14.htm#V21)), the churches' common text counts "an exodus from bondage" among the images of baptism, citing Paul, who writes that Israel "were all baptised into Moses in the cloud and in the sea" ([1 Corinthians 10:1–2](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1CO10.htm#V1)). The Catechism of the Catholic Church teaches that the crossing of the Red Sea "announces the liberation wrought by Baptism" ([Catechism of the Catholic Church, 1221](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P3I.HTM)). At Vespers on Holy Saturday, the Orthodox Church reads fifteen Old Testament lessons, among them "the passover-exodus of the Israelites", and then sings the baptismal verse "As many as have been baptized into Christ have put on Christ" ([Orthodox Church in America, Holy Saturday](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/worship/the-church-year/holy-saturday)).
- `8` [Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith, Instruction on Certain Aspects of the "Theology of Liberation", IV.3 and X.5](https://www.vatican.va/roman_curia/congregations/cfaith/documents/rc_con_cfaith_doc_19840806_theology-liberation_en.html) (1984): on **the exodus as liberation** ([Exodus 3:7–10](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/EXO03.htm#V7), [Exodus 19:3–6](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/EXO19.htm#V3)), the Catholic Church's Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith noted that "theologies of liberation" "make wide use of readings from the book of Exodus", giving "major importance" to the exodus "inasmuch as it is a liberation from political servitude". It taught that the liberation of the exodus "cannot be reduced to a liberation which is principally or exclusively political in nature", because it is "ordered to the foundation of the people of God and the Covenant cult celebrated on Mt. Sinai". Its second instruction calls the exodus the "model and reference" for God's liberating action, with "a meaning which is both religious and political" ([Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith, Instruction on Christian Freedom and Liberation, 44](https://www.vatican.va/roman_curia/congregations/cfaith/documents/rc_con_cfaith_doc_19860322_freedom-liberation_en.html)). An ethic of liberation, as the St Andrews Encyclopaedia of Theology describes it, understands God as "a God of justice" and "a God who is on the side of the poor and marginalized" ([Christian Ethics, 4.5: An ethic of liberation](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Christianity/ChristianEthics#section4.5)).
- `7` [Catechism of the Catholic Church, 2066](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P78.HTM): on **numbering the Ten Commandments** ([Exodus 20:2–17](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/EXO20.htm#V2)), the **Catholic Church** follows "the division of the Commandments established by St. Augustine", which "is also that of the Lutheran confessions", while "the Greek Fathers worked out a slightly different division, which is found in the Orthodox Churches and Reformed communities". So **Luther's Small Catechism** counts "Thou shalt not take the name of the Lord, thy God, in vain" as the second commandment and divides coveting into the ninth and tenth ([Luther's Small Catechism, The Ten Commandments](https://bookofconcord.org/small-catechism/ten-commandments/)), while the **Reformed** Heidelberg Catechism and the **Orthodox** Longer Catechism count the commandment against images as the second ([Heidelberg Catechism, Q&A 92](https://www.crcna.org/welcome/beliefs/confessions/heidelberg-catechism), [Longer Catechism of the Eastern Church, Q. 489](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/schaff/creeds2.vi.iii.iv.html)). William P. Brown notes that in the **Jewish** numbering the first of the ten "words" is God's self-presentation in [Exodus 20:2](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/EXO20.htm#V2), while the Christian traditions begin the series at verse 3 ([Bible Odyssey, The Ten Commandments](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/the-ten-commandments/)).
- `7` [Aidan Hart, The Theology of the Icon, 3.2: The Second Commandment](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Christianity/TheologyoftheIcon#section3.2) (St Andrews Encyclopaedia of Theology): on **images** ([Exodus 20:4–6](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/EXO20.htm#V4)), Hart notes that in the same book God orders cherubim of gold for the ark and cherubim worked into the curtains of the tabernacle ([Exodus 25:18–22](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/EXO25.htm#V18), [Exodus 26:1](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/EXO26.htm#V1)), so that "the commandment against making idols was not against making images as such", and that the Second Council of Nicaea, the seventh ecumenical council, defended icons as made "for the purpose of ascertaining the incarnation of the Word of God". The **Catholic** Catechism teaches that this council "justified against the iconoclasts the veneration of icons", which "is not contrary to the first commandment which proscribes idols" ([Catechism of the Catholic Church, 2129–2132](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P7F.HTM)), and the **Orthodox** Longer Catechism that "it is not in the least contrary to this commandment to honor icons as sacred representations" ([Longer Catechism of the Eastern Church, Q. 516–521](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/schaff/creeds2.vi.iii.iv.html)). The **Reformed** Heidelberg Catechism teaches that "God can not and may not be visibly portrayed in any way", and that images should not be permitted in churches in place of books, because God wants his people taught "by the living preaching of his Word" ([Heidelberg Catechism, Q&A 96–98](https://www.crcna.org/welcome/beliefs/confessions/heidelberg-catechism)). Hart adds that **Luther** "was not opposed to images", while Zwingli and Calvin opposed them ([The Theology of the Icon, 10.3: The Reformation and iconoclasm](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Christianity/TheologyoftheIcon#section10.3)).
- `6` [Herbert R. Marbury, The Legacy of the Bible in Justifying Slavery](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/the-legacy-of-the-bible-in-justifying-slavery/) (Bible Odyssey): in the history of **African American Christianity**, Marbury writes, interpreters such as Absalom Jones, Maria Stewart, Harriet Tubman and Sojourner Truth, faced with the use of the Bible to justify slavery, "took up the Bible, particularly the book of Exodus", and came to "a different truth: because they were enslaved, God would rescue them".

## Open questions

Questions on which the churches or scholars disagree, for example about the book's authorship or the meaning of a passage. These guidelines don't pick a side.

- `7` [John Kaltner, Egypt](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/egypt/) (Bible Odyssey): did the exodus happen as the book tells it, and when? Kaltner notes that "The exodus and Moses are not mentioned in written records from ancient Egypt", and that the only mention of Israel in an Egyptian text is on a stela of Pharaoh Merneptah, who ruled from 1213 to 1203 BC. Andrew Tobolowsky writes that over the last century scholars "have broadly given up on the historicity" of the exodus and the wilderness wanderings ([Bible Odyssey, The Twelve Tribes of Israel](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/the-twelve-tribes-of-israel/)). The ESV Global Study Bible dates the event, while noting "no consensus among scholars as to the date": a common view places it around 1446 BC, counting back 480 years from the building of Solomon's temple ([1 Kings 6:1](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1KI06.htm#V1)), and others in the reign of Ramesses II, around 1260 BC, because Israel builds a city called Raamses ([Exodus 1:11](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/EXO01.htm#V11), [Crossway, ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to Exodus](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-exodus/)). Hopko places the events of the five books "probably" in the second millennium before Christ ([Thomas Hopko, The Orthodox Faith: Old Testament, Law](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/doctrine-scripture/old-testament/law)).
- `7` [Jeffrey Stackert, Moses](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/moses/) (Bible Odyssey): who wrote the book, and how was it put together? Stackert holds that the Torah combines "four literary sources", which he sees in differences within the story, for example over whether Moses or Aaron strikes the Nile. John Huddlestun notes continuing disagreement "over the identity, nature, and date of these sources", and that some scholars argue for the "compositional unity" of the plagues story ([Bible Odyssey, The Plagues of Egypt](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/the-plagues-of-egypt/)). The ESV Global Study Bible holds that Moses wrote the book "primarily" ([Crossway, ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to Exodus](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-exodus/)), and Hopko that, whatever its history, tradition keeps the Law "essentially connected with Moses" ([Thomas Hopko, The Orthodox Faith: Old Testament, Law](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/doctrine-scripture/old-testament/law)).
- `7` [Philip Yoo, Red Sea](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/red-sea/) (Bible Odyssey): where did Israel cross the sea, and where is Mount Sinai? Yoo notes that the Hebrew name of the sea, *yam suph*, cannot mean "red" and may mean "reed", though many English translations, the WEBBE among them, call it the Red Sea ([Exodus 13:18](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/EXO13.htm#V18)). He concludes: "There is no clear answer to the question on whether or not the Red Sea is the sea of the exodus". Benjamin Sommer writes that "Nobody knows where Mount Sinai is" ([Bible Odyssey, Sinai](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/sinai/)), while the ESV Global Study Bible says that "most agree that Mount Sinai is the site that today is called Jebel Musa" ([Crossway, ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to Exodus](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-exodus/)).
