# Genesis

Genesis is the book of beginnings. It opens with "In the beginning, God created the heavens and the earth" ([Genesis 1:1](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/GEN01.htm#V1)), tells of the first human beings, the spread of sin, the flood and the tower of Babel, and then follows one family through four generations: Abraham and Sarah, Isaac, Jacob and Joseph. It ends with Jacob's family in Egypt and Joseph telling his brothers, "you meant evil against me, but God meant it for good" ([Genesis 50:20](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/GEN50.htm#V20)). Its English name comes from the Greek of [Genesis 2:4](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/GEN02.htm#V4), "the book of the generation (genesis) of the heavens and earth", and its Hebrew title, Bereshit, is its first word, "in the beginning". The introduction to the New American Bible divides it into two sections, the creation and spread of the human race and the story of Abraham and his descendants: "The first section deals with God and the nations, and the second deals with God and a particular nation, Israel" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Genesis](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/genesis/0)).

Genesis is the first of the five books of the Pentateuch, or Torah. "Late antique Jewish and Christian authors believed that Moses authored this collection", while most scholars now hold that it is "the result of a long compositional and editorial process, stretching over several centuries" ([Bible Odyssey, The Torah/The Pentateuch](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/the-torah-the-pentateuch/)). The New American Bible's introduction holds that ancient traditions "were edited in the sixth or fifth century B.C. for a Jewish audience that had suffered the effects of the exile" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Genesis](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/genesis/0)). The Orthodox Church in America's catechism reports that "scholars tell us that the Law was not written by the personal hand of Moses", and holds that "in the Tradition of Israel and of the Christian Church, the Law remains essentially connected with Moses" ([Orthodox Church in America, The Orthodox Faith: Law](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/doctrine-scripture/old-testament/law)).

Every church reads Genesis as the first book of the Old Testament. The New Testament returns to it again and again: Jesus quotes it on marriage, John's gospel opens with its first words, and Paul reads Adam as "a foreshadowing of him who was to come" ([Romans 5:14](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ROM05.htm#V14)) and Abraham as the example of faith ([Romans 4:3](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ROM04.htm#V3), [USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Genesis](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/genesis/0)).

## How the churches read it

Where the churches, or their interpreters, read the book differently, or where it has played a part in their history. Each entry starts with its source's score.

- `7` [Catechism of the Catholic Church, 410–411](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P1C.HTM): on **the "first gospel"** ([Genesis 3:15](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/GEN03.htm#V15)), **Catholic** teaching calls the passage the Protoevangelium, or "first gospel": "the first announcement of the Messiah and Redeemer, of a battle between the serpent and the Woman, and of the final victory of a descendant of hers". The Catechism adds that Christian tradition sees in it an announcement of the "New Adam", and that "many Fathers and Doctors of the Church have seen the woman announced in the 'Proto-evangelium' as Mary, the mother of Christ, the 'new Eve'". The **Reformed** Heidelberg Catechism, citing the same verse, teaches that "God began to reveal the gospel already in Paradise", and "finally God fulfilled it through his own beloved Son" ([Heidelberg Catechism, Q&A 19](https://www.crcna.org/welcome/beliefs/confessions/heidelberg-catechism)).
- `7` [Catechism of the Catholic Church, 390](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P1C.HTM): on **the fall** ([Genesis 3](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/GEN03.htm)), the **Catholic Church** teaches that the account "uses figurative language, but affirms a primeval event, a deed that took place at the beginning of the history of man". The **Heidelberg Catechism** traces our corrupt nature to "the fall and disobedience of our first parents, Adam and Eve, in Paradise" ([Heidelberg Catechism, Q&A 7](https://www.crcna.org/welcome/beliefs/confessions/heidelberg-catechism)), and **Orthodox** teaching reads the story as showing that "the origin of evil does not lie in God but in His most perfect creature whose free act of sin brought wickedness and death to the world" ([Orthodox Church in America, The Orthodox Faith: Pre-History](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/doctrine-scripture/salvation-history/pre-history)). The churches describe what humanity inherits from Adam differently; the [Romans](../new-testament/romans.md#how-the-churches-read-it) page sets out Catholic, Lutheran, Reformed and Orthodox teaching on original sin.
- `7` [Orthodox Church in America, The Orthodox Faith: Pre-History](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/doctrine-scripture/salvation-history/pre-history): **Orthodox** teaching calls Genesis 1–11 the "prehistory" of salvation, "almost exclusively the record of sin", and reads it in the light of Christ: "Christ is the True Adam", and his mother Mary "is the new Eve, for she is the true 'mother of all living'" ([Genesis 3:20](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/GEN03.htm#V20)). The church is the true "ark of salvation", and "the events of Pentecost reverse the tragedy of Babel" ([Genesis 11:1–9](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/GEN11.htm#V1)).
- `7` [Orthodox Church in America, The Orthodox Faith: Abraham](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/doctrine-scripture/salvation-history/abraham): on **Abraham as a figure of Christ**, **Orthodox** teaching sees in the testing of Abraham ([Genesis 22:1–19](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/GEN22.htm#V1)) "the original sign that God Himself does what He does not allow the first and foremost of His People to do. No ram is put in the place of God's Son, His only Son Jesus, when He is sacrificed on the cross". In Melchizedek's bread and wine ([Genesis 14:18–20](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/GEN14.htm#V18)) it sees the priesthood of Christ prefigured, and the **Catholic Church** "sees in the gesture of the king-priest Melchizedek, who 'brought out bread and wine,' a prefiguring of her own offering" ([Catechism of the Catholic Church, 1333](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P3Z.HTM)). How the churches understand Christ's priesthood and the ordained ministry is set out on the [Hebrews](../new-testament/hebrews.md#how-the-churches-read-it) page.
- `7` [Orthodox Church in America, The Orthodox Faith: Abraham](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/doctrine-scripture/salvation-history/abraham): on **the three visitors at Mamre** ([Genesis 18:1–15](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/GEN18.htm#V1)), "the Orthodox Church sees the prefiguration of the full revelation of the Holy Trinity", and Orthodox iconography paints the Trinity as the three angels who came to Abraham, most famously in the icon of Saint Andrew Rublev. In the past, Christian interpreters also saw the Trinity in God's words "Let's make man in our image" ([Genesis 1:26](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/GEN01.htm#V26)); most scholars now read the plural as "a 'plural of deliberation'" or as a sign of a divine "council" ([Bible Odyssey, The Book of Genesis](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/the-book-of-genesis/)).

## Open questions

Questions on which the churches or scholars disagree, for example about the book's authorship or the meaning of a passage. These guidelines don't pick a side.

- `7` [Who Wrote the Bible?](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/who-wrote-the-bible/) (Sarah Shectman, Society of Biblical Literature, Bible Odyssey): who wrote Genesis, and when? Jewish and Christian tradition held that Moses wrote the five books of the Pentateuch; Jesus speaks of "the book of Moses" ([Mark 12:26](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MRK12.htm#V26)) and says that Moses "wrote about me" ([John 5:46](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JHN05.htm#V46)). Ligonier Ministries, a Reformed teaching ministry, holds that "there is no good reason to deny the Pentateuch is essentially the work of Moses", who "likely used sources" ([Ligonier Ministries, Mosaic Authorship](https://learn.ligonier.org/devotionals/mosaic-authorship)). Shectman writes that "the books of the Torah bear markers that Moses did not write them", such as stories told twice with conflicting details. The Documentary Hypothesis "stipulates four authors: J, E, D, and P", joined by a later editor, and other models see a more layered growth. In the flood story, for example, Noah takes two of every kind of animal ([Genesis 6:19](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/GEN06.htm#V19)) or seven pairs of every clean animal ([Genesis 7:2](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/GEN07.htm#V2)), which many scholars take as a sign of two traditions woven together ([Bible Odyssey, Two Flood Narratives](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/two-flood-narratives/)). The New American Bible's introduction follows the scholars' view ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Genesis](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/genesis/0)), and the Orthodox Church in America's catechism reports it, while holding that the Law "remains essentially connected with Moses" ([Orthodox Church in America, The Orthodox Faith: Law](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/doctrine-scripture/old-testament/law)).
- `7` [USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Genesis](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/genesis/0): how should the stories of Genesis 1–11 be read? The New American Bible's introduction says that "the stories are neither history nor myth", calls them a "creation-flood story" whose plot "has been borrowed from creation-flood stories attested in Mesopotamian literature", and says that they "provide profound answers to perennial questions about God and human beings". The Catechism of the Catholic Church teaches that the first chapters of Genesis "may have had diverse sources" and express "the truths of creation" ([Catechism of the Catholic Church, 289](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P19.HTM)), and that Scripture presents the work of creation "symbolically as a succession of six days" ([Catechism of the Catholic Church, 337](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P1A.HTM)). Augustine wrote that what kind of light made evening and morning before the sun was made "is beyond the reach of our senses" ([Augustine, City of God, XI.7](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/120111.htm)). The Lutheran Church–Missouri Synod holds that God created the world in "six natural days" ([Lutheran Church–Missouri Synod, Reporter, 24 July 2019](https://reporter.lcms.org/2019/convention-confesses-god-created-the-world-in-six-natural-days/)). [Care for creation](../../general/creation.md#open-questions) sets out how the churches relate the creation accounts to the sciences.
- `7` [The Book of Genesis](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/the-book-of-genesis/) (David Carr and Marc Zvi Brettler, Society of Biblical Literature, Bible Odyssey): were Abraham, Isaac and Jacob historical figures? Brettler writes that to treat Abraham as "a datable historical figure" is "a claim that many, if not most, biblical scholars would contest". The New American Bible's introduction notes that "there is unfortunately no direct extra-biblical evidence confirming (or disproving) the stories", and that a few scholars regard them as "purely fictional", but that their names fit the language of the second millennium BC, and holds that "it is reasonable to accept the Bible's own chronology that the patriarchs were the ancestors of Israel" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Genesis](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/genesis/0)).
