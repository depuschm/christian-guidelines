# Joshua

Joshua is the sixth book of the Old Testament and, in the words of the HarperCollins Bible Dictionary, "the first of the Former Prophets (the historical books of Joshua through 2 Kings)" ([Bible Odyssey, Joshua, The Book of](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/dictionary/joshua-the-book-of/)). It tells how Israel, led by Joshua, the successor of Moses, crossed the Jordan, took the land of Canaan and divided it among the twelve tribes, and it ends with Joshua's farewell and a renewal of the covenant at Shechem ([Joshua 1:1–9](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JOS01.htm#V1), [Joshua 24:1–28](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JOS24.htm#V1)). The introduction to the New American Bible finds its message "unmistakable": "God has been faithful to the promise of the land". The book shows Joshua carrying on the work of Moses, and tells of a crossing of the Jordan "patterned after the crossing of the Red Sea" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Joshua](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/joshua/0)). Moses had given Hoshea son of Nun the name Joshua ([Numbers 13:16](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/NUM13.htm#V16)), and "Joshua and Jesus are two versions of the same name, and for this reason, early Christian interpreters saw Joshua's story as allegorically foreshadowing that of Jesus" ([Bible Odyssey, Zev Farber, Joshua](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/joshua/)).

The book names no author, though it says that Joshua wrote "these words in the book of the law of God" ([Joshua 24:26](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JOS24.htm#V26)). The ESV Global Study Bible notes that the book "does not claim he wrote the book", and that its final writing "may have taken place in the time of the exile (post-587 B.C.), but the writing probably began much earlier" ([ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to Joshua](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-joshua/)). Many scholars read Joshua as part of the Deuteronomistic History, a history running from Deuteronomy to Kings ([Bible Odyssey, Steven L. McKenzie, Deuteronomistic History](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/deuteronomistic-history/)). The New American Bible's introduction holds that the book was written for "a late preexilic audience", to teach "how Israel is to avoid losing the land" rather than to report how Israel took it ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Joshua](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/joshua/0); see "Open questions").

Every church reads Joshua as the first of the historical books of the Old Testament ([Orthodox Church in America, The Orthodox Faith: Old Testament, History](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/doctrine-scripture/old-testament/history)). It is also Jewish Scripture: *Yehoshua* is "the first book of the Prophets" ([Sefaria, Joshua](https://www.sefaria.org/Joshua)). In the New Testament, Stephen recalls that the fathers brought the tabernacle into the land "with Joshua" ([Acts 7:44–45](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ACT07.htm#V44)); Hebrews and James recall the fall of Jericho and Rahab's faith ([Hebrews 11:30–31](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/HEB11.htm#V30), [James 2:25](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JAS02.htm#V25)), and the rest that Joshua did not give ([Hebrews 4:8](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/HEB04.htm#V8)).

## How the churches read it

Where the churches, or their interpreters, read the book differently, or where it has played a part in their history. Each entry starts with its source's score.

- `7` [Justin Martyr, Dialogue with Trypho, 113](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/01288.htm) (New Advent): on **Joshua as a figure of Christ**, early Christian writers read the man who bore the name Jesus as a sign of Christ. Justin Martyr argues that the name was given to Hoshea son of Nun "not purposelessly nor by chance", and that as Joshua, not Moses, led the people into the land, so Christ will give a better inheritance: "the former gave them a temporary inheritance... but the latter, after the holy resurrection, shall give us the eternal possession". Cyril of Jerusalem teaches that "Jesus the son of Nave was in many things a type of Him": Joshua's leadership began at the Jordan, where Christ "after He was baptized, began to preach the gospel", Joshua chose twelve to divide the land as Christ sent twelve apostles, and "The typical Jesus saved Rahab the harlot when she believed" ([Cyril of Jerusalem, Catechetical Lecture 10, 11](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/310110.htm)). The **Reformed** John Calvin saw a pointer to Christ in what the conquest left undone: "The apparent failure reminded the children of God that they were to look forward to a more excellent state", and "Hence their thoughts were raised to Christ" ([John Calvin, Commentary on Joshua, The Argument](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/calvin/calcom07.iii.html)). Hebrews makes a similar point about the rest that Joshua did not give ([Hebrews 4:8–9](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/HEB04.htm#V8)).
- `7` [Catechism of the Catholic Church, 1217–1228](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P3I.HTM): on **the crossing of the Jordan and baptism** ([Joshua 3:14–17](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JOS03.htm#V14)), **Catholic** teaching holds that "Baptism is prefigured in the crossing of the Jordan River by which the People of God received the gift of the land promised to Abraham's descendants, an image of eternal life" (1222). In the **Orthodox Church**, the crossing of the Jordan ([Joshua 3:7–8](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JOS03.htm#V7), [Joshua 3:15–17](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JOS03.htm#V15)) is among the Old Testament readings on the eve of Theophany, the feast of Christ's baptism in the Jordan ([Orthodox Church in America, Scripture Readings for 5 January 2026](https://www.oca.org/readings/daily/2026/01/05)).
- `7` [Clement of Rome, First Letter to the Corinthians, 12](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/1010.htm) (New Advent): on **Rahab** ([Joshua 2:1–21](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JOS02.htm#V1)), the letter of the church of Rome to the church of Corinth, known as First Clement, holds that "On account of her faith and hospitality, Rahab the harlot was saved", and reads the scarlet thread in her window as a sign "that redemption should flow through the blood of the Lord to all them that believe and hope in God". On her lie to the king's men ([Joshua 2:4–5](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JOS02.htm#V4)), Augustine held that God rewarded "not their deceit, but their benevolence": Rahab is to be praised for her hospitality, her faith and her courage, but her lie is "not as meet to be imitated" ([Augustine, To Consentius, Against Lying, 32 and 34](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/1313.htm)). The **Reformed** John Calvin likewise judged of the lie that "though it was done for a good purpose, it was not free from fault", while "Her faith is commended by two Apostles" ([John Calvin, Commentary on Joshua 2:1–24](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/calvin/calcom07.v.i.html)). How the churches read James's words that Rahab was "justified by works" is set out on the [James](../new-testament/james.md) page.

## Open questions

Questions on which the churches or scholars disagree, for example about the book's authorship or the meaning of a passage. These guidelines don't pick a side.

- `7` [Bible Odyssey, Steven L. McKenzie, Deuteronomistic History](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/deuteronomistic-history/): **who wrote Joshua, and when?** The book names no author ([Joshua 24:26](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JOS24.htm#V26)). John Calvin thought it "better to suspend our judgment than to make random assertions", and judged it probable "that a summary of events was framed by the high priest Eleazar, and furnished the materials out of which the Book of Joshua was composed" ([John Calvin, Commentary on Joshua, The Argument](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/calvin/calcom07.iii.html)). The ESV Global Study Bible holds that the writing "probably began much earlier" than its final form, which may come from the exile ([ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to Joshua](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-joshua/)). In 1943 Martin Noth proposed "that Deuteronomy through Kings were originally an extended history of Israel written by a single author/compiler"; McKenzie describes how later scholars divided that history into editions, and how Thomas Römer's view of "a school of court scribes who maintained a library of scrolls that were revised under Josiah, in the exile, and then in the Persian period" has become something of a consensus. The New American Bible's introduction holds that "the fall of the Northern Kingdom in 722/721 B.C. shows its influence throughout" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Joshua](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/joshua/0)).
- `7` [Bible Odyssey, Lorenzo Nigro, Jericho](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/jericho/): **what does archaeology say about the conquest?** The book says both that "Joshua took the whole land" and that "there remains yet very much land to be possessed" ([Joshua 11:23](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JOS11.htm#V23), [Joshua 13:1](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JOS13.htm#V1)), and Judges lists cities that Israel did not take ([Judges 1:27–28](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JDG01.htm#V27)); Zev Farber notes that "in one part of Joshua, Israel conquers the whole land, and in another much of the land remains unconquered" ([Bible Odyssey, Zev Farber, Joshua](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/joshua/)). The ESV Global Study Bible dates the setting of the book to "c. 1406 B.C." and reads its events as "the fulfillment of God's promises to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob" ([ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to Joshua](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-joshua/)). The archaeologist Lorenzo Nigro writes that the ruins of Jericho "are far older than the date of Joshua's conquest" and that "there is no evidence connecting the remains of this impressive city with the Jericho described in Joshua". Ann Killebrew sets out five theories of how Israel emerged in Canaan: "the conquest theory", "the peaceful infiltration theory", "the social revolutionary theory", "the pastoral Canaanite theory" and her own "mixed multitude theory"; she finds that some accounts, such as those of "Mount Ebal and Hazor", "do seem to be corroborated in the archaeological record" ([Bible Odyssey, Ann E. Killebrew, Archaeology and Conquest](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/video-gallery/archaeology-and-conquest/)). The New American Bible's introduction concludes that Israel's emergence was "a slow and piecemeal affair", and calls the book's account a "highly idealized depiction of the 'conquest'" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Joshua](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/joshua/0)).
- `7` [USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Joshua](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/joshua/0) (New American Bible, Revised Edition): **how should Christians read the destruction of the Canaanites?** At Jericho Israel "utterly destroyed all that was in the city, both man and woman, both young and old", and in the south Joshua "utterly destroyed all that breathed, as the LORD, the God of Israel, commanded" ([Joshua 6:21](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JOS06.htm#V21), [Joshua 10:40](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JOS10.htm#V40)). The New American Bible's introduction calls "the wholesale destruction of the Canaanites" "an idealization of the deuteronomic idea that pagans are to be wiped out so they will not be an occasion for apostasy from the Lord", notes that "by the time the book was written, the Canaanites were long gone", and recalls that "the New Testament teaches us to love even our enemies". John Calvin writes that the slaughter "might seem an inhuman massacre, had it not been executed by the command of God", who "had justly doomed those nations to destruction" ([John Calvin, Commentary on Joshua 6:20–27](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/calvin/calcom07.ix.ii.html)). The wider debate, with the positions of scholars and of Benedict XVI, is set out under the open questions of [Deuteronomy](deuteronomy.md#open-questions).
- `7` [USCCB, Joshua, chapter 10, with notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/joshua/10) (New American Bible, Revised Edition): **did the sun stand still?** The account quotes a poem from "the book of Jashar" ([Joshua 10:12–14](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JOS10.htm#V12)). The New American Bible's note calls it an "obscure passage" that "may suppose a longer than natural day caused when the sun stopped moving across the sky, or it may refer to the sun stopping its light-giving function, perhaps through an eclipse. In any case it was seen as a sign that God fought Israel's battle". John Calvin read it as "a more distinguished miracle, in which the omnipotence of God was openly displayed" ([John Calvin, Commentary on Joshua 10:1–14](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/calvin/calcom07.xiii.i.html)).
