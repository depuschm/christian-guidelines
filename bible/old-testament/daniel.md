# Daniel

The Book of Daniel joins two kinds of writing. Its first half tells stories of Daniel and his three companions at the courts of Babylon and Persia, and in its second half Daniel sees visions of the empires of the world and the end of their rule. Sefaria sums it up: "The book's first half, written mostly in Aramaic, includes stories about Daniel, a Jewish advisor to Babylonian kings, and his peers", and "In the book's second half, Daniel describes prophetic visions about the declines of powerful empires and the end of days" ([Sefaria, Daniel](https://www.sefaria.org/Daniel)). The New American Bible notes that at [Daniel 2:4](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/DAN02.htm#V4) "the text switches from Hebrew to Aramaic, which continues through the end of chap. 7; at 8:1, the text switches back to Hebrew" ([USCCB, Daniel, chapter 2, with notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/daniel/2)). The USCCB introduction holds that "Strictly speaking, the book does not belong to the prophetic writings but rather to a distinctive type of literature known as 'apocalyptic'", and that its message is "that history unrolls under the watchful eye of God, who does not abandon those who trust in him and will finally deliver and re-establish them" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Daniel](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/daniel/0)). For the ESV Global Study Bible, "The book's central theme is God's sovereignty over history, empires, and kings" ([Crossway, ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to Daniel](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-daniel/)).

The book is named after its hero, and its visions are told in his voice. The ESV Global Study Bible holds the traditional view that "Daniel wrote this book in the sixth century B.C." ([Crossway, ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to Daniel](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-daniel/)); the Talmud says that "The members of the Great Assembly wrote" it, with Ezekiel, the Twelve Prophets and Esther ([Sefaria, Bava Batra 15a](https://www.sefaria.org/Bava_Batra.15a.2)). The USCCB introduction holds that the author "is actually unknown", and that the book "was composed during the bitter persecution carried on by Antiochus IV Epiphanes (167–164 B.C.) and was written to strengthen and comfort the Jewish people in their ordeal" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Daniel](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/daniel/0)). When it was written is one of the open questions below.

In the Jewish canon Daniel stands among the Writings, between Lamentations and Esther ([Sefaria, Bava Batra 14b](https://www.sefaria.org/Bava_Batra.14b.11)); in the Greek Septuagint and in Christian Bibles it stands among the prophets ([Bible Odyssey, Daniel, The book of](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/dictionary/daniel-the-book-of/)), and Jesus speaks of "Daniel the prophet" ([Matthew 24:15](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MAT24.htm#V15); see [Matthew](../new-testament/matthew.md)). The Greek version of the book has more: a prayer and a song of the three young men in the furnace, and the stories of Susanna and of Bel and the Dragon. Catholic and Orthodox Bibles include them; the New American Bible notes that "The Roman Catholic Church has always regarded them as part of the canonical Scriptures" ([USCCB, Daniel, chapter 3, with notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/daniel/3)), and the Orthodox Church holds that the song "forms a genuine part of the Bible in the Orthodox Church, as do the books of Susanna and Bel and the Dragon, also part of Daniel" ([Orthodox Church in America, The Orthodox Faith: Old Testament, Prophets](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/doctrine-scripture/old-testament/prophets)). The Church of England counts them among the books "the Church doth read for example of life and instruction of manners" but not "to establish any doctrine" ([Church of England, Articles of Religion, VI](https://www.churchofengland.org/liturgy/book-of-common-prayer/articles-religion#VI)), and the Reformed commentator Matthew Henry calls Susanna and Bel and the Dragon "apocryphal stories", "never found in the Hebrew or Chaldee, but only in the Greek" ([Matthew Henry, Commentary on the Whole Bible, Daniel: Introduction](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/henry/mhc4.Dan.i.html)). They have their own page in the [deuterocanon](../README.md#deuterocanon); this page follows the twelve chapters of the Hebrew and Aramaic book.

## Outline

The book has two halves, "stories set at the Babylonian and Persian courts, narrated in the third person" and "apocalyptic revelations, narrated in the first person" ([Bible Odyssey, Daniel, The book of](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/dictionary/daniel-the-book-of/)). The outline follows the WEBBE's numbering. Catholic Bibles print the Greek prayer and song inside chapter 3, so that the WEBBE's 3:24–30 is 3:91–97 in the New American Bible, and its 4:1–3 is 3:98–100 there ([USCCB, Daniel, chapter 3, with notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/daniel/3)).

## How the churches read it

Where the churches, or their interpreters, read the book differently, or where it has played a part in their history. Each entry starts with its source's score.

- `7` [Vatican, Catechism of the Catholic Church, 659–664](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P1T.HTM): **"one like a son of man" and Christ.** At his trial Jesus answers the high priest in the words of Daniel's vision: "You will see the Son of Man sitting at the right hand of Power, and coming with the clouds of the sky" ([Mark 14:61–62](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MRK14.htm#V61); see [Mark](../new-testament/mark.md)). The **Catholic** Catechism teaches that Christ's being seated at the Father's right hand "signifies the inauguration of the Messiah's kingdom, the fulfilment of the prophet Daniel's vision concerning the Son of man", and quotes [Daniel 7:14](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/DAN07.htm#V14). **Orthodox** teaching names as "Central among the book's messages" the "victorious coming of the heavenly Son of Man, who, in the New Testament, is identified with Christ" ([Orthodox Church in America, The Orthodox Faith: Old Testament, Prophets](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/doctrine-scripture/old-testament/prophets)). The **Reformed** commentator Matthew Henry writes that "The Messiah is here called the Son of man", and that "He is like unto the son of man, but is indeed the Son of God" ([Matthew Henry, Commentary on the Whole Bible, Daniel 7](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/henry/mhc4.Dan.viii.html)).
- `7` [Orthodox Church in America, The Three Holy Youths: Ananias, Azarias and Misael](https://www.oca.org/saints/lives/2026/12/17/103560-the-three-holy-youths-ananias-azarias-and-misael): **the three young men in the furnace.** The **Orthodox** Church commemorates the three young men on 17 December, and with the prophet Daniel on the Sunday of the Holy Forefathers and the Sunday of the Holy Fathers before the Nativity, and "The Seventh and Eighth Odes of the nine Biblical Odes at the back of the Psalter are taken from The Song of the Three Holy Youths". It identifies the angel in the fire, the "Angel of Great Counsel", "with the Son of God" ([Orthodox Church in America, Prophet Daniel](https://www.oca.org/saints/lives/2026/12/17/103559-prophet-daniel); [Daniel 3:25](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/DAN03.htm#V25)), and an Orthodox canon to the Mother of God sings that the young men "clearly prefigured" the Virgin birth "as they stood in the midst of the fire and were not burnt" ([St Jonah Orthodox Church, Akathist Canon](https://saintjonah.org/akathists-canons-and-services/akathist-for-lent/akathist-canon/)). The song of the three, "Bless the Lord, all works of the Lord", is in the Greek additions ([deuterocanon](../README.md#deuterocanon)); John Paul II calls it the canticle "which the Liturgy of the Hours proposes for Sunday Lauds in the first and third weeks" in the **Catholic** Church ([Vatican, John Paul II, General Audience of 2 May 2001](https://www.vatican.va/content/john-paul-ii/en/audiences/2001/documents/hf_jp-ii_aud_20010502.html)), and the **Anglican** Book of Common Prayer sets it at Morning Prayer as the Benedicite, which ends "O Ananias, Azarias, and Misael, bless ye the Lord" ([Church of England, Book of Common Prayer: The Order for Morning Prayer](https://www.churchofengland.org/liturgy/book-of-common-prayer/order-morning-prayer)).
- `7` [Vatican, Catechism of the Catholic Church, 992–1004](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P2H.HTM): **the resurrection of the dead.** The **Catholic** Catechism teaches that "God revealed the resurrection of the dead to his people progressively", with a reference to [Daniel 12:1–13](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/DAN12.htm#V1), and that "All the dead will rise, 'those who have done good, to the resurrection of life, and those who have done evil, to the resurrection of judgment'", quoting Jesus' words ([John 5:28–29](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JHN05.htm#V28)) with a reference to [Daniel 12:2](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/DAN12.htm#V2). The New American Bible notes that "Daniel does not envisage the universal resurrection as later developed. Two groups are distinguished" ([USCCB, Daniel, chapter 12, with notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/daniel/12)). Matthew Henry finds in the verse a deliverance from persecution that is "a kind of resurrection" and the awakening that the gospel brings, but concludes: "It must be meant of the general resurrection at the last day" ([Matthew Henry, Commentary on the Whole Bible, Daniel 12](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/henry/mhc4.Dan.xiii.html)). In the **Orthodox** Church the prophecy of Daniel is "read in the Church at the vigil of Easter" ([Orthodox Church in America, The Orthodox Faith: Old Testament, Prophets](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/doctrine-scripture/old-testament/prophets)).
- `7` [Orthodox Church in America, Prophet Daniel](https://www.oca.org/saints/lives/2026/12/17/103559-prophet-daniel): **Daniel's prayer for his people.** The **Orthodox** life of the prophet recalls that he "grieved deeply for his people", and quotes his prayer: "we do not make our supplication before Thee because of our own righteousness, but because of Thy great mercy" ([Daniel 9:18](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/DAN09.htm#V18)). The **Reformed** Heidelberg Catechism cites [Daniel 9:17–19](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/DAN09.htm#V17) for the prayer that pleases God, resting "on this unshakable foundation: even though we do not deserve it, God will surely listen to our prayer because of Christ our Lord" ([Heidelberg Catechism, Q&A 117](https://www.crcna.org/welcome/beliefs/confessions/heidelberg-catechism)).
- `6` [Jerome, Commentary on Daniel](https://www.tertullian.org/fathers/jerome_daniel_02_text.htm) (translated by Gleason L. Archer, 1958, on tertullian.org): **the stone cut out without hands.** Jerome reads the stone of Nebuchadnezzar's dream as Christ: "a rock (namely, the Lord and Savior) was cut off without hands, that is, without copulation or human seed and by birth from a virgin's womb; and after all the empires had been crushed, He became a great mountain and filled the whole earth" ([Daniel 2:34–35](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/DAN02.htm#V34)). He notes that Jewish interpreters of his time applied the stone instead to the people of Israel, and he reads the son of man of chapter 7 as the same person as the stone. The Orthodox life of Daniel reads the kingdom of [Daniel 2:44](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/DAN02.htm#V44) as "the Kingdom of our Lord Jesus Christ" ([Orthodox Church in America, Prophet Daniel](https://www.oca.org/saints/lives/2026/12/17/103559-prophet-daniel)), and Matthew Henry the vision of chapter 7 as "the same with Nebuchadnezzar's foresight of the stone cut out of the mountain without hands" ([Matthew Henry, Commentary on the Whole Bible, Daniel 7](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/henry/mhc4.Dan.viii.html)).

## Open questions

Questions on which the churches or scholars disagree. These guidelines don't pick a side.

- `7` [USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Daniel](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/daniel/0): **when was Daniel written, and do its visions foretell the future or speak to those persecuted under Antiochus IV?** The USCCB introduction holds that the book "was composed during the bitter persecution carried on by Antiochus IV Epiphanes (167–164 B.C.)", and that the stories "bristle with historical problems and have the character of historical novels rather than factual records". Christine Hayes dates chapters 7–12 between 167 and 164 BCE, which makes Daniel "the latest book of the Hebrew Bible", and the stories of chapters 1–6 "perhaps the end of the third century" ([Christine Hayes, Introduction to the Old Testament, Lecture 23, Open Yale Courses](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-23)); Orthodox teaching notes that "Scholars consider this book among the latest written in the Old Testament" ([Orthodox Church in America, The Orthodox Faith: Old Testament, Prophets](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/doctrine-scripture/old-testament/prophets)). The ESV Global Study Bible holds that "Daniel wrote this book in the sixth century B.C.", recording events from 605 to 536 BC ([Crossway, ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to Daniel](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-daniel/)), and in 1908 the *Catholic Encyclopedia* reported that "Catholic scholars generally abide by the traditional view, although they are not bound to it by any decision of the Church" ([Catholic Encyclopedia (1908), Daniel, Book of](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/04621b.htm)). The question is old: the philosopher Porphyry held that the book was written "by some individual living in Judaea at the time of the Antiochus who was surnamed Epiphanes", and Jerome wrote his commentary to answer him ([Jerome, Commentary on Daniel, Prologue](https://www.tertullian.org/fathers/jerome_daniel_02_text.htm)). David M. Valeta sums up: "Scholars debate the dating of the composition of the book of Daniel—as early as the sixth century B.C.E. and as late as the second century B.C.E. are possibilities, and both viewpoints have strong defenders" ([David M. Valeta, Daniel in the Lions' Den, Bible Odyssey](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/daniel-in-the-lions-den/)).
- `7` [Benjamin E. Reynolds, The Son of Man](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/the-son-of-man/) (Bible Odyssey): **who is "one like a son of man"?** The New American Bible notes that "Scholars disagree as to whether this figure should be taken as a collective symbol for the people of God (cf. 7:27) or identified as a particular individual, e.g., the archangel Michael (cf. 12:1) or the messiah", and that "The phrase 'Son of Man' becomes a title for Jesus in the gospels" ([USCCB, Daniel, chapter 7, with notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/daniel/7)). Hayes holds that the figure is "in human form, but more than a human. Probably an angel like Michael or Gabriel" ([Christine Hayes, Introduction to the Old Testament, Lecture 23, Open Yale Courses](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-23)). Reynolds notes that "The three Jewish apocalypses known to interpret this passage all present the son of man figure as a royal or messianic figure who will judge the wicked and redeem the righteous", and that scholars differ on whether Jesus used "the Son of Man" as an ordinary idiom or as "an allusion to the 'one like a son of man' in Dan 7"; he finds the second view "more plausible" ([Daniel 7:13–14](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/DAN07.htm#V13); [Mark 13:26](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MRK13.htm#V26)). The churches read the figure as Christ (see above).
- `6` [Catholic Encyclopedia (1908), Daniel, Book of](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/04621b.htm): **how should the seventy weeks be read?** The *Catholic Encyclopedia* sets out three views of [Daniel 9:24–27](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/DAN09.htm#V24): "the ancient view, which may be called traditional", which refers the prophecy "directly to the appearance of Christ in the flesh, His death, His establishment of the New Covenant, and the destruction of Jerusalem by the Romans"; that of "most recent scholars", who "refer the whole passage directly to the time of Antiochus Epiphanes", with or without a further reference to Christ; and an eschatological view, reaching to "Christ's second Advent". Jerome gave the views of earlier teachers side by side, because "it is unsafe to pass judgment upon the opinions of the great teachers of the Church and to set one above another" ([Jerome, Commentary on Daniel, chapter 9](https://www.tertullian.org/fathers/jerome_daniel_02_text.htm)); Matthew Henry calls the passage "the most illustrious prediction of Christ and gospel-grace that is extant in all the Old Testament" ([Matthew Henry, Commentary on the Whole Bible, Daniel 9](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/henry/mhc4.Dan.x.html)); and the Orthodox life of Daniel holds that it "speaks about the signs of the First and the Second Comings of the Lord Jesus Christ" ([Orthodox Church in America, Prophet Daniel](https://www.oca.org/saints/lives/2026/12/17/103559-prophet-daniel)). The New American Bible reads the "anointed one" who is cut off as "the high priest Onias III, murdered in 171 B.C.", and the last week as "the time of Antiochus' persecution", adding that "Jesus refers to this passage in his prediction of the destruction of Jerusalem in Mt 24:15" ([USCCB, Daniel, chapter 9, with notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/daniel/9)). Dispensationalist teachers read the seventieth week as still to come: Dallas Theological Seminary's doctrinal statement holds that "the translation of the church will be followed by the fulfillment of Israel's seventieth week", a time of tribulation, citing [Daniel 9:27](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/DAN09.htm#V27) ([Dallas Theological Seminary, Doctrinal Statement, Article XIX](https://www.dts.edu/why-dts/doctrinal-statement)). How Christians read the end of the age is set out further on the [Revelation](../new-testament/revelation.md#open-questions) page.
- `6` [Matthew Henry, Commentary on the Whole Bible, Daniel 2](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/henry/mhc4.Dan.iii.html): **which are the four kingdoms?** Henry reads the gold, silver, bronze and iron of the statue as the monarchies of Babylon, the Medes and Persians, the Greeks, and Rome: "it has been the more received opinion that it is the Roman monarchy that is here intended, because it was in the time of that monarchy… that the kingdom of Christ was set up in the world", though "Some make this to signify the latter part of the Grecian monarchy, the two empires of Syria and Egypt" ([Daniel 2:36–45](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/DAN02.htm#V36)). Jerome had written that "the fourth empire, which clearly refers to the Romans, is the iron empire" ([Jerome, Commentary on Daniel, chapter 2](https://www.tertullian.org/fathers/jerome_daniel_02_text.htm)). The New American Bible reads the four as "the Babylonian (gold), the Median (silver), the Persian (bronze), and the Hellenistic (iron)" ([USCCB, Daniel, chapter 2, with notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/daniel/2)), as does Hayes ([Christine Hayes, Introduction to the Old Testament, Lecture 23, Open Yale Courses](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-23)), and notes that the beasts of chapter 7 are taken up in the Book of Revelation, "where it is applied to the Roman empire, the persecutor of the Church" ([USCCB, Daniel, chapter 7, with notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/daniel/7)).

## Tutorials and further reading

- `7` [Christine Hayes, Visions of the End: Daniel and Apocalyptic Literature](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-23) (Open Yale Courses, Introduction to the Old Testament, lecture 23): a university lecture, with a transcript, whose later chapters read Daniel's court stories and visions as apocalyptic literature.
- `7` [Amy C. Merrill Willis, Daniel](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/daniel/) (Bible Odyssey): a short introduction to Daniel's visions of "the end", why the book gives several different calculations of it, and whether Daniel was a historical person.
- `6` [David M. Valeta, Daniel in the Lions' Den](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/daniel-in-the-lions-den/) (Bible Odyssey): faith, politics and civil disobedience in Daniel 6, and how Gandhi and Martin Luther King Jr. read the stories.
- `6` [BibleProject, Daniel](https://bibleproject.com/videos/daniel/): an animated overview of the book, "apocalyptic literature that includes several strange visions that point to the coming Son of Man".
