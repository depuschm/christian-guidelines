# 2 Samuel

2 Samuel tells the story of David's reign. It opens as David hears of the deaths of Saul and Jonathan and laments them. He is then anointed king over Judah at Hebron and later over all Israel, takes Jerusalem, and brings the ark of God into the city ([2 Samuel 2:4](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2SA02.htm#V4), [2 Samuel 5:1–5](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2SA05.htm#V1), [2 Samuel 6:12–15](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2SA06.htm#V12)). Through the prophet Nathan the LORD promises David a dynasty: "Your throne will be established forever" ([2 Samuel 7:16](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2SA07.htm#V16)). The second half of the book tells of David's adultery with Bathsheba, the killing of her husband Uriah and Nathan's rebuke, and of the violence that follows in David's own family, up to the revolt of his son Absalom. Four closing chapters gather further stories, songs and lists from his reign. The introduction to the New American Bible presents David as "the king after the Lord's own heart", and names the theme that unites the books of Samuel: "Israel's God acts justly, prospering those who remain faithful and destroying those who reject his ways" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Books of Samuel](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/1samuel/0)). Christine Hayes explains that Nathan's oracle gives "the idea of an eternal and unconditional covenant between God and the House of David", and that after the fall of the kingdom Israel "looked to the future for a restoration of the Davidic line or Davidic king, or a messiah"; "the Hebrew word messiah simply means anointed" ([Christine Hayes, The Deuteronomistic History: Prophets and Kings (1 and 2 Samuel), Open Yale Courses](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-13)).

1 and 2 Samuel were one book in the Hebrew Bible. Sefaria describes Samuel II as "the second part of the third book of the Prophets" ([Sefaria, II Samuel](https://www.sefaria.org/II_Samuel)). Christian Bibles count it among the books "which are called historical", which follow the Law, and in the Orthodox Church's listing, "which is generally that of the Septuagint", "1 and 2 Samuel are called 1 and 2 Kings, and 1 and 2 Kings are called 3 and 4 Kings" ([Orthodox Church in America, The Orthodox Faith: Old Testament, History](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/doctrine-scripture/old-testament/history)). The story of Samuel, of Saul and of David's rise is told in [1 Samuel](1-samuel.md).

The book names no author. The ESV Global Study Bible holds that "The author or authors of 1 and 2 Samuel are not known" and that the books "were probably written soon after the end of his reign" ([ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to 1–2 Samuel](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-1-2-samuel/)). The USCCB introduction sees the hand of "A Deuteronomistic editor" who shaped older blocks of material, among them a "Succession Narrative" (2 Samuel 9–20 and 1 Kings 1–2), so that the books became "an object lesson for biblical Israel" after the destruction of Jerusalem in 587/586 BC ([USCCB, Introduction to the Books of Samuel](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/1samuel/0)). The debate is set out under open questions below.

## How the churches read it

Where the churches, or their interpreters, read the book differently, or where it has played a part in their history. Each entry starts with its source's score.

- `7` [Catechism of the Catholic Church, 436–440](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P1F.HTM): on **the promise to David and Jesus as Son of David** ([2 Samuel 7:12–16](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2SA07.htm#V12)), the churches read Nathan's oracle in the light of the New Testament, which calls Jesus "the son of David" ([Matthew 1:1](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MAT01.htm#V1)) and proclaims that "From this man's offspring, God has brought salvation to Israel according to his promise" ([Acts 13:22–23](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ACT13.htm#V22)). The **Catholic** Catechism teaches that many "recognized in Jesus the fundamental attributes of the messianic 'Son of David', promised by God to Israel", and that Jesus "accepted his rightful title of Messiah, though with some reserve because it was understood by some of his contemporaries in too human a sense, as essentially political" (439). The **Reformed** Heidelberg Catechism, citing 2 Samuel 7:12–16, teaches that the eternal Son of God took a truly human nature "so that he might also become David's true descendant" ([Heidelberg Catechism, Q&A 35](https://www.crcna.org/welcome/beliefs/confessions/heidelberg-catechism)), and the evangelical ESV Global Study Bible calls 2 Samuel 7 "a turning point in the history of salvation", in which David "becomes the prototype of the future Messiah, Jesus Christ" ([ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to 1–2 Samuel](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-1-2-samuel/)). The **Orthodox** Church honours "The Holy Prophet-King David" as "a forefather of our Lord Jesus Christ according to the flesh", commemorated on the Sunday after the Nativity with Joseph the Betrothed and James the Brother of the Lord ([Orthodox Church in America, Lives of the saints commemorated on 27 December](https://www.oca.org/saints/all-lives/2026/12/27)).
- `7` [Orthodox Church in America, Prophet Nathan](https://www.oca.org/saints/lives/2019/12/15/608-prophet-nathan): on **David's repentance** ([2 Samuel 12:13](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2SA12.htm#V13)), the **Orthodox** Church commemorates Nathan, who "is mentioned in the Prayer of Absolution in the Mystery of Confession: 'It was God Who pardoned David through the Prophet Nathan when he had confessed his sin....'", and teaches that David's life "was darkened by a grievous falling", but that he "was also an example of great repentance" and "gave a model for repentance in Psalm 50/51" ([Orthodox Church in America, Lives of the saints commemorated on 27 December](https://www.oca.org/saints/all-lives/2026/12/27)). The **Catholic** Catechism quotes the same Byzantine formula of absolution, "May the same God, who through the Prophet Nathan forgave David when he confessed his sins..." ([Catechism of the Catholic Church, 1481](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P4H.HTM)), and holds that David's "submission to the will of God, his praise, and his repentance, will be a model for the prayer of the people", citing his prayer in 2 Samuel 7 ([Catechism of the Catholic Church, 2579](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P91.HTM)). Pope John Paul II called Psalm 51 "the most intense and commonly used penitential psalm", which "The Liturgy of the Hours makes us pray ... at Lauds every Friday", and noted that "The Jewish tradition placed the psalm on the lips of David, who was called to repentance by the severe words of the prophet Nathan" ([John Paul II, General Audience on Psalm 50 (51), 24 October 2001](https://www.vatican.va/content/john-paul-ii/en/audiences/2001/documents/hf_jp-ii_aud_20011024.html)). In the **Church of England**'s Book of Common Prayer, the service for the first day of Lent has the priest and clerks kneel and say Psalm 51, "Miserere mei, Deus" ([Church of England, Book of Common Prayer, A Commination](https://www.churchofengland.org/liturgy/book-of-common-prayer/commination)).
- `6` [Pope Francis, The prayer of praise (morning meditation, 28 January 2014)](https://www.vatican.va/content/francesco/en/cotidie/2014/documents/papa-francesco-cotidie_20140128_prayer-praise.html): on **David dancing before the ark** ([2 Samuel 6:14–23](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2SA06.htm#V14)), Pope Francis held David up as "an example of the prayer of praise", "a Christian prayer for all of us", and warned that those who "close themselves into the formality of a cold, measured prayer perhaps end up like Michal, in the sterility of formality".

## Open questions

Questions on which the churches or scholars disagree, for example about the book's authorship or the meaning of a passage. These guidelines don't pick a side.

- `7` [USCCB, Introduction to the Books of Samuel](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/1samuel/0) (New American Bible, Revised Edition): **who wrote the books of Samuel, and when?** The books name no author; 1 Chronicles refers to the acts of David as written "in the history of Samuel the seer, and in the history of Nathan the prophet, and in the history of Gad the seer" ([1 Chronicles 29:29](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1CH29.htm#V29)). The ESV Global Study Bible holds that the books "were probably written soon after the end of his reign" ([ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to 1–2 Samuel](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-1-2-samuel/)). The USCCB introduction instead sees a Deuteronomistic editor who "developed a narrative timeline around freely composed speeches" and shaped older blocks such as the Succession Narrative, and holds that the material "does not present either a continuous history or a systematic account of this period". Steven McKenzie explains that since Martin Noth in 1943 many scholars have read Deuteronomy to Kings as one "Deuteronomistic History", and that they debate how many editions it went through, under King Josiah, in the exile and later, while some deny that it was ever one work ([Bible Odyssey, Steven L. McKenzie, Deuteronomistic History](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/deuteronomistic-history/)).
- `7` [Bible Odyssey, Christopher Rollston, The Tel Dan Inscription](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/the-tel-dan-inscription/): **how much of David's story is history?** An Aramaic inscription from the ninth century BC found at Tel Dan speaks of a king "of the house of David". Rollston writes that although some scholars propose other readings, "the majority of scholars consider 'house of David' to be the only compelling translation", so that the inscription "certainly refers to the Davidic dynasty". Kent Harold Richards calls the link "tantalizing but unproven", and notes that "current scholars debate the very existence and kingship of David, a united monarchy, and David the psalmist" ([Bible Odyssey, Kent Harold Richards, David](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/david/)). The ESV Global Study Bible, by contrast, dates David's reign to about 1010–971 BC ([ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to 1–2 Samuel](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-1-2-samuel/)).
- `7` [Bible Odyssey, Sara Koenig, Bathsheba](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/bathsheba/): **how should Bathsheba's part in chapter 11 be read?** Many readers, John Paul II among them, speak of David's "adultery with Bathsheba" ([John Paul II, General Audience on Psalm 50 (51), 24 October 2001](https://www.vatican.va/content/john-paul-ii/en/audiences/2001/documents/hf_jp-ii_aud_20011024.html)). Koenig notes that the chapter is "especially terse", so that interpreters have described Bathsheba "on the spectrum from passive victim to active seductress"; against the second reading she points out that the text calls her bath an act of purification, places David, not Bathsheba, on the roof, and asks whether "any subject, especially a woman" could "reasonably refuse to come when summoned" by the king ([2 Samuel 11:2–4](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2SA11.htm#V2)). Martien Halvorson-Taylor reads the story as "an abuse of power and a violation of law", and the narrative's silence about Bathsheba's motives as suggesting "that she may not have been a willing participant" ([Bible Odyssey, Martien A. Halvorson-Taylor, David's Downfall](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/davids-downfall/)); Cameron Howard sees in Nathan's parable that "the powerful exploit the powerless", as Samuel warned that a king would take from his people ([Bible Odyssey, Cameron B. R. Howard, David and Bathsheba](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/david-and-bathsheba/), [1 Samuel 8:11–18](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1SA08.htm#V11)).
- `6` [Iain Duguid, Who Called David's Census—God or Satan? (Ligonier Ministries)](https://learn.ligonier.org/articles/davids-census): **who moved David to count the people?** 2 Samuel says that the LORD's anger burnt against Israel, "and he moved David against them" ([2 Samuel 24:1](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2SA24.htm#V1)), while 1 Chronicles says that "Satan stood up against Israel, and moved David to take a census of Israel" ([1 Chronicles 21:1](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1CH21.htm#V1)). Duguid, who teaches Old Testament at Westminster Theological Seminary, reads the two as complementary: "the Lord is ultimately in control of all temptations that come to us", and, as Luther put it, the devil is "God's devil". The commentary of Luther Seminary's Enter the Bible notes that "The Chronicler is usually thought to have altered the story from 2 Samuel to avoid attributing evil to God", finds that view "somewhat forced", and points out that the Hebrew word satan "usually means a human enemy or adversary in the Old Testament" ([Enter the Bible, 1 Chronicles 21:1–22:1, David's Census](https://enterthebible.org/passage/1-chronicles-211-221-davids-census/)).
