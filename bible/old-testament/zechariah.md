# Zechariah

The Book of Zechariah opens "In the eighth month, in the second year of Darius", when "the LORD's word came to the prophet Zechariah the son of Berechiah, the son of Iddo" ([Zechariah 1:1](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ZEC01.htm#V1)), in 520 BC. With Haggai, he urged the people who had come back from exile to rebuild the temple in Jerusalem: "The elders of the Jews built and prospered, through the prophesying of Haggai the prophet and Zechariah the son of Iddo" ([Ezra 5:1](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/EZR05.htm#V1), [Ezra 6:14](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/EZR06.htm#V14); see the [Ezra](ezra.md) and [Haggai](haggai.md) pages). The USCCB introduction calls "The most striking feature of First Zechariah" "a series of visions in which the prophet describes the centrality of Jerusalem, its Temple, and its leaders", visions that "clearly relate to the Temple restoration begun in 520 B.C." ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Zechariah](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/zechariah/0)). After a call to return to God come eight night visions, the crowning of the high priest Joshua, and an answer to a question about fasting ([Zechariah 1:1–6](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ZEC01.htm#V1), [Zechariah 7:1–7](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ZEC07.htm#V1)). Chapters 9–14 are two collections, each headed "A revelation" ([Zechariah 9:1](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ZEC09.htm#V1), [Zechariah 12:1](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ZEC12.htm#V1)), which in the USCCB's words "maintain the hope of previous prophets by depicting a glorious eschatological restoration". The Reformed commentator Matthew Henry writes that Zechariah "soared higher in visions and revelations, wrote more, and prophesied more particularly concerning Christ, than Haggai had done" ([Matthew Henry, Commentary on Zechariah: Introduction](https://www.ccel.org/h/henry/mhc2/MHC38000.HTM)).

The Orthodox Church in America's life of the prophet holds that he "was descended from the tribe of Levi, and seems to have been a priest" ([Orthodox Church in America, Lives of all saints commemorated on February 8: Prophet Zachariah the Sickle-Seer](https://www.oca.org/saints/all-lives/2026/02/08); [Nehemiah 12:16](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/NEH12.htm#V16)), and the ESV Global Study Bible that "Zechariah was a prophet and a priest. He began his ministry in 520 B.C." ([ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to Zechariah](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-zechariah/)). The Talmud counts the Twelve Prophets among the books that "The members of the Great Assembly wrote" ([Sefaria, Bava Batra 15a:2](https://www.sefaria.org/Bava_Batra.15a.2)). Most scholars now see the book, "because of its great variation in style, content, and language", as "a composite work", "attributed to at least two different prophets": the prophecies of chapters 1–8 "can be dated to the late sixth century B.C.", and those of "Second Zechariah" (9–14) come "nearly a century later" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Zechariah](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/zechariah/0)). Whether the whole book is Zechariah's is one of the open questions below.

Zechariah is Jewish Scripture: "The Book of Zechariah is the eleventh of the Tere Asar (Twelve Minor Prophets)" ([Sefaria, Zechariah](https://www.sefaria.org/Zechariah)). The Talmud explains that the twelve are written "in one book of the Twelve Prophets", and that "Haggai, Zechariah, and Malachi were the last of the prophets" ([Sefaria, Bava Batra 14b:9](https://www.sefaria.org/Bava_Batra.14b.9)). Chapter 14 is read in the synagogue on the first day of Sukkot, the feast of booths: "Recitation of Zechariah 14 on the first day of Sukkot is ordained in [the Talmud,] Megillah 31a" ([Michael Fishbane, How Zechariah Relates to Sukkot, My Jewish Learning](https://www.myjewishlearning.com/article/how-zechariah-relates-to-sukkot/); [Zechariah 14:16–19](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ZEC14.htm#V16)). Zechariah is also eleventh of the Twelve in the Greek Septuagint, the Old Testament of the Orthodox churches, whose text of the book is "reasonably close to the MT", the traditional Hebrew text ([NETS, The Twelve Prophets, translated by George E. Howard](https://ccat.sas.upenn.edu/nets/edition/32-twelve-nets.pdf)). The gospels quote the second half of the book in their accounts of Jesus' last days in Jerusalem, as the sections below set out.

## How the churches read it

Where the churches, or their interpreters, read the book differently, or where it has played a part in their history. Each entry starts with its source's score.

- `7` [Catechism of the Catholic Church, 512–560](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P1L.HTM): **the lowly king and Palm Sunday.** Matthew and John quote [Zechariah 9:9](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ZEC09.htm#V9) when Jesus rides into Jerusalem: "Tell the daughter of Zion, behold, your King comes to you, humble, and riding on a donkey" ([Matthew 21:4–5](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MAT21.htm#V4), [John 12:14–15](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JHN12.htm#V14); see the [Matthew](../new-testament/matthew.md) and [John](../new-testament/john.md) pages). The **Catholic** Catechism, citing the verse, teaches that the "King of glory" enters his City "riding on an ass", and that "Jesus conquers the Daughter of Zion, a figure of his Church, neither by ruse nor by violence, but by the humility that bears witness to the truth" (559). The Catholic lectionary reads 9:9–10 on the Fourteenth Sunday in Ordinary Time, Year A, with Jesus' prayer of thanks in [Matthew 11:25–30](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MAT11.htm#V25) ([USCCB, Readings for the Fourteenth Sunday in Ordinary Time (5 July 2026)](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/readings/070526.cfm)); the **Orthodox** Church reads 9:9–15 at Vespers of Palm Sunday ([Orthodox Church in America, Scripture Readings for 5 April 2026 (Palm Sunday)](https://www.oca.org/readings/daily/2026/04/05)); and the Revised Common Lectionary reads 9:9–12 in Year A ([Vanderbilt Divinity Library, Revised Common Lectionary: Sunday citations](https://lectionary.library.vanderbilt.edu/sunday-citations/)). The **Reformed** reformer John Calvin reads the verse as a promise of Christ, for "the hope of the ancient people, as our hope, was founded on Christ" ([John Calvin, Commentary on Zechariah 9:9](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/calvin/calcom30.iii.x.xi.html)).
- `7` [Augustine, The Harmony of the Gospels, Book III, chapter 7](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/1602307.htm) (New Advent): **the thirty pieces of silver.** Judas is paid "thirty pieces of silver" ([Matthew 26:14–16](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MAT26.htm#V14)), and when the priests buy the potter's field with them, Matthew gives the words of [Zechariah 11:12–13](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ZEC11.htm#V12) as "spoken through Jeremiah the prophet" ([Matthew 27:9–10](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MAT27.htm#V9); see the [Jeremiah](jeremiah.md) page). Augustine grants that "this passage is not found in the writings of the prophet Jeremiah", and that some copies give no name, but finds the name in "the more ancient Greek exemplars"; he suggests that "the word Jeremiah occurred to his mind, in accordance with a familiar experience, instead of Zechariah", and that Matthew left it to show "that the prophets maintain so complete a harmony with each other in the matter of their utterances". The **Catholic** Encyclopedia (1912) holds instead that Matthew "refers to the fulfilment of two prophecies, that of Jeremias (xxxii, 6-9) about the purchase of the potter's field and that of Zacharias (xi, 12, 13) about the thirty pieces of silver" ([Catholic Encyclopedia (1912), Zacharias](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/15741b.htm); [Jeremiah 32:6–9](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JER32.htm#V6)). The **Reformed** commentator Matthew Henry holds that "The prophet here is made a type of Christ" ([Matthew Henry, Commentary on Zechariah 11](https://www.ccel.org/h/henry/mhc2/MHC38011.HTM)), and the **Orthodox** Church reads [Zechariah 11:10–13](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ZEC11.htm#V10) among the readings of Great and Holy Friday ([Orthodox Church in America, Scripture Readings for 10 April 2026 (Great and Holy Friday)](https://www.oca.org/readings/daily/2026/04/10)).
- `7` [Catechism of the Catholic Church, 1430–1433](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P4A.HTM): **"They will look to me whom they have pierced".** John sees [Zechariah 12:10](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ZEC12.htm#V10) fulfilled when a soldier pierces the side of the dead Jesus: "They will look on him whom they pierced" ([John 19:37](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JHN19.htm#V37)), and Revelation applies it to his coming: "every eye will see him, including those who pierced him" ([Revelation 1:7](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/REV01.htm#V7); see the [John](../new-testament/john.md) and [Revelation](../new-testament/revelation.md) pages). The **Catholic** Catechism, citing both verses, teaches that "the human heart is converted by looking upon him whom our sins have pierced" (1432). Matthew Henry, a **Reformed** commentator, applies the verse to every sinner: "it is true of us all as sinners, we have pierced Christ, inasmuch as our sins were the cause of his death" ([Matthew Henry, Commentary on Zechariah 12](https://www.ccel.org/h/henry/mhc2/MHC38012.HTM)). The **Orthodox** life of the prophet counts "the piercing of the Savior's side (Zach 12:10)" among his prophecies ([Orthodox Church in America, Lives of all saints commemorated on February 8: Prophet Zachariah the Sickle-Seer](https://www.oca.org/saints/all-lives/2026/02/08)). Other readings of the verse are set out in the open questions below.
- `7` [Justin Martyr, Dialogue with Trypho, chapters 109–124](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/01288.htm) (New Advent): **Joshua the high priest and the Branch.** Justin writes the high priest's name as "Jesus (Joshua)", and tells Trypho, "you ought to believe Zechariah when he shows in parable the mystery of Christ"; he reads the filthy garments taken from Joshua ([Zechariah 3:1–5](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ZEC03.htm#V1)) as the sins taken from "us who believe in Christ the High Priest" (chapters 115–116). The **Catholic** Catechism, citing [Zechariah 4:14](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ZEC04.htm#V14) and [Zechariah 6:13](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ZEC06.htm#V13), teaches that "It was necessary that the Messiah be anointed by the Spirit of the Lord at once as king and priest, and also as prophet" ([Catechism of the Catholic Church, 436–440](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P1F.HTM)); the **Orthodox** life of the prophet finds in [Zechariah 6:12](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ZEC06.htm#V12) "inspired details about the coming of the Messiah" ([Orthodox Church in America, Lives of all saints commemorated on February 8: Prophet Zachariah the Sickle-Seer](https://www.oca.org/saints/all-lives/2026/02/08)). Christine Hayes reads the passage historically: the visions "focus hope on Zerubabbel, the governor, and on the priest Joshua", and chapter 6 "seems to refer originally to Zerubabbel" but "is altered so that it now depicts Joshua as a shoot or a branch" ([Christine Hayes, Introduction to the Old Testament, Lecture 23, Open Yale Courses](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-23)).
- `7` [Orthodox Church in America, Lives of all saints commemorated on February 8: Prophet Zachariah the Sickle-Seer](https://www.oca.org/saints/all-lives/2026/02/08): **the Orthodox commemoration and the struck shepherd.** The **Orthodox** Church keeps the feast of "the Prophet Zachariah the Sickle-Seer" on 8 February. Its life calls him, "in the wondrous expression of church hymnology, 'a spectator of supra-worldly visions'", and counts among his prophecies "the scattering of the apostles from the Garden of Gethsemane (Zach 13:7)" and "the eclipse of the sun at the time of the Crucifixion (Zach 14:6-7)". Jesus quotes [Zechariah 13:7](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ZEC13.htm#V7) on the way to Gethsemane: "I will strike the shepherd, and the sheep will be scattered" ([Mark 14:27](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MRK14.htm#V27); see the [Mark](../new-testament/mark.md) page); the **Catholic** New American Bible notes that in Matthew's Gospel "Jesus makes use of this text before his arrest in the Garden of Gethsemane and the flight of the disciples" ([USCCB, Zechariah, chapter 13, with notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/zechariah/13); [Matthew 26:31](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MAT26.htm#V31)). The title "Sickle-Seer" "comes from a vision in which he saw a sickle flying in the air, destroying thieves and perjurors (Zach 5:1-3)": the Greek Septuagint reads "a flying sickle" where the Hebrew has "a flying scroll" ([NETS, The Twelve Prophets, translated by George E. Howard](https://ccat.sas.upenn.edu/nets/edition/32-twelve-nets.pdf); [Zechariah 5:1–4](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ZEC05.htm#V1)).

## Open questions

Questions on which the churches or scholars disagree. These guidelines don't pick a side.

- `7` [USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Zechariah](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/zechariah/0) (New American Bible, Revised Edition): **one prophet or two?** The USCCB introduction holds that the book is "widely believed to be a composite work", "attributed to at least two different prophets", with Second Zechariah ([Zechariah 9:1–8](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ZEC09.htm#V1) onwards) "nearly a century later". Christine Hayes calls chapters 9–14 "obscure writings from a later hand", "probably fifth-century or later" ([Christine Hayes, Introduction to the Old Testament, Lecture 23, Open Yale Courses](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-23)). The **Catholic** Encyclopedia of 1912 notes that "It is generally allowed that Zacharias is the author of the first part" and that the debate over chapters 9–14 began with Joseph Mede in 1653, who, "Wishing to save from error Matthew 27:9", "attributed the latter portion of Zacharias to Jeremias"; it defends "The traditional view taken by Catholic exegetes on the unity of authorship of the book", since "both in Judaism and the Church there has never been a serious doubt in the matter" ([Catholic Encyclopedia (1912), Zacharias](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/15741b.htm)). Matthew Henry reads chapters 9–14 as two sermons of Zechariah "which probably were preached some time after" the visions ([Matthew Henry, Commentary on Zechariah: Introduction](https://www.ccel.org/h/henry/mhc2/MHC38000.HTM)).
- `7` [USCCB, Zechariah, chapter 12, with notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/zechariah/12) (New American Bible, Revised Edition): **who is the pierced one?** The WEBBE has "They will look to me whom they have pierced; and they shall mourn for him" ([Zechariah 12:10](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ZEC12.htm#V10)); the NRSVUE has "when they look on the one whom they have pierced", with the note that the Hebrew reads "on me" ([Bible Gateway, Zechariah 1–14 (NRSVUE)](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Zechariah%201-14&version=NRSVUE)); and the Greek Septuagint reads "they have danced" for "they have pierced" ([NETS, The Twelve Prophets, translated by George E. Howard](https://ccat.sas.upenn.edu/nets/edition/32-twelve-nets.pdf)). The **Catholic** New American Bible notes that "the victim is an enigmatic figure, perhaps referring to a Davidic descendant, a priestly leader, or even a true prophet", that "Some historical event, unknown to us from any surviving source, may underlie this reference", and that "The Gospel of John applies this text to the piercing of Christ's side after his death". The churches read the verse of Christ, as set out above. In the Talmud, "Rabbi Dosa and the Rabbis disagree": one said that the mourning "is for Messiah ben Yosef who was killed in the war of Gog from the land of Magog prior to the ultimate redemption with the coming of Messiah ben David", "And one said that this eulogy is for the evil inclination that was killed" ([Sefaria, Sukkah 52a:2](https://www.sefaria.org/Sukkah.52a.2)), and the first view reads 12:10 as its proof ([Sefaria, Sukkah 52a:3](https://www.sefaria.org/Sukkah.52a.3)).
- `7` [USCCB, Zechariah, chapter 14, with notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/zechariah/14) (New American Bible, Revised Edition): **how should the war of chapter 14 be read?** The New American Bible calls the chapter an "apocalyptic description, with its astonishing images of the day of the Lord", and the LORD's feet on the Mount of Olives "a remarkable anthropomorphic image" that "adds emphasis to the traditional Old Testament scene of God appearing on a mountain" ([Zechariah 14:1–5](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ZEC14.htm#V1)); Christine Hayes reads it as "a vision of this global battle that will bring history to an end" ([Christine Hayes, Introduction to the Old Testament, Lecture 23, Open Yale Courses](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-23)). John Calvin holds that the prophet "does not here promise a miracle, such as even the ignorant might conceive to be literal", for "This has never happened, that mount has never been rent" ([John Calvin, Commentary on Zechariah 14:4](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/calvin/calcom30.iii.xv.iv.html)). Matthew Henry finds the verses "dark and hard to be understood", and reads them of the fall of Jerusalem and the church, in which "The partition-wall between Jews and Gentiles shall be taken away" ([Matthew Henry, Commentary on Zechariah 14](https://www.ccel.org/h/henry/mhc2/MHC38014.HTM)). Others read the chapter as events still to come: the evangelical ministry Got Questions holds that "Since none of this has taken place yet, the prophecy points to a future time", the second coming of Christ, and links it with the battle of Revelation 19 ([Got Questions Ministries, Is Zechariah 14:4 a prophecy about the second coming of Christ?](https://www.gotquestions.org/Zechariah-14-4-second-coming.html)). The wider question of how to read the visions of the end is set out on the [Revelation](../new-testament/revelation.md) and [Daniel](daniel.md) pages.

## Tutorials and further reading

- `7` [Christine Hayes, Visions of the End: Daniel and Apocalyptic Literature](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-23) (Open Yale Courses, Introduction to the Old Testament, lecture 23): a university lecture, with a transcript, that covers Haggai and the visions of First Zechariah, and then the apocalyptic chapters of Second Zechariah.
- `6` [Michael Fishbane, How Zechariah Relates to Sukkot](https://www.myjewishlearning.com/article/how-zechariah-relates-to-sukkot/) (My Jewish Learning): why Zechariah 14 is read in the synagogue on the first day of the feast of booths, and its link with prayers for rain.
- `6` [Zechariah, The Book of](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/dictionary/zechariah-the-book-of/) (HarperCollins Bible Dictionary, on Bible Odyssey): a short introduction with an outline of the eight visions and the two collections of oracles.
- `6` [BibleProject, Zechariah](https://bibleproject.com/videos/zechariah/): an animated overview of a book that "contains a series of dream visions filled with bizarre images".
