# Jeremiah

The Book of Jeremiah "combines history, biography, and prophecy. It portrays a nation in crisis and introduces the reader to an extraordinary person whom the Lord called to prophesy under the trying circumstances of the final days of the kingdom of Judah" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Jeremiah](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/jeremiah/0)). Jeremiah, the son of Hilkiah, "one of the priests who were in Anathoth", received the LORD's word from the thirteenth year of King Josiah "to the carrying away of Jerusalem captive" ([Jeremiah 1:1–3](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JER01.htm#V1)). He warned that Babylon would take the city, and "Arrest, imprisonment, and public disgrace were his lot". After Jerusalem fell in 587 BC, "The prophet remained in Jerusalem, but was later forced into Egyptian exile. We do not know the details of his death" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Jeremiah](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/jeremiah/0); [Jeremiah 43:4–7](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JER43.htm#V4)). Orthodox teaching calls him "a most reluctant prophet" who "suffered greatly at the hands of the people" ([Orthodox Church in America, The Orthodox Faith: Old Testament, Prophets](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/doctrine-scripture/old-testament/prophets)), and Brian Rainey notes that the book is "our only source of information about him" ([Brian Rainey, The Prophet Jeremiah, Bible Odyssey](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/the-prophet-jeremiah/)).

The book tells how it was written down. Jeremiah dictated his words to his scribe Baruch; King Jehoiakim cut the scroll up and burned it, and Jeremiah dictated it again, "and many similar words were added to them" ([Jeremiah 36:1–4](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JER36.htm#V1), [Jeremiah 36:21–23](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JER36.htm#V21), [Jeremiah 36:32](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JER36.htm#V32)). The Talmud holds that "Jeremiah wrote his own book, and the book of Kings, and Lamentations" ([Sefaria, Bava Batra 15a:2](https://www.sefaria.org/Bava_Batra.15a.2)). Christine Hayes describes three kinds of material in the book: poetic oracles "that generally are attributed to Jeremiah", narratives about him "attributed to his amanuensis and assistant", Baruch, and "editorial notes about Jeremiah that are in the style of the Deuteronomistic historian" ([Christine Hayes, Introduction to the Old Testament, Lecture 18, Open Yale Courses](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-18)). The USCCB introduction notes that "In the postexilic period, the Book of Jeremiah circulated in various editions" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Jeremiah](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/jeremiah/0)), and the Greek Septuagint text is about one-eighth shorter than the Hebrew and places the oracles against the nations elsewhere ([Catholic Encyclopedia (1910), Jeremias](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/08334a.htm)). How the book came together is one of the open questions below.

Jeremiah is Jewish Scripture: *Yirmiyahu* "is the sixth book of the Prophets, with prophecies from the period leading up to the First Temple's destruction" ([Sefaria, Jeremiah](https://www.sefaria.org/Jeremiah)). In Christian Bibles it is the second of the four major prophets, "Isaiah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel and Daniel", and "The books of Baruch and the Letter of Jeremiah from the apocrypha go together with this prophetic book in the Orthodox version of the Bible" ([Orthodox Church in America, The Orthodox Faith: Old Testament, Prophets](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/doctrine-scripture/old-testament/prophets)); Catholic Bibles also include them, with the letter as the sixth chapter of Baruch ([Catholic Encyclopedia (1910), Jeremias](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/08334a.htm); [deuterocanon](../README.md#deuterocanon)). [Lamentations](lamentations.md), traditionally ascribed to Jeremiah, follows it. Every church reads it.

## How the churches read it

Where the churches, or their interpreters, read the book differently, or where it has played a part in their history. Each entry starts with its source's score.

- `7` [Catechism of the Catholic Church, 1965](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P6W.HTM): **the new covenant** ([Jeremiah 31:31–34](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JER31.htm#V31)). At the Last Supper Jesus says, "This cup is the new covenant in my blood" ([Luke 22:20](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/LUK22.htm#V20), [1 Corinthians 11:25](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1CO11.htm#V25)), and Hebrews quotes Jeremiah's promise as fulfilled in Christ ([Hebrews](../new-testament/hebrews.md)). The **Catholic** Catechism teaches that the New Law of the Gospel "is also the work of the Holy Spirit and through him it becomes the interior law of charity", and quotes the promise to "write them on their hearts". The **Reformed** reformer John Calvin held that "this passage necessarily refers to the kingdom of Christ", but that the covenant "is not so called, because it is contrary to the first covenant; for God is never inconsistent with himself" ([John Calvin, Commentary on Jeremiah 31:31–32](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/calvin/calcom20.iii.xl.html)), and the evangelical ESV Global Study Bible that "Hebrews 8:8–12 quotes Jer. 31:31–34 as evidence that the new covenant has come through the life, death, and resurrection of Jesus Christ" ([ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to Jeremiah](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-jeremiah/)). Walter Brueggemann notes that "Christians quickly took the 'new covenant' to be a reference to the graciousness of God given in Jesus", while "In its biblical context … the oracle in the context of Jeremiah pertains exactly to the crisis among Israelites in the sixth century B.C.E." ([Walter Brueggemann, A New Covenant, Bible Odyssey](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/a-new-covenant/)). How the churches understand God's covenant with the Jewish people today is discussed on the [Romans](../new-testament/romans.md#how-the-churches-read-it) page.
- `7` [Cyril of Jerusalem, Catechetical Lecture 13, 19](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/310113.htm): **Jeremiah and the Passion.** Asked to "show me from some prophet the Wood of the Cross", Cyril answers, "Hear then from Jeremias": "I was like a harmless lamb led to be slaughtered", and reads the words "let us place a beam upon His bread" as a prophecy of the cross ([Jeremiah 11:19](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JER11.htm#V19)). The **Orthodox** Church reads Jeremiah 11:18–12:5, with verses from the rest of chapter 12, on Great and Holy Friday ([Orthodox Church in America, Scripture Readings for Great and Holy Friday, 10 April 2026](https://www.oca.org/readings/daily/2026/04/10)), and the **Catholic** Church reads Jeremiah 11:18–20 on the Saturday of the fourth week of Lent, with John 7:40–53 ([USCCB, Readings for Saturday of the Fourth Week of Lent, 21 March 2026](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/readings/032126.cfm); [Jeremiah 11:18–20](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JER11.htm#V18), [John 7:40–53](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JHN07.htm#V40)). Whether the verse speaks of Christ is one of the open questions below.
- `7` [Orthodox Church in America, Prophet Jeremiah](https://www.oca.org/saints/lives/2026/05/01/101254-prophet-jeremiah): **the Orthodox commemoration.** The **Orthodox** Church commemorates the Prophet Jeremiah on 1 May. Its life of him holds that he "was called to prophetic service at the age of fifteen", that "According to Tradition, the Prophet Jeremiah took the Ark of the Covenant with the Tablets of the Law and hid it in one of the caves of Mount Nabath (Nebo)", as 2 Maccabees tells ([deuterocanon](../README.md#deuterocanon)), and that he "wrote his Book of Prophecies and also the Book of Lamentations". It notes that Matthew ascribes to Jeremiah the prophecy of the thirty pieces of silver ([Matthew 27:9–10](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MAT27.htm#V9)): "Perhaps Jeremiah 32:6-15 is meant."
- `6` [John Calvin, Commentary on Jeremiah 29:7](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/calvin/calcom19.xii.v.html) (Christian Classics Ethereal Library): **the letter to the exiles** ([Jeremiah 29:4–7](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JER29.htm#V4)). Calvin read the command as a call for the exiles "to be faithful subjects of their conqueror", and to "pray to God, that all things might turn out prosperously and happily to the Babylonian king". The churches' teaching on taking part in public life cites the same verse beside the Catholic Catechism, the Augsburg Confession and the Westminster Confession (`CIV-6` in [Public life](../../general/public-life.md)).
- `6` [USCCB, Readings for the First Sunday of Advent, 1 December 2024](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/readings/120124.cfm): **the righteous Branch and Advent** ([Jeremiah 33:14–16](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JER33.htm#V14)). The **Catholic** lectionary and the Revised Common Lectionary, used by many Protestant churches, both read Jeremiah 33:14–16, "I will raise up for David a just shoot", on the First Sunday of Advent in Year C ([Vanderbilt Divinity Library, Revised Common Lectionary: Sunday citations](https://lectionary.library.vanderbilt.edu/sunday-citations/)). Calvin taught that "Christ is justly called a righteous Branch", and that he bears the name of the LORD "because he is the only-begotten Son of God", noting that "the Jews object and say, that he was God's minister" ([John Calvin, Commentary on Jeremiah 23:5–6](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/calvin/calcom19.vi.v.html)). The New American Bible's note reads the promise as a new king "from David's line who will rule justly", and "The Lord our justice" as "an ironic wordplay on the name of the weak King Zedekiah" ([USCCB, Jeremiah, chapter 23, with notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/jeremiah/23)).

## Open questions

Questions on which the churches or scholars disagree. These guidelines don't pick a side.

- `7` [Christine Hayes, Introduction to the Old Testament, Lecture 18, Open Yale Courses](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-18): **how was the book put together, and which text is older?** The Talmud holds that Jeremiah wrote his own book ([Sefaria, Bava Batra 15a:2](https://www.sefaria.org/Bava_Batra.15a.2)), and the ESV Global Study Bible that "Jeremiah and Baruch left a record of the difficult times in which they lived" ([ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to Jeremiah](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-jeremiah/)). Hayes finds oracles, narratives and Deuteronomistic editing, and notes that the Septuagint's Jeremiah "is much shorter than the Hebrew version of Jeremiah and it's arranged differently", and that "There are also significant differences between the Hebrew text that we have now and some fragments of Jeremiah that have been found among the Dead Sea scrolls". In 1910 the *Catholic Encyclopedia* held that the scroll of chapter 36, "This first authentic recension of the prophecies", "forms the basis of the present Book of Jeremias", and that "the first transcript was enlarged by various insertions and additions from the pen of Baruch or of a later prophet"; that chapter 52 is "an addition of the post-Jeremianic period", because the chapter before ends, "Thus far are the words of Jeremiah" ([Jeremiah 51:64](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JER51.htm#V64)); and, of the shorter Greek text, that "In most cases the Alexandrian translation has retained the better and original reading" ([Catholic Encyclopedia (1910), Jeremias](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/08334a.htm)).
- `7` [USCCB, Jeremiah, chapter 20, with notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/jeremiah/20) (New American Bible, Revised Edition): **how should Jeremiah's complaints against God be read?** The New American Bible translates "You seduced me, LORD, and I let myself be seduced", and notes that "Jeremiah accuses the Lord of having deceived him"; the WEBBE reads "LORD, you have persuaded me, and I was persuaded" ([Jeremiah 20:7](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JER20.htm#V7)). Hayes reads it the same way: "he accuses God of deceiving him, of enticing him" ([Christine Hayes, Introduction to the Old Testament, Lecture 18, Open Yale Courses](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-18)). Calvin held that "there is no doubt but that his language is ironical", against those who think Jeremiah spoke "inconsiderately through the influence of a hasty impulse" ([John Calvin, Commentary on Jeremiah 20:7](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/calvin/calcom19.iii.ix.html)), but judged that when Jeremiah cursed his birth "the Prophet most grievously sinned when he became thus calumnious towards God" ([John Calvin, Commentary on Jeremiah 20:14–16](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/calvin/calcom19.iii.xvii.html); [Jeremiah 20:14–18](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JER20.htm#V14)). The New American Bible's note holds that the prophet is brought "to the point of despair; nevertheless he maintains confidence in God", and the Catechism of the Catholic Church teaches that the prophets' prayer is "At times … an argument or a complaint, but it is always an intercession" ([Catechism of the Catholic Church, 2584](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P91.HTM)). The churches agree that "lament is prayer too" (`PRAY-5` in [Prayer](../../general/prayer.md)).
- `6` [John Calvin, Commentary on Jeremiah 11:19](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/calvin/calcom18.iii.xviii.html) (Christian Classics Ethereal Library): **does Jeremiah 11:19 speak of Christ?** Cyril of Jerusalem read the words "let us place a beam upon His bread" as a prophecy of the cross ([Cyril of Jerusalem, Catechetical Lecture 13, 19](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/310113.htm)), and the Orthodox and Catholic Churches read the passage in Holy Week and in Lent (see above). The WEBBE reads "Let's destroy the tree with its fruit" ([Jeremiah 11:19](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JER11.htm#V19)). Calvin read the words as Jeremiah's enemies plotting against the prophet himself, and wrote that "The ancients perverted this passage in the most childish manner when they applied it to the body of Christ", asking: "if we grant that the Prophet was a type of Christ, what has this to do with the similitude of his body, since he speaks here only of food?"

## Tutorials and further reading

- `7` [Christine Hayes, Literary Prophecy: Micah, Zephaniah, Nahum and Habbakuk](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-18) (Open Yale Courses, Introduction to the Old Testament, lecture 18): a university lecture, with a transcript, whose last two chapters read the Book of Jeremiah: its structure, the temple sermon, the confessions, the letter to the exiles and the new covenant.

- `7` [Brian Rainey, The Prophet Jeremiah](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/the-prophet-jeremiah/) (Bible Odyssey): Jeremiah as a true prophet against his opponents, his symbolic acts and his "Book of Comfort".
