# Malachi

The Book of Malachi is "A revelation, the LORD's word to Israel by Malachi" ([Malachi 1:1](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MAL01.htm#V1)). It speaks to the Jewish community in Judah a few generations after the return from exile, when the temple had been rebuilt. The USCCB introduction sums up what it finds there: the people "withhold tithes and sacrificial contributions" and "cheat God by providing defective goods for sacrifice"; "People divorce their spouses and marry worshipers of other gods"; "Priests, who could strengthen discipline by their instruction, connive with the people"; and "Underlying all this is a weary attitude, a cynical notion that nothing is to be gained by doing what God wants and that wrongdoers prosper" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Malachi](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/malachi/0)). The ESV Global Study Bible describes the book as "six disputations between God and Israel" with "a common pattern": God makes a charge, the people answer "But you say", and God replies ([ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to Malachi](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-malachi/); [Malachi 1:2](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MAL01.htm#V2)).

The ESV Global Study Bible holds that Malachi "probably lived at the same time as Ezra and Nehemiah, around 460 B.C.", "nearly a hundred years after the decree of Cyrus", and that his name "means 'my messenger'" ([ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to Malachi](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-malachi/); see the [Ezra](ezra.md), [Nehemiah](nehemiah.md), [Haggai](haggai.md) and [Zechariah](zechariah.md) pages). The USCCB introduction notes that the book "may have been written before Nehemiah's first return to Jerusalem in 445 B.C.", or while he was there, "or even later", and that "many believe" the name is "a pseudonym based on mal'ākî, 'my messenger,' in 3:1 and that the author's real name is unknown" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Malachi](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/malachi/0); [Malachi 3:1](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MAL03.htm#V1)). The Greek Septuagint reads the first verse as "An issue of the Lord's word to Israel by the hand of his messenger" ([NETS, The Twelve Prophets, translated by George E. Howard](https://ccat.sas.upenn.edu/nets/edition/32-twelve-nets.pdf)), and the Talmud records one rabbi's view that "Malachi is in fact Ezra", against the Rabbis, who held that "Malachi was his real name" ([Sefaria, Megillah 15a:5](https://www.sefaria.org/Megillah.15a.5)). Who Malachi was is one of the open questions below.

Malachi closes the Twelve, the minor prophets, which the Hebrew Bible keeps as "one book of the Twelve Prophets" ([Sefaria, Bava Batra 14b:9](https://www.sefaria.org/Bava_Batra.14b.9)), and it comes last of the Twelve in the Greek Septuagint too ([NETS, The Twelve Prophets, translated by George E. Howard](https://ccat.sas.upenn.edu/nets/edition/32-twelve-nets.pdf)). In Jewish Scripture "Malachi is the last book of the Prophets, and according to rabbinic tradition, marks the end of prophecy in Israel" ([Sefaria, Malachi](https://www.sefaria.org/Malachi)); its closing verses (3:4–24 in the Hebrew numbering, 3:4–4:6 in the WEBBE) are the prophetic reading on Shabbat HaGadol, the Sabbath before Passover, a reading that "speaks of the 'great day' of God on which the Messiah will appear" ([Ronald H. Isaacs, Shabbat HaGadol, My Jewish Learning](https://www.myjewishlearning.com/article/shabbat-hagadol/)). In Christian Bibles it is "the last book of the OT" ([Bible Odyssey, Malachi (HarperCollins Bible Dictionary)](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/dictionary/malachi/)), so that its closing promise of Elijah stands just before the Gospels, and Mark's Gospel opens by quoting Malachi's "messenger" of John the Baptist ([Malachi 4:5–6](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MAL04.htm#V5), [Mark 1:2](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MRK01.htm#V2)). The Orthodox Church in America notes that "the holy Fathers call him 'the seal of the prophets'" ([Orthodox Church in America, Lives of all saints commemorated on January 3: Prophet Malachi](https://www.oca.org/saints/all-lives/2026/01/03)), and the **Reformed** commentator Matthew Henry passes on the observation that "the last word of the Old Testament is a curse", while "it is with a blessing, with the choicest of blessings, that the New Testament ends" ([Matthew Henry, Commentary on Malachi 4](https://www.ccel.org/h/henry/mhc2/MHC39004.HTM)).

## How the churches read it

Where the churches, or their interpreters, read the book differently, or where it has played a part in their history. Each entry starts with its source's score.

- `7` [Catechism of the Catholic Church, 717–730](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P23.HTM): **the messenger and Elijah in John the Baptist.** The Gospels apply Malachi to John the Baptist. Mark begins, "As it is written in the prophets, 'Behold, I send my messenger before your face, who will prepare your way before you'" ([Mark 1:2](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MRK01.htm#V2)); Jesus says of John, "this is Elijah, who is to come", and "Elijah has come already" ([Matthew 11:10–14](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MAT11.htm#V10), [Matthew 17:10–13](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MAT17.htm#V10)); and the angel says that John "will go before him in the spirit and power of Elijah, 'to turn the hearts of the fathers to the children'" ([Luke 1:17](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/LUK01.htm#V17); see the [Matthew](../new-testament/matthew.md), [Mark](../new-testament/mark.md) and [Luke](../new-testament/luke.md) pages). The **Catholic** Catechism teaches that "John is 'Elijah (who) must come'" and that "John completes the cycle of prophets begun by Elijah", and the New American Bible notes that "the gospels saw Elijah in the person of John the Baptist" ([USCCB, Malachi, chapter 3, with notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/malachi/3)). **Orthodox** teaching calls Malachi "the last of the prophets before John the Baptist whose coming he foretells" ([Orthodox Church in America, The Orthodox Faith: Old Testament, Prophets](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/doctrine-scripture/old-testament/prophets)), and readings from Malachi 3 and 4 are among the Old Testament readings for the feast of the Beheading of John the Baptist on 29 August ([Orthodox Church in America, Scripture Readings for 29 August 2026](https://www.oca.org/readings/daily/2026/08/29)). The **Reformed** commentator Matthew Henry reads the promise of Elijah as "by John Baptist, the harbinger of the Messiah" ([Matthew Henry, Commentary on Malachi 4](https://www.ccel.org/h/henry/mhc2/MHC39004.HTM)).
- `7` [The Didache, 14](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0714.htm) (New Advent): **"a pure offering" and the Eucharist.** The Didache, an early Christian manual, tells Christians to "break bread, and give thanksgiving" every Lord's day, "that your sacrifice may be pure", "For this is that which was spoken by the Lord: In every place and time offer to me a pure sacrifice" ([Malachi 1:11](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MAL01.htm#V11)). Augustine reads the verse as "foretelling the Church which we now behold propagated through Christ", whose sacrifice is "offered to God in every place, from the rising of the sun to his going down, through Christ's priesthood after the order of Melchisedec" ([Augustine, City of God, Book XVIII, chapter 35](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/120118.htm)). The **Catholic** Council of Trent teaches that the sacrifice of the Mass "is indeed that clean oblation, which cannot be defiled by any unworthiness, or malice of those that offer (it); which the Lord foretold by Malachias was to be offered in every place" ([Council of Trent, Session XXII, chapter I, Hanover Historical Texts Project](https://history.hanover.edu/texts/trent/ct22.html)), and the Catechism calls the Eucharist "'the pure offering' of the whole Body of Christ to the glory of God's name", with a reference to Malachi 1:11 ([Catechism of the Catholic Church, 2639–2643](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P9A.HTM)). The **Reformed** reformer John Calvin reads the offering and incense as a picture of "the spiritual worship of God": "what are the sacrifices of the New Testament? They are prayers and thanksgivings", and he rejects the reading of the verse as the sacrifice of the Mass ([John Calvin, Commentary on Malachi 1:11](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/calvin/calcom30.iv.ii.xii.html); [Hebrews 13:15](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/HEB13.htm#V15)). Whether, and in what sense, the Eucharist is a sacrifice is an open question on the [Worship and the sacraments](../../general/worship.md#open-questions) page.
- `7` [Augustine, City of God, Book XVIII, chapter 35](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/120118.htm) (New Advent): **the coming of the Lord and the Sun of righteousness.** Augustine reads [Malachi 3:1–2](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MAL03.htm#V1) as foretelling "both the first and second advent of Christ": "He shall come suddenly into His temple; that is, into His flesh", and the day "who shall abide" is his second coming; he quotes the promise that "the Sun of Righteousness" will arise ([Malachi 4:2](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MAL04.htm#V2)). The **Orthodox** troparion of the Nativity sings that the wise men "were taught by a star to adore You, the Sun of Righteousness", with a reference to Malachi 4:2 ([Orthodox Church in America, Troparia and kontakia for December 25](https://www.oca.org/saints/troparia/2026/12/25)), and the Orthodox Church in America notes that the verse is "a reference made, according to Christians, explicitly to their Lord" ([Orthodox Church in America, The Orthodox Faith: Old Testament, Prophets](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/doctrine-scripture/old-testament/prophets)). Matthew Henry holds that "by the Sun of righteousness here we are certainly to understand Jesus Christ" ([Matthew Henry, Commentary on Malachi 4](https://www.ccel.org/h/henry/mhc2/MHC39004.HTM)), and Charles Wesley's Christmas hymn "Hark! the herald angels sing" (1739), published in more than 1,400 hymnals, among them The United Methodist Hymnal and the Baptist Hymnal 2008, greets Christ: "Hail the Sun of Righteousness! Light and life to all he brings, risen with healing in his wings" ([Hymnary.org, Hark! The Herald Angels Sing](https://hymnary.org/text/hark_the_herald_angels_sing_glory_to)).
- `7` [Orthodox Church in America, Lives of all saints commemorated on January 3: Prophet Malachi](https://www.oca.org/saints/all-lives/2026/01/03): **the Orthodox commemoration.** The **Orthodox** Church keeps the feast of the Prophet Malachi on 3 January. Its life holds that he "lived 400 years before the Birth of Christ, at the time of the return of the Jews from the Babylonian Captivity", that he "was called Malachi, i.e., an angel", and that in his book "he upbraids the Jews, foretelling the coming of Jesus Christ and His Forerunner, and also the Last Judgment".
- `6` [USCCB, Readings for the Feast of the Presentation of the Lord (2 February 2026)](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/readings/020226.cfm): **Malachi in the lectionaries.** In the **Catholic** lectionary, "the LORD whom you seek" who will come "to the temple" ([Malachi 3:1–4](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MAL03.htm#V1)) is the first reading on the feast of the Presentation, with the Gospel of Jesus brought to the temple ([Luke 2:22–40](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/LUK02.htm#V22)); the same passage, with the promise of Elijah, is read on 23 December with the birth of John the Baptist ([USCCB, Readings for Wednesday of the Fourth Week of Advent (23 December 2026)](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/readings/122326.cfm); [Luke 1:57–66](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/LUK01.htm#V57)). The Revised Common Lectionary, used by most mainline **Protestant** churches in Canada and the United States ([Vanderbilt Divinity Library, Revised Common Lectionary: FAQ](https://lectionary.library.vanderbilt.edu/faq/)), reads Malachi 3:1–4 at the Presentation of the Lord in every year and on the Second Sunday of Advent in Year C, and the sun of righteousness ([Malachi 4:1–2](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MAL04.htm#V1)) at Proper 28 in Year C ([Vanderbilt Divinity Library, Revised Common Lectionary: Sunday citations](https://lectionary.library.vanderbilt.edu/sunday-citations/)).

## Open questions

Questions on which the churches or scholars disagree. These guidelines don't pick a side.

- `7` [USCCB, Malachi, chapter 3, with notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/malachi/3) (New American Bible, Revised Edition): **who is the messenger, and who comes to the temple?** The New American Bible notes that "Some take God's messenger in v. 1a to be a person distinct from 'the lord' and 'the messenger of the covenant' in v. 1b; others hold that they are one and the same person. Some consider 'the lord' and 'the messenger of the covenant' to be divine, while others hold that in the text's literal sense he is a messianic earthly ruler" ([Malachi 3:1](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MAL03.htm#V1)). On Elijah, "A Jewish tradition interpreted this literally; the gospels saw Elijah in the person of John the Baptist". Augustine reads "the Angel of the testament" as Christ himself: "Nor is it to be wondered at that Christ Jesus is called the Angel of the Almighty God" ([Augustine, City of God, Book XVIII, chapter 35](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/120118.htm)). Enter the Bible holds that the closing verses "are probably an addendum to the book by a different author", so that "there is no reason within the book itself to assume the messenger and Elijah are the same" ([Enter the Bible, Introductory Issues in Malachi](https://enterthebible.org/courses/malachi/lessons/introductory-issues-in-malachi/)). In John's Gospel, John the Baptist answers the question "Are you Elijah?" with "I am not" ([John 1:21](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JHN01.htm#V21)); in Jewish practice Elijah is still awaited, with "a cup for Elijah" set out at the Passover Seder "to welcome Elijah in case he comes to announce the messiah" ([Enter the Bible, Bible in the World – Malachi](https://enterthebible.org/courses/malachi/lessons/bible-in-the-world-malachi/)).
- `7` [Eve Levavi Feinstein, Divorce in the Hebrew Bible](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/divorce-in-the-hebrew-bible/) (Bible Odyssey): **does God "hate divorce"?** Feinstein notes that [Malachi 2:10–16](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MAL02.htm#V10) "is usually understood as a condemnation of divorce as hated by God. However, the Hebrew is difficult, and the passage can also be understood as condemning the abandonment of Israelite wives in favor of foreign ones. Some ancient translations, as well as a variant text from Qumran, even understand it as explicitly condoning divorce". The translations differ: the New American Bible has "For I hate divorce, says the LORD" ([USCCB, Malachi, chapter 2, with notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/malachi/2)), as does the NRSVUE, with the note that the Hebrew reads "he hates" ([Bible Gateway, Malachi 1–4 (NRSVUE)](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Malachi%201-4&version=NRSVUE)); the WEBBE has "One who hates and divorces", says the LORD, "covers his garment with violence!" ([Malachi 2:16](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MAL02.htm#V16)). Calvin rendered it "If thou hatest, dismiss", reading it as a rebuke that made polygamy worse than divorce, and his editor notes that this follows the Septuagint and the Targum, as Cyril, Jerome and Theodoret did ([John Calvin, Commentary on Malachi 2:16](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/calvin/calcom30.iv.iii.xxi.html)); Matthew Henry's text has "he hateth putting away" ([Matthew Henry, Commentary on Malachi 2](https://www.ccel.org/h/henry/mhc2/MHC39002.HTM)). Enter the Bible concludes that "divorce, where it serves the husband and injures the wife, is to be avoided", but that "Malachi is not a blanket prohibition on divorce" ([Enter the Bible, Bible in the World – Malachi](https://enterthebible.org/courses/malachi/lessons/bible-in-the-world-malachi/)). What the churches teach on divorce and remarriage is set out in [Marriage, family and singleness](../../general/family.md#open-questions).
- `7` [USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Malachi](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/malachi/0) (New American Bible, Revised Edition): **a name or a title, and when?** The New American Bible notes that "many believe" Malachi is "a pseudonym based on mal'ākî, 'my messenger,' in 3:1", and dates the book around Nehemiah's time, before, during or after 445 BC. Enter the Bible holds that "Malachi, 'my messenger,' is more likely a descriptive title rather than a proper name for the author", and that because Zechariah 9:1, 12:1 and Malachi 1:1 share a heading found nowhere else, "many scholars believe that Zechariah 9-14 and Malachi may be by the same author" ([Enter the Bible, Introductory Issues in Malachi](https://enterthebible.org/courses/malachi/lessons/introductory-issues-in-malachi/)). The Septuagint reads "by the hand of his messenger" ([NETS, The Twelve Prophets, translated by George E. Howard](https://ccat.sas.upenn.edu/nets/edition/32-twelve-nets.pdf)), and the NRSVUE notes "Or by my messenger" ([Bible Gateway, Malachi 1–4 (NRSVUE)](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Malachi%201-4&version=NRSVUE)). The Talmud records the view that "Malachi is in fact Ezra", because both confronted marriages to foreign women, against the Rabbis, who held that "Malachi was his real name" ([Sefaria, Megillah 15a:5](https://www.sefaria.org/Megillah.15a.5)). Matthew Henry calls the identification with Ezra "groundless" and concludes that Malachi "was a person whose proper name was that by which he is here called" ([Matthew Henry, Commentary on Malachi: Introduction](https://www.ccel.org/h/henry/mhc2/MHC39000.HTM)), and the ESV Global Study Bible treats Malachi as a prophet who "probably lived at the same time as Ezra and Nehemiah, around 460 B.C." ([ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to Malachi](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-malachi/)).
- `6` [Enter the Bible, Theological Themes in Malachi](https://enterthebible.org/courses/malachi/lessons/theological-themes-in-malachi/) (Luther Seminary): **must Christians tithe, and what does "test me" promise?** Enter the Bible notes that [Malachi 3:10–12](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MAL03.htm#V10) is "cited by proponents of so-called 'prosperity theology,' who promise that generous contributions to the church will guarantee a profitable return", and holds that "for Malachi, the tithe is not a 'let's make a deal' arrangement to secure favor with God": tithes bring blessing "because the relationship with God that the faithful tithe implies is itself a blessing". The Lausanne Theology Working Group describes the prosperity gospel as teaching that believers obtain health and wealth through "the 'sowing of seeds' through the faithful payments of tithes and offerings" ([Lausanne Theology Working Group, A Statement on the Prosperity Gospel](https://lausanne.org/content/a-statement-on-the-prosperity-gospel)). Matthew Henry draws from the verse that "our quota must be contributed for the support of religion in the place where we live", and that "Those that will deal with God must deal upon trust" ([Matthew Henry, Commentary on Malachi 3](https://www.ccel.org/h/henry/mhc2/MHC39003.HTM)). Historically, "In the time of Ezra and Nehemiah, the tithe was a tax collected at the Temple to support the priests and Levites", and Jesus "did not, however, condemn the practice of tithing as such" ([Bible Odyssey, Tithe (HarperCollins Bible Dictionary)](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/dictionary/tithe/); [Nehemiah 13:10–14](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/NEH13.htm#V10), [Matthew 23:23](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MAT23.htm#V23)). Whether Christians must give a tenth, and the churches' teaching on it, are open questions on the [Money and generosity](../../general/money.md#open-questions) page.
