# Habakkuk

The Book of Habakkuk is "The revelation which Habakkuk the prophet saw" ([Habakkuk 1:1](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/HAB01.htm#V1)). The USCCB introduction calls Habakkuk "the only prophet to devote his entire work to the question of the justice of God's government of the world", adding that "In the Bible as a whole, only Job delivers a more pointed challenge to divine rule" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Habakkuk](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/habakkuk/0); see the [Job](job.md) page). The book is set out as a dialogue: the prophet complains of injustice in Judah, God answers that he is raising up the Chaldeans, or Babylonians, the prophet complains of the Chaldeans' own violence, and God answers again, assuring him "of the reliability of God's rule and calling for human faithfulness"; then come sayings against tyrants and a hymn of God's appearing to save his people. The ESV Global Study Bible notes that the book "never addresses the people of Judah directly. Rather it is a dialogue between the prophet and God" ([ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to Habakkuk](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-habakkuk/)), and Matthew Henry that "it is the office of the prophet to carry messages both ways" ([Matthew Henry, Commentary on Habakkuk: Introduction](https://www.ccel.org/h/henry/mhc2/MHC35000.HTM)). The closing prayer, "set to victorious music", carries the word "Selah" and a note "For the music director, on my stringed instruments" ([Habakkuk 3:1](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/HAB03.htm#V1), [Habakkuk 3:19](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/HAB03.htm#V19)); the New American Bible notes that these terms "are found elsewhere in the Bible only in the Psalter, and they indicate that, like the psalms, this poem was once used in worship" ([USCCB, Habakkuk, chapter 3, with notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/habakkuk/3)).

Nothing is known of the prophet outside his book: "The prophet Habakkuk is not mentioned outside of his book in the Old Testament" ([Enter the Bible, Bible in the World – Habakkuk](https://enterthebible.org/courses/habakkuk/lessons/bible-in-the-world-habakkuk/)). His time is inferred from the book. The USCCB introduction holds that "Two important events frame Habakkuk's prophecy: the great Babylonian (Chaldean) victory over the Egyptians at Carchemish (605 B.C.) and the second Babylonian invasion of Judah (587 B.C.)"; Christine Hayes dates the book to "600 to the destruction" of Jerusalem ([Christine Hayes, Introduction to the Old Testament, Lecture 18, Open Yale Courses](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-18)); the ESV Global Study Bible holds that he "probably prophesied no later than the end of Josiah's reign (640–609 B.C.)"; and the HarperCollins Bible Dictionary that he "appears to have been active during the last quarter of the seventh century BCE" ([Bible Odyssey, Habakkuk (HarperCollins Bible Dictionary)](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/dictionary/habakkuk/)). The Orthodox Church in America's life of the prophet says that he "was descended from the Tribe of Simeon, and he prophesied around 650 B.C." ([Orthodox Church in America, Lives of all saints commemorated on December 2: Prophet Habakkuk](https://www.oca.org/saints/all-lives/2026/12/02)). In the Greek additions to Daniel, the story of Bel and the Dragon, an angel carries "the prophet Habakkuk" from Judea to Babylon to bring food to Daniel in the lions' den ([Daniel (Greek) 14:33–39](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/DAG14.htm#V33); [deuterocanon](../README.md#deuterocanon)).

Habakkuk is Jewish Scripture, "one of 12 books of Minor Prophets ('Trei Asar'), marked by their shortness" ([Sefaria, Habakkuk](https://www.sefaria.org/Habakkuk)). In the Hebrew Bible the twelve form one book: the Talmud explains that Hosea's prophecy "is written together with those of Haggai, Zechariah, and Malachi in one book of the Twelve Prophets", because "Were it written separately, since it is small it would be lost" ([Sefaria, Bava Batra 14b:9](https://www.sefaria.org/Bava_Batra.14b.9)), and Sirach already speaks of "the twelve prophets" ([Sirach 49:10](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/SIR49.htm#V10)). Habakkuk is "the eighth of the Twelve Minor Prophets" ([Orthodox Church in America, Lives of all saints commemorated on December 2: Prophet Habakkuk](https://www.oca.org/saints/all-lives/2026/12/02)), in the Greek Septuagint as in the Hebrew Bible ([NETS, The Twelve Prophets, translated by George E. Howard](https://ccat.sas.upenn.edu/nets/edition/32-twelve-nets.pdf)). The prayer of chapter 3, with the verse before it ([Habakkuk 2:20–3:19](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/HAB02.htm#V20)), is the synagogue reading from the prophets on the second day of Shavuot, chosen because it describes "a manifestation of God" and "the language in places recalls the revelation at Sinai" ([Ronald H. Isaacs, Shavuot Torah & Haftarah Readings, My Jewish Learning](https://www.myjewishlearning.com/article/shavuot-torah-haftarah-readings/)), and the Talmud holds that "Habakkuk came and established the 613 mitzvot upon one, as it is stated: 'But the righteous person shall live by his faith'" ([Sefaria, Makkot 24a:27](https://www.sefaria.org/Makkot.24a.27)). Among the Dead Sea Scrolls is a commentary on Habakkuk from the first century BC that reads the book's first two chapters of its own time ([Israel Museum, The Digital Dead Sea Scrolls: The Commentary on Habakkuk Scroll](http://dss.collections.imj.org.il/habakkuk)). Every church reads Habakkuk among the minor prophets.

## How the churches read it

Where the churches, or their interpreters, read the book differently, or where it has played a part in their history. Each entry starts with its source's score.

- `7` [Martin Luther, "Tower Experience" (1545)](https://germanhistorydocs.org/en/from-the-reformations-to-the-thirty-years-war-1500-1648/ghdi:document-4232) (German History in Documents and Images): **"the righteous will live by faith" in the Reformation.** Paul quotes [Habakkuk 2:4](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/HAB02.htm#V4) in [Romans 1:17](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ROM01.htm#V17), "But the righteous shall live by faith", and in [Galatians 3:11](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/GAL03.htm#V11), to show "that no man is justified by the law before God"; Hebrews quotes it too ([Hebrews 10:37–38](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/HEB10.htm#V37); see the [Romans](../new-testament/romans.md), [Galatians](../new-testament/galatians.md) and [Hebrews](../new-testament/hebrews.md) pages). In the preface to his Latin writings, **Luther** recalls how he "hated that word, 'justice of God'", until "I paid attention to their context: 'The justice of God is revealed in it, as it is written: "The just person lives by faith."'" He came to understand it as "a passive justice, i.e. that by which the merciful God justifies us by faith", and "All at once I felt that I had been born again and entered into paradise itself through open gates." The editors note that "the accuracy of Luther's account has long been debated", but that the text "was the key source for many twentieth-century accounts of Luther's 'breakthrough'". Enter the Bible concludes that "Habakkuk 2:4, then, as interpreted by Paul and then Luther, played a significant role in the Reformation, as 'faith alone' became one of the rallying cries of the movement" ([Enter the Bible, Bible in the World – Habakkuk](https://enterthebible.org/courses/habakkuk/lessons/bible-in-the-world-habakkuk/)). Among the **Reformed**, Calvin reads the prophet's word for faith as "that faith which strips us of all arrogance, and leads us naked and needy to God, that we may seek salvation from him alone" ([John Calvin, Commentary on Habakkuk 2:4](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/calvin/calcom29.iii.iii.vi.html)), and Matthew Henry holds that the verse "is quoted in the New Testament… for the proof of the great doctrine of justification by faith only" ([Matthew Henry, Commentary on Habakkuk 2](https://www.ccel.org/h/henry/mhc2/MHC35002.HTM)). How the Catholic Church and the Lutheran churches have since stated their common understanding of justification is set out on the [Romans](../new-testament/romans.md) page; see also the [Lutheran](../../traditions/lutheran.md) church page.
- `7` [USCCB, Habakkuk, chapter 2, with notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/habakkuk/2) (New American Bible, Revised Edition): **the Catholic reading of 2:4.** The **Catholic** New American Bible translates the verse "the just one who is righteous because of faith shall live", and notes that "Several New Testament passages cite these words (Rom 1:17; Gal 3:11; cf. Heb 10:38) to confirm the teaching that people receive justification and supernatural life through faith in Christ." In the Catholic lectionary, the prophet's cry and God's answer ([Habakkuk 1:2–3](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/HAB01.htm#V2), [Habakkuk 2:2–4](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/HAB02.htm#V2)) are read on the Twenty-seventh Sunday in Ordinary Time in Year C, with the gospel in which the apostles ask, "Increase our faith" ([Luke 17:5–10](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/LUK17.htm#V5); [USCCB, Readings for the Twenty-seventh Sunday in Ordinary Time (5 October 2025)](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/readings/100525.cfm)). The Revised Common Lectionary, used by most mainline **Protestant** churches in Canada and the United States ([Vanderbilt Divinity Library, Revised Common Lectionary: FAQ](https://lectionary.library.vanderbilt.edu/faq/)), reads Habakkuk 1:1–4 and 2:1–4 on two Sundays after Pentecost in Year C ([Vanderbilt Divinity Library, Revised Common Lectionary: Sunday citations](https://lectionary.library.vanderbilt.edu/sunday-citations/)).
- `7` [Orthodox Church in America, Lives of all saints commemorated on December 2: Prophet Habakkuk](https://www.oca.org/saints/all-lives/2026/12/02): **the Orthodox commemoration and the fourth ode.** The **Orthodox** Church keeps the feast of the Prophet Habakkuk on 2 December. Its life says that he "foresaw the destruction of the Jerusalem Temple, the Babylonian Captivity and the later return of the captives", retells the story of his carrying food to Daniel in Babylon ([Daniel (Greek) 14:33–39](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/DAG14.htm#V33)), and records that "The Fourth Ode of the Psalter ('O Lord, I have heard thy report, and was afraid...') is based on Habakkuk 3:2-19".
- `7` [Augustine, City of God, Book XVIII, chapter 32](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/120118.htm) (New Advent): **Habakkuk's prayer read of Christ.** In the Greek Septuagint, [Habakkuk 3:2](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/HAB03.htm#V2) reads "You will be known in the midst of two living creatures" ([NETS, The Twelve Prophets, translated by George E. Howard](https://ccat.sas.upenn.edu/nets/edition/32-twelve-nets.pdf)), where the WEBBE, following the Hebrew, has "Renew your work in the middle of the years". Augustine asks of the prayer, "to whom but the Lord Christ does he say" it, and reads the two living creatures as "either between the two testaments, or between the two thieves, or between Moses and Elias talking with Him on the mount". He reads "in wrath You will be mindful of mercy" of Christ on the cross, "when He, mindful of mercy, said, Father, forgive them, for they know not what they do" ([Luke 23:34](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/LUK23.htm#V34)).

## Open questions

Questions on which the churches or scholars disagree. These guidelines don't pick a side.

- `7` [NETS, The Twelve Prophets, translated by George E. Howard](https://ccat.sas.upenn.edu/nets/edition/32-twelve-nets.pdf) (International Organization for Septuagint and Cognate Studies): **"faith" or "faithfulness" in 2:4?** Translations differ: the WEBBE has "the righteous will live by his faith" ([Habakkuk 2:4](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/HAB02.htm#V4)), the NRSVUE "the righteous live by their faithfulness" ([Bible Gateway, Habakkuk 1–3 (NRSVUE)](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Habakkuk%201-3&version=NRSVUE)), and the New American Bible "the just one who is righteous because of faith shall live" ([USCCB, Habakkuk, chapter 2, with notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/habakkuk/2)). The Greek Septuagint has "But the just shall live by my faith", with the note "Or faithfulness", that is, God's own. Christine Hayes reads the verse in its setting: "The righteous simply have to have faith that justice will prevail and this faith has to sustain them through the trials that challenge that very idea" ([Christine Hayes, Introduction to the Old Testament, Lecture 18, Open Yale Courses](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-18)). Calvin notes that some take the word "as meaning integrity", the just person's "faithfulness and pure conscience", and rejects this: "in this case they frigidly extenuate the Prophet's declaration" ([John Calvin, Commentary on Habakkuk 2:4](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/calvin/calcom29.iii.iii.vi.html)). The Talmud reads the verse as summing up all the commandments ([Sefaria, Makkot 24a:27](https://www.sefaria.org/Makkot.24a.27)), and the community at Qumran applied it to the followers of its "Teacher of Righteousness" ([Enter the Bible, Bible in the World – Habakkuk](https://enterthebible.org/courses/habakkuk/lessons/bible-in-the-world-habakkuk/)).
- `7` [USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Habakkuk](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/habakkuk/0): **does the book answer its question about God's justice?** The USCCB introduction sees the prophet's struggle "with difficult and important theological questions about divine justice". Hayes notes that, unlike the Deuteronomistic historians, "Habakkuk doesn't assert that the people are suffering for their sins", calls God's answer "Not a terribly deep answer", and holds that the prophet "sees the problem's resolution only in some vision of the future" ([Christine Hayes, Introduction to the Old Testament, Lecture 18, Open Yale Courses](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-18)). Enter the Bible holds that "The book does not resolve the theodicy question that it raises. Rather, the book puts forth a model for waiting", in which "Waiting does not silence the lament" ([Enter the Bible, Introductory Issues in Habakkuk](https://enterthebible.org/courses/habakkuk/lessons/introductory-issues-in-habakkuk/)), and that "Habakkuk rejects a 'the end justifies the means' argument" ([Enter the Bible, Theological Themes in Habakkuk](https://enterthebible.org/courses/habakkuk/lessons/theological-themes-in-habakkuk/)). The ESV Global Study Bible asks "How could a good and just God use a more wicked nation (Babylon) to punish a less wicked one (Judah)?", and answers that "both nations are to be judged and appropriately punished for their sin", and that the prophet "has learned to rely totally on God's wisdom and justice" ([ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to Habakkuk](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-habakkuk/)). Matthew Henry finds the book "of general use, especially to help us through that great temptation" of "the power and prosperity of the wicked and the sufferings of the righteous" ([Matthew Henry, Commentary on Habakkuk: Introduction](https://www.ccel.org/h/henry/mhc2/MHC35000.HTM)). The wider question is among the open questions on the [Job](job.md#open-questions) page and in [Suffering, death and hope](../../general/suffering.md#open-questions).
- `7` [Israel Museum, The Digital Dead Sea Scrolls: The Commentary on Habakkuk Scroll](http://dss.collections.imj.org.il/habakkuk): **is the prayer of chapter 3 part of the first book?** The commentary on Habakkuk found at Qumran "interprets the first two chapters of the biblical book". The USCCB introduction holds that "Chapter 3 may be the prophet's prayer that God fulfill the promises made earlier to Habakkuk, or a hymn praising God's power added to Habakkuk's speeches by editors" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Habakkuk](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/habakkuk/0)). Hayes notes that the chapter "shifts gears. So much so that once again scholars say it must be an interpolation", but warns "that dramatic shifts in tone and theme are not that uncommon in the prophetic books and we have to be careful" ([Christine Hayes, Introduction to the Old Testament, Lecture 18, Open Yale Courses](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-18)). Enter the Bible notes that "The rare words and grammar suggest a separate author in the view of many interpreters", but holds that "the third chapter is integral to the flow of the book", since "The exhortation to silence at the end of chapter two is not a sufficient endpoint for the flow of the dialogue up to that point" ([Enter the Bible, Introductory Issues in Habakkuk](https://enterthebible.org/courses/habakkuk/lessons/introductory-issues-in-habakkuk/)).

## Tutorials and further reading

- `7` [Christine Hayes, Literary Prophecy: Micah, Zephaniah, Nahum and Habakkuk](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-18) (Open Yale Courses, Introduction to the Old Testament, lecture 18): a university lecture, with a transcript, whose fifth chapter reads Habakkuk as "philosophical musings on God's behavior" and the problem of evil.
- `6` [Enter the Bible, Bible in the World – Habakkuk](https://enterthebible.org/courses/habakkuk/lessons/bible-in-the-world-habakkuk/) (Luther Seminary): Habakkuk in the Apocrypha, in the Qumran commentary, in the Talmud, and in Paul and Luther.
- `6` [BibleProject, Habakkuk](https://bibleproject.com/videos/habakkuk/): an animated overview, in which "Babylon is an example of any nation that exalts itself above God and practices injustice, violence, and idolatry".
