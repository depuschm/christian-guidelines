# Ezekiel

The Book of Ezekiel gathers the visions, symbolic actions and oracles of Ezekiel, a priest who was deported to Babylon with King Jehoiachin in 597 BC. Five years later he became "the first prophet to be commissioned outside Judah or Israel". Before Jerusalem was destroyed in 587 BC, Ezekiel sought "to convince his audience that they are responsible for the punishment of exile"; afterwards he promised a people "enlivened by a new heart, imbued with new breath", and "restored to a re-created land, Temple, and covenant relationship". The USCCB introduction sums up the book: "the Lord punishes and restores for one reason—for the sake of his name, in order to demonstrate once and for all that he is Lord" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Ezekiel](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/ezekiel/0)). Its first dated message comes from the summer of 593 BC and its last from April 571 BC ([Crossway, ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to Ezekiel](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-ezekiel/)). Sara Wells notes that Ezekiel "describes the departure of God's presence from the desecrated Jerusalem temple", and "anticipates the return of the divine presence to the temple following the purification of Israel" ([Sara Wells, Ezekiel, Bible Odyssey](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/ezekiel/)).

The book speaks in the prophet's own voice and dates his visions ([Ezekiel 1:1–3](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/EZK01.htm#V1)). The Babylonian Talmud records that "The members of the Great Assembly wrote" Ezekiel, with the Twelve Prophets, Daniel and Esther ([Sefaria, Bava Batra 15a](https://www.sefaria.org/Bava_Batra.15a.2)). The 1909 *Catholic Encyclopedia* held that "The genuineness of the book of Ezekiel is generally conceded" ([Catholic Encyclopedia (1909), Ezekiel](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/05737b.htm)); critical scholars have since often seen "much of the book as the work of later hands", though "little consensus has been reached among the experts" ([Enter the Bible, Introductory Issues in Ezekiel](https://enterthebible.org/courses/ezekiel/lessons/introductory-issues-in-ezekiel/)). Who wrote the book is one of the open questions below.

Ezekiel is Jewish Scripture: "Ezekiel ('Yechezkel') is the seventh book of the Prophets", which "focuses on religious life in the absence of a Temple and personal responsibility for actions" ([Sefaria, Ezekiel](https://www.sefaria.org/Ezekiel)). The rabbis handled it with care. The Mishnah forbids expounding "the Design of the Divine Chariot" of its first chapter to anyone "unless he is wise and understands most matters on his own" ([Sefaria, Mishnah Chagigah 2:1](https://www.sefaria.org/Mishnah_Chagigah.2.1)), and the Talmud recalls that "the book of Ezekiel would have been suppressed because its contents, in many details, contradict matters of Torah", until Hananiah ben Hezekiah reconciled them ([Sefaria, Shabbat 13b](https://www.sefaria.org/Shabbat.13b.8)). Jerome wrote that its beginning and end "are not studied by the Hebrews until they are thirty years old" ([Jerome, Letter 53, 8](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/3001053.htm)). Every church reads it as one of the four major prophets, with Isaiah, Jeremiah and Daniel ([Orthodox Church in America, The Orthodox Faith: Old Testament, Prophets](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/doctrine-scripture/old-testament/prophets)). The book of Revelation "has often followed Ezekiel" ([Catholic Encyclopedia (1909), Ezekiel](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/05737b.htm)), from the four living creatures to the river of the water of life ([Revelation 4:6–8](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/REV04.htm#V6), [Revelation 22:1–2](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/REV22.htm#V1); see [Revelation](../new-testament/revelation.md)).

## How the churches read it

Where the churches, or their interpreters, read the book differently, or where it has played a part in their history. Each entry starts with its source's score.

- `8` [Pastores dabo vobis, 13 and 22](https://www.vatican.va/content/john-paul-ii/en/apost_exhortations/documents/hf_jp-ii_exh_25031992_pastores-dabo-vobis.html) (John Paul II, 1992): **the shepherds of Israel and the Good Shepherd** ([Ezekiel 34:1–31](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/EZK34.htm#V1)). In his apostolic exhortation on the formation of priests, John Paul II writes that "Jesus is the promised good shepherd (cf. Ez. 34)", and that "Fulfilling the prophetic proclamation of the Messiah and savior joyfully announced by the psalmist and the prophet Ezekiel (cf. Ps. 22-23; Ez. 34:11ff.), Jesus presents himself as 'the good shepherd'". The **Catholic** Catechism calls the Church "the flock of which God himself foretold that he would be the shepherd", citing this chapter ([Vatican, Catechism of the Catholic Church, 751–776](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P27.HTM)). The **Reformed** commentator Matthew Henry reads the shepherds as "their rulers both in church and state", and the chapter as a promise "that God would in the fulness of time send the Messiah, to be the great and good Shepherd of the sheep" ([Matthew Henry, Commentary on the Whole Bible, Ezekiel 34](https://www.ccel.org/h/henry/mhc2/MHC26034.HTM)). See also [John](../new-testament/john.md) and `CHURCH-4`.
- `7` [Vatican, Catechism of the Catholic Church, 702–716](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P22.HTM): **the new heart and the Spirit** ([Ezekiel 36:25–28](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/EZK36.htm#V25)). The **Catholic** Catechism counts Ezekiel's promises ([Ezekiel 11:19–20](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/EZK11.htm#V19), [Ezekiel 37:1–14](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/EZK37.htm#V1)) among "The prophetic texts that directly concern the sending of the Holy Spirit": "at the 'end time' the Lord's Spirit will renew the hearts of men, engraving a new law in them" (715). Chapter 36 is the seventh Old Testament reading at the Easter Vigil ([USCCB, Readings for the Easter Vigil (4 April 2026)](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/readings/040426.cfm); [Ezekiel 36:16–28](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/EZK36.htm#V16)). The **Reformed** Westminster Confession uses Ezekiel's words for God's effectual calling, "taking away their heart of stone, and giving unto them an heart of flesh", and cites these verses ([Christian Classics Ethereal Library, The Westminster Confession of Faith, 1647, with the American Alterations (Schaff, Creeds of Christendom)](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/schaff/creeds3.iv.xvii.ii.html), chapter X).
- `7` [Irenaeus, Against Heresies, Book III, chapter 11](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0103311.htm): **the four living creatures and the four Gospels** ([Ezekiel 1:5–10](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/EZK01.htm#V5); [Revelation 4:6–8](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/REV04.htm#V6)). Irenaeus writes that "the cherubim, too, were four-faced, and their faces were images of the dispensation of the Son of God", and that "the Gospels are in accord with these things". For Jerome, "Matthew, Mark, Luke and John are the Lord's team of four, the true cherubim" ([Jerome, Letter 53, 9](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/3001053.htm)). The **Orthodox** life of the prophet gives the pairing that became usual: "a man (Saint Matthew), a lion (Saint Mark), an ox (Saint Luke), and an eagle (Saint John)" ([Orthodox Church in America, Prophet Ezekiel](https://www.oca.org/saints/lives/2026/07/21/102064-prophet-ezekiel)); Irenaeus himself paired them differently. **John Calvin** rejected this reading: "It was formerly the received opinion, that under this figure were depicted the four Evangelists", "But in this fiction there is no stability", and he took the creatures to be cherubim ([John Calvin, Commentary on Ezekiel 1:4](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/calvin/calcom22.v.vi.html)).
- `7` [Orthodox Church in America, Prophet Ezekiel](https://www.oca.org/saints/lives/2026/07/21/102064-prophet-ezekiel): **the shut gate** ([Ezekiel 44:1–3](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/EZK44.htm#V1)). The **Orthodox** Church commemorates Ezekiel on 21 July, and its life of the prophet teaches that "Ezekiel's description of the shut gate of the sanctuary, through which the Lord God would enter (Ez. 44: 2), is a prophecy of the Virgin giving birth to Christ, yet remaining a virgin". The passage is read on the feast of the Nativity of the Theotokos ([Orthodox Church in America, Scripture Readings for 8 September 2026 (Nativity of the Theotokos)](https://www.oca.org/readings/daily/2026/09/08); [Ezekiel 43:27–44:4](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/EZK43.htm#V27)). Jerome wrote of Mary: "She is the east gate, spoken of by the prophet Ezekiel, always shut and always shining" ([Jerome, Letter 48, 21](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/3001048.htm)). Matthew Henry reads the shut gate as an honour to "the God of Israel", whose glory entered by it, and to the prince, by whom some "understand the high priests": "Christ is the high priest of our profession" ([Matthew Henry, Commentary on the Whole Bible, Ezekiel 44](https://www.ccel.org/h/henry/mhc2/MHC26044.HTM)).
- `7` [Orthodox Church in America, The Orthodox Faith: Old Testament, Prophets](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/doctrine-scripture/old-testament/prophets): **the dry bones and the resurrection** ([Ezekiel 37:1–14](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/EZK37.htm#V1)). In the **Orthodox** Church, "The moving passage about God's resurrection of the 'dry bones' of dead Israel through the breathing in of His Holy Spirit is read over the tomb of Christ at the Great Saturday service", and the life of the prophet teaches that "The vision of the dry bones prefigured the universal resurrection of the dead" ([Orthodox Church in America, Prophet Ezekiel](https://www.oca.org/saints/lives/2026/07/21/102064-prophet-ezekiel)). In the **Catholic** lectionary, "I will open your graves and have you rise from them" is read on the Fifth Sunday of Lent with the raising of Lazarus ([USCCB, Readings for the Fifth Sunday of Lent (22 March 2026)](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/readings/032226.cfm); [Ezekiel 37:12–14](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/EZK37.htm#V12), [John 11:1–45](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JHN11.htm#V1)). Matthew Henry sees in the vision "a most lively representation of a threefold resurrection": of souls from sin, of the church from persecution, and "The resurrection of the body at the great day" ([Matthew Henry, Commentary on the Whole Bible, Ezekiel 37](https://www.ccel.org/h/henry/mhc2/MHC26037.HTM)). Whether the vision itself speaks of the resurrection is one of the open questions below.
- `7` [Church of England, Book of Common Prayer: The Order for Morning Prayer](https://www.churchofengland.org/liturgy/book-of-common-prayer/order-morning-prayer): **"I have no pleasure in the death of the wicked"** ([Ezekiel 18:23](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/EZK18.htm#V23), [Ezekiel 33:11](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/EZK33.htm#V11)). The **Anglican** Morning Prayer may open with Ezekiel's words, "When the wicked man turneth away from his wickedness that he hath committed, and doeth that which is lawful and right, he shall save his soul alive" ([Ezekiel 18:27](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/EZK18.htm#V27)), and its absolution names God as the one "who desireth not the death of a sinner, but rather that he may turn from his wickedness, and live". The **Lutheran** Formula of Concord cites these verses to teach that God "is not willing that any should perish, but that all should come to repentance and believe in the Lord Christ" ([BookOfConcord.org, Formula of Concord: Epitome, XI. Election](https://bookofconcord.org/epitome/election/)). John Calvin, for the **Reformed**, held that "God wills not the death of a sinner, since he calls all equally to repentance", but that "the Prophet does not here speak of God's secret counsel" ([John Calvin, Commentary on Ezekiel 18:23](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/calvin/calcom23.vii.xiv.html)). How the churches teach God's election is set out on the [Romans](../new-testament/romans.md#how-the-churches-read-it) page.

## Open questions

Questions on which the churches or scholars disagree. These guidelines don't pick a side.

- `7` [Christine Hayes, Introduction to the Old Testament, Lecture 19, Open Yale Courses](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-19): **does the valley of dry bones speak of the resurrection of the dead?** Hayes holds that the passage "has often been de-contextualized and cited as an Old Testament or Hebrew Bible source for the doctrine of literal resurrection after death", but that "in its context it's quite clear that it is one of many metaphors that Ezekiel uses throughout this section for the redemption of the community from exile". Sara Wells agrees that "Resurrection, as Ezekiel describes it, does not involve individual resurrection of the dead. Rather, it concerns the life of the nation" ([Sara Wells, Ezekiel, Bible Odyssey](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/ezekiel/)). The rabbis of the Talmud were already divided: Rabbi Eliezer held that "The dead that Ezekiel revived stood on their feet and recited song to God and died", while another sage concluded, "In truth, it was a parable" ([Sefaria, Sanhedrin 92b](https://www.sefaria.org/Sanhedrin.92b.3)). The Orthodox life of the prophet teaches that the vision "prefigured the universal resurrection of the dead" ([Orthodox Church in America, Prophet Ezekiel](https://www.oca.org/saints/lives/2026/07/21/102064-prophet-ezekiel)), and Matthew Henry finds in it "The resurrection of the body at the great day" ([Matthew Henry, Commentary on the Whole Bible, Ezekiel 37](https://www.ccel.org/h/henry/mhc2/MHC26037.HTM)).
- `7` [C. A. Strine, Descriptions of the Temple in First Kings and Ezekiel](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/descriptions-of-the-temple-in-first-kings-and-ezekiel/) (Bible Odyssey): **what is the temple of chapters 40–48?** Strine notes that Ezekiel's temple appears "in a vision", that the prophet gives the measurements of every part "but never the height of anything", and that "Ezekiel's temple can't be built, only drawn on a flat surface". Enter the Bible holds that "These are not literal building plans, ritual codes, and territorial maps but an idealized and parable-like presentation" ([Enter the Bible, Introductory Issues in Ezekiel](https://enterthebible.org/courses/ezekiel/lessons/introductory-issues-in-ezekiel/)). The 1909 *Catholic Encyclopedia* reads it as "a symbolic representation of the temple, that was to find spiritual realization in God's new kingdom" ([Catholic Encyclopedia (1909), Ezekiel](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/05737b.htm)), and Matthew Henry as pointing to "a spiritual temple, even the gospel-church", for "these things cannot be literally, but must spiritually, understood" ([Matthew Henry, Commentary on the Whole Bible, Ezekiel 40](https://www.ccel.org/h/henry/mhc2/MHC26040.HTM)). Many dispensationalist Protestants expect a literal temple: Randall Price argues that "this future fulfillment must be in a literally rebuilt Temple" in the millennial kingdom, and reports that most who read it literally hold its sacrifices "will be memorials of the sacrifice of Christ" ([Randall Price, How Can Dispensationalists Believe in the Literal Fulfillment of Ezekiel's Temple?, The Pre-Trib Research Center](https://pre-trib.org/articles/dr-randall-price/popup/text/print/655)). Steve Gregg replies that the sacrifices are said to "make atonement" ([Ezekiel 45:17](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/EZK45.htm#V17)), which conflicts with Christ's once-for-all sacrifice ([Hebrews 10:11–18](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/HEB10.htm#V11)), and that the witness of Christ and the apostles "provides the strongest objections to any literalistic interpretation of Ezekiel 40–48" ([Steve Gregg, Making Sense of Ezekiel's Temple Vision, Christian Research Institute](https://www.equip.org/articles/making-sense-ezekiels-temple-vision/)). The rabbis noted that its laws seemed to "contradict matters of Torah" ([Sefaria, Shabbat 13b](https://www.sefaria.org/Shabbat.13b.8)).
- `7` [Origen, De Principiis, Book I](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/04121.htm): **is the king of Tyre a figure of Satan?** ([Ezekiel 28:11–19](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/EZK28.htm#V11)) Origen held that the second prophecy against the prince of Tyre "cannot be at all understood of a man, but of some superior power which had fallen away from a higher position" (chapter 5), and Tertullian read it "in reference to the devil", an angel "both by creation good and by choice corrupt" ([Tertullian, Against Marcion, Book II](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/03122.htm), chapter 10). Matthew Henry reports that "Many expositors have suggested" an allusion "to the fall of the angels that sinned", but would rather refer it, if anything, "to the fall of Adam" ([Matthew Henry, Commentary on the Whole Bible, Ezekiel 28](https://www.ccel.org/h/henry/mhc2/MHC26028.HTM)). Enter the Bible notes that "Classical Christian authors have often taken the king of Tyre to be a 'type' or figure of Satan", but holds that this "reads too much into the text, which is actually about a human being" ([Enter the Bible, Introductory Issues in Ezekiel](https://enterthebible.org/courses/ezekiel/lessons/introductory-issues-in-ezekiel/)).
- `7` [Sara Wells, Ezekiel](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/ezekiel/) (Bible Odyssey): **how should the violent imagery of chapters 16 and 23 be read?** ([Ezekiel 16:1–63](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/EZK16.htm#V1), [Ezekiel 23:1–49](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/EZK23.htm#V1)) Wells explains that Jerusalem, "representing Judah's leaders, is depicted as God's unfaithful wife", and that the metaphor "takes aim at Jerusalem's shifting loyalties and alliances". Its "graphic imagery and portrayal of God as an enraged husband and Jerusalem as a punished woman have often troubled interpreters": some scholars suggest that the violence "echoes the trauma of war and exile", while "others caution that the metaphor may reveal negative assumptions about women". Stuart Macwilliam writes that "Some scholars attempt to rehabilitate the text, pointing out, for instance, the scattered references to reconciliation", while "feminist scholars have led the way in exposing the damage caused by a metaphor that associates man with God and woman with lust and betrayal" ([Stuart Macwilliam, Marriage Metaphors in the Prophets, Bible Odyssey](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/marriage-metaphors-in-the-prophets/)). The Catholic Catechism cites chapter 16 for a love that "will be victorious over even the worst infidelities" ([Vatican, Catechism of the Catholic Church, 199–231](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P16.HTM), 219), and Matthew Henry reads it as God "justifying himself", and as "designed for admonition to us" ([Matthew Henry, Commentary on the Whole Bible, Ezekiel 16](https://www.ccel.org/h/henry/mhc2/MHC26016.HTM)).
- `6` [Enter the Bible, Introductory Issues in Ezekiel](https://enterthebible.org/courses/ezekiel/lessons/introductory-issues-in-ezekiel/) (Luther Seminary): **who wrote the book?** Enter the Bible notes that critical scholars "sought to find the 'real' words of Ezekiel", "often seeing much of the book as the work of later hands. However, little consensus has been reached among the experts"; contemporary scholars read it "as a whole literary work while also acknowledging that the book has been edited and expanded some over time". The 1909 *Catholic Encyclopedia* held that "The genuineness of the book of Ezekiel is generally conceded", and that those who doubted chapters 40–48 "overlook the fact that Ezekiel here gives a symbolic representation of the temple" ([Catholic Encyclopedia (1909), Ezekiel](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/05737b.htm)). The ESV Global Study Bible dates the oracles by the book's own dates, from 593 to 571 BC ([Crossway, ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to Ezekiel](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-ezekiel/)), and the Talmud says that the members of the Great Assembly "wrote" it ([Sefaria, Bava Batra 15a](https://www.sefaria.org/Bava_Batra.15a.2)).
- `6` [Bible Odyssey, Gog (HarperCollins Bible Dictionary)](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/dictionary/gog/): **who are Gog and Magog?** ([Ezekiel 38:1–39:29](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/EZK38.htm#V1)) The HarperCollins Bible Dictionary takes Gog to be "Historically probably Gyges, a seventh-century BCE king of Lydia", "transformed in (Ezek 38-39) into an apocalyptic figure", who reappears with Magog in Revelation ([Revelation 20:7–9](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/REV20.htm#V7)). Enter the Bible notes that "Scholars are divided": one option is "disguised prophecies against Babylon", while "A popular view today" sees "end-time or apocalyptic enemies of God" ([Enter the Bible, Introductory Issues in Ezekiel](https://enterthebible.org/courses/ezekiel/lessons/introductory-issues-in-ezekiel/)). Augustine held that Gog and Magog "are not to be understood of some barbarous nations in some part of the world", but of the nations "spread over the whole earth" ([Augustine, City of God, Book XX](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/120120.htm), chapter 11), and Matthew Henry that the prophecy was most probably fulfilled after the return from exile, perhaps under Antiochus Epiphanes ([Matthew Henry, Commentary on the Whole Bible, Ezekiel 38](https://www.ccel.org/h/henry/mhc2/MHC26038.HTM)). The WEBBE reads "the prince of Rosh" ([Ezekiel 38:2](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/EZK38.htm#V2)); the dispensationalist Thomas Ice holds that the evidence "is overwhelming that this is a reference to the modern Russians" ([Thomas Ice, Ezekiel 38 & 39 (Part 3), The Pre-Trib Research Center](https://pre-trib.org/dr-thomas-ice/popup/text/print/204)), while Ligonier Ministries rejects that reading "because the nation of Russia did not exist in Ezekiel's day" ([Ligonier Ministries, Gog and Magog (Tabletalk devotional, 2013)](https://learn.ligonier.org/devotionals/gog-and-magog)).
