# Deuteronomy

Deuteronomy is the fifth and last book of the Torah, or Pentateuch. It gathers the farewell speeches of Moses to Israel "beyond the Jordan, in the land of Moab" ([Deuteronomy 1:5](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/DEU01.htm#V5)), as the people wait to enter the promised land. Moses recalls their journey from Horeb, repeats the Ten Commandments and the law, and renews the covenant with them before he dies. Its Hebrew name, *Devarim* (also spelt *Debarim*), means "words", from its opening phrase: "These are the words which Moses spoke to all Israel" ([Deuteronomy 1:1](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/DEU01.htm#V1)). Its English name comes from the Greek of the Septuagint, *deuteronomion*, "copy of the law", and the introduction to the New American Bible calls it fitting, because the book serves as a "second law". The same introduction describes the book as "a kind of survival manual" for Israel: the people must choose between loving the LORD and keeping his commandments, or serving "other gods", and the choice brings "blessing" or "curse" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Deuteronomy](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/deuteronomy/0)).

Jewish and Christian tradition attributed the book, with the rest of the Torah, to Moses ([Bible Odyssey, The Torah/The Pentateuch](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/the-torah-the-pentateuch/)). The book itself says that "Moses wrote this law" and gave it to the priests ([Deuteronomy 31:9](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/DEU31.htm#V9)), and it ends with his death ([Deuteronomy 34:5](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/DEU34.htm#V5)). Most modern scholars link the book with the "book of the law" found in the temple in Jerusalem under King Josiah ([2 Kings 22:8](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2KI22.htm#V8)) and the reform that followed ([2 Kings 22–23](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2KI22.htm)). The HarperCollins Bible Dictionary dates the reform to about 620 BC and notes that the book is "generally considered some form of Deuteronomy" ([Bible Odyssey, Josiah](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/dictionary/josiah/)), and the New American Bible's introduction holds that the book was "probably composed over the course of three centuries, from the eighth century to the exile and beyond" (see "Open questions").

Every church reads Deuteronomy as part of the Old Testament. It is also Jewish Scripture, the last book of the Torah. Its call "Hear, Israel" ([Deuteronomy 6:4–9](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/DEU06.htm#V4)), the Shema, is recited with [Deuteronomy 11:13–21](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/DEU11.htm#V13) and [Numbers 15:37–41](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/NUM15.htm#V37) in Jewish morning and evening prayer, a practice "known since late Second Temple times" ([Jeffrey Tigay, Deuteronomy 6:4–The Shema](https://www.myjewishlearning.com/article/deuteronomy-64-the-shema/)). Jesus names the Shema as the first commandment ([Mark 12:28–34](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MRK12.htm#V28)), and he answers the tempter in the wilderness with words of Deuteronomy ([Matthew 4:1–11](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MAT04.htm#V1)).

The New American Bible's introduction divides the book into three addresses by Moses and the account of his death. The table follows that division, with the long second address split into its parts.

## How the churches read it

Where the churches, or their interpreters, read the book differently, or where it has played a part in their history. Each entry starts with its source's score.

- `7` [Catechism of the Catholic Church, 2083](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P7A.HTM): asked which commandment is the greatest, Jesus answers with the Shema: "Hear, Israel, the Lord our God, the Lord is one" ([Mark 12:28–34](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MRK12.htm#V28)). The **Catechism of the Catholic Church** opens its teaching on the commandments about God with Jesus' summary, which "immediately echoes the solemn call: 'Hear, O Israel: the LORD our God is one LORD.'" The **Heidelberg Catechism** answers the question "What does God's law require of us?" with the same summary, citing Deuteronomy 6:5 ([Heidelberg Catechism, Q&A 4](https://www.crcna.org/welcome/beliefs/confessions/heidelberg-catechism)), and the **Westminster Larger Catechism** sums up our duty to God as "to love the Lord our God with all our heart, and with all our soul, and with all our strength, and with all our mind" ([Westminster Larger Catechism, Q. 102](https://www.opc.org/lc.html)). See `LOVE-1` in [Love of God and neighbour](../../general/love.md).
- `7` [Philip Alexander, Sabbath in the Christian Tradition, 3.2: Sabbath and the structure of salvation-history](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Christianity/Sabbath#section3.2) (St Andrews Encyclopaedia of Theology): Deuteronomy's Ten Commandments ground the Sabbath not in creation, as Exodus does, but in the memory of slavery in Egypt ([Deuteronomy 5:12–15](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/DEU05.htm#V12)); the master who lets his slaves rest "should recall that he was himself once a slave in Egypt, and God freed him". The **Catechism of the Catholic Church** sees in the Lord's day "a memorial of Israel's liberation from bondage in Egypt", and calls the sabbath "a day of protest against the servitude of work and the worship of money" ([Catechism of the Catholic Church, 2170–2172](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P7N.HTM)). **Luther's Large Catechism** holds that the outward Sabbath law "was given to the Jews alone", but keeps a day of rest first of all for "man-servants and maid-servants, who have been attending to their work and trade the whole week", so that they may "rest and be refreshed" ([Luther's Large Catechism, Third Commandment, 80–83](https://bookofconcord.org/large-catechism/ten-commandments/)). The churches differ on which day to keep and how strictly to rest; [Worship and the sacraments](../../general/worship.md) sets out their positions under its open questions.
- `7` [William S. Morrow, Law in the Old Testament, 7.1: Divine presence](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Christianity/LawintheOldTestament#section7.1) (St Andrews Encyclopaedia of Theology): Deuteronomy's tithe is eaten before the LORD with rejoicing, and every third year it is stored for the Levite, the foreigner, the fatherless and the widow ([Deuteronomy 14:22–29](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/DEU14.htm#V22)). Morrow notes that the book revised the system of tithes "in part because they can now be used to support indigent persons". The churches differ on whether Christians must still give a tenth. The **Pentecostal Assemblies of Canada** teach that "All Christians should conscientiously and systematically tithe their income to God" ([Pentecostal Assemblies of Canada, Statement of Essential Truths and Positions and Practices, 6.5](https://paoc.org/docs/default-source/fellowship-services-documents/constitutions/2026/2026-statement-of-essential-truths-and-positions-and-practices.pdf?sfvrsn=9e0cc06a_4)). The **Baptist Faith and Message** asks Christians to give "cheerfully, regularly, systematically, proportionately, and liberally", without naming a share ([Baptist Faith and Message, XIII](https://bfm.sbc.net/bfm2000/#xiii)), and the **Catechism of the Catholic Church** speaks of providing for the material needs of the Church "each according to his abilities" ([Catechism of the Catholic Church, 2043](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P75.HTM)). [Money and generosity](../../general/money.md) sets out these positions under its open questions.

## Open questions

Questions on which the churches or scholars disagree, for example about the book's authorship or the meaning of a passage. These guidelines don't pick a side.

- `7` [William S. Morrow, Law in the Old Testament, 7: Law in Deuteronomy](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Christianity/LawintheOldTestament#section7) (St Andrews Encyclopaedia of Theology): when was Deuteronomy written, and by whom? Jewish and Christian tradition attributed the book, with the rest of the Torah, to Moses ([Bible Odyssey, The Torah/The Pentateuch](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/the-torah-the-pentateuch/)). The ESV Global Study Bible, an evangelical study Bible, introduces it as "largely a sermon, or set of sermons, preached by Moses to all of Israel shortly before his death", and dates its setting to about 1406 BC ([ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to Deuteronomy](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-deuteronomy/)). Most modern scholars link the book with the "book of the law" found under King Josiah ([2 Kings 22:8–13](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2KI22.htm#V8), [Bible Odyssey, Josiah](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/dictionary/josiah/)). The New American Bible's introduction holds that it was "probably composed over the course of three centuries, from the eighth century to the exile and beyond" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Deuteronomy](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/deuteronomy/0)). Morrow writes that "A late pre-exilic version was rewritten for an exilic and then for a post-exilic audience", and notes that "it is uncertain whether the original version of Deuteronomy corresponds completely to the policies of either king", Hezekiah or Josiah ([Law in the Old Testament, 7.1](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Christianity/LawintheOldTestament#section7.1)).
- `7` [Charlie Trimm, Destruction of the Canaanites](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/destruction-of-the-canaanites/) (Society of Biblical Literature, Bible Odyssey): how should Christians read the commands to destroy the peoples of Canaan? Deuteronomy commands Israel to "utterly destroy" the seven nations of Canaan and to "save alive nothing that breathes" in their cities, so that they will not lead Israel to "serve other gods" ([Deuteronomy 7:1–5](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/DEU07.htm#V1), [Deuteronomy 20:16–18](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/DEU20.htm#V16)). Trimm calls this "a major ethical problem for many modern readers because of its similarity to modern acts of genocide", and describes three kinds of response among scholars. Some read the texts in their ancient setting, for example as hyperbole like other ancient Near Eastern accounts of war. Others reject the texts' ethical claims, on historical grounds or through a "pacifistic-Jesus lens". Others defend them on theological grounds, appealing to God's sovereignty, Canaanite sin and divine judgement. William Morrow notes that when Deuteronomy 7 was composed, "likely in the exilic era", the peoples it names "had long disappeared or been assimilated" ([St Andrews Encyclopaedia of Theology, Law in the Old Testament, 7.2](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Christianity/LawintheOldTestament#section7.2)). Benedict XVI writes that revelation "is suited to the cultural and moral level of distant times", and so describes "acts of violence and massacre, without explicitly denouncing the immorality of such things". He encourages scholars and pastors to help the faithful read such passages "in the light of the mystery of Christ" ([Verbum Domini, 42](https://www.vatican.va/content/benedict-xvi/en/apost_exhortations/documents/hf_ben-xvi_exh_20100930_verbum-domini.html)).
