# 1 Kings

1 Kings tells the story of Israel's kings from the last days of David to the death of Ahab. It opens as the aged David has Solomon anointed king, and gives most of its first half to Solomon's reign: his prayer for wisdom, his judgements and his wealth, and above all the temple he builds in Jerusalem and dedicates with a long prayer [1 Kings 3:9](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1KI03.htm#V9), [1 Kings 8:22–30](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1KI08.htm#V22). Late in life, "his wives turned away his heart after other gods" [1 Kings 11:4](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1KI11.htm#V4), and after his death the kingdom splits in two: the northern tribes make Jeroboam their king, while Solomon's son Rehoboam keeps Judah [1 Kings 12:16–20](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1KI12.htm#V16). The second half follows the kings of both kingdoms, and in it the prophets come forward, above all Elijah, who confronts King Ahab and Queen Jezebel over the worship of Baal [1 Kings 17:1](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1KI17.htm#V1), [1 Kings 18:21](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1KI18.htm#V21). The USCCB introduction explains that the book judges each king "on theological grounds, with no allowance made for political or economic successes or failures": "The northern kings are condemned without exception", while Judah's kings "follow a cyclic pattern of infidelity followed by reform", and "Into the stories of the kings, almost as a counterpoint, are woven numerous stories of prophets" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Books of Kings](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/1kings/0)). The evangelical ESV Global Study Bible reads the books as showing "that Israel suffers again and again because of its great sinfulness", while "God remains ready to forgive those who repent" ([ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to 1–2 Kings](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-1-2-kings/)).

1 and 2 Kings were one book in the Hebrew Bible, where Sefaria describes Kings I, *Melakhim Aleph*, as "the fourth book of the Prophets" ([Sefaria, I Kings](https://www.sefaria.org/I_Kings)). Christian Bibles count it among the historical books, and in the Orthodox Church's listing, "which is generally that of the Septuagint", "1 and 2 Samuel are called 1 and 2 Kings, and 1 and 2 Kings are called 3 and 4 Kings" ([Orthodox Church in America, The Orthodox Faith: Old Testament, History](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/doctrine-scripture/old-testament/history)). The story continues in [2 Kings](2-kings.md), from Elijah's departure to the fall of Jerusalem; the books of [1 Chronicles](1-chronicles.md) and [2 Chronicles](2-chronicles.md) tell much of it again.

The book names no author. The Talmud holds that "Jeremiah wrote his own book, and the book of Kings, and Lamentations" ([Sefaria, Bava Batra 15a:2](https://www.sefaria.org/Bava_Batra.15a.2)). The ESV Global Study Bible holds that "The author or authors of these two books is unknown". The USCCB introduction notes that the books are "regarded by many as the last part of a work commonly known as the Deuteronomistic History", drawing on older sources such as "The Book of the Chronicles of the Kings", and that "in the form we have it the work comes from the time of the exile" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Books of Kings](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/1kings/0)). The debate is set out under open questions below.

## How the churches read it

Where the churches, or their interpreters, read the book differently, or where it has played a part in their history. Each entry starts with its source's score.

- `8` [Paul VI, Populorum Progressio, 23](https://www.vatican.va/content/paul-vi/en/encyclicals/documents/hf_p-vi_enc_26031967_populorum.html) (1967): on **Naboth's vineyard** [1 Kings 21:1–19](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1KI21.htm#V1), St Ambrose of Milan wrote a treatise, *On Naboth*, in the late 380s, drawing "Extractions for Christian living from the story of Naboth's vineyard" ([Fourth Century Christianity, Ambrose, On Naboth](https://www.fourthcentury.com/ambrose-on-naboth/)). In the **Catholic Church**, Pope Paul VI quoted it, citing *De Nabute*, on "the duty of the rich toward the poor": "You are not making a gift of what is yours to the poor man, but you are giving him back what is his". These words, the encyclical continues, "indicate that the right to private property is not absolute and unconditional". The **Reformed** commentator Matthew Henry reads Ahab's desire for the vineyard as "contrary to the law of contentment, and the letter of the tenth commandment", and the story as a call "to lament the hard case of oppressed innocency, and to mingle our tears with the tears of the oppressed" ([Matthew Henry, Commentary on the Whole Bible, 1 Kings 21](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/henry/mhc2.iKi.xxii.html)).
- `7` [Catechism of the Catholic Church, 2568–2589](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P91.HTM): on **Solomon's prayer at the dedication of the temple** [1 Kings 8:22–53](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1KI08.htm#V22), the **Catholic** Catechism teaches that the prayer "relies on God's promise and covenant", and that the king "begs the Lord, on his own behalf, on behalf of the entire people, and of the generations yet to come, for the forgiveness of their sins and for their daily needs" (2580). In the **Orthodox Church**, the readings listed for 13 September 2026, when it commemorates the founding of the Church of the Resurrection in Jerusalem in 335, begin with "3[1] Kings 8:22-23, 27-30" ([Orthodox Church in America, Scripture Readings for 13 September 2026](https://www.oca.org/readings/daily/2026/09/13)).
- `7` [Orthodox Church in America, Holy, Glorious Prophet Elijah](https://www.oca.org/saints/lives/2026/07/20/102060-holy-glorious-prophet-elijah): on **Elijah** [1 Kings 17–19](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1KI17.htm), the **Orthodox Church** commemorates him on 20 July as "one of the greatest of the prophets and the first dedicated to virginity in the Old Testament", tells how at Horeb "The Lord was in 'a gentle breeze' (3 Kings 19: 12)", and holds that "According to the Tradition of Holy Church, the Prophet Elijah will be the Forerunner of the Dread Second Coming of Christ"; Orthodox Christians "pray to him for deliverance from drought". The **Catholic** Catechism calls Elijah "the 'father' of the prophets", notes that "the Eastern liturgies repeat Elijah's plea in the Eucharistic epiclesis", and teaches that only at the Transfiguration "will Moses and Elijah behold the unveiled face of him whom they sought" ([Catechism of the Catholic Church, 2582–2583](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P91.HTM), [Matthew 17:3](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MAT17.htm#V3)). The Catholic **Carmelite** order, which began among hermits on Mount Carmel, holds that "Carmelites from the very beginning of the Order have looked to Elijah for inspiration", as "a man of deep contemplation, one who sought solitude in the wadi Carith or in the cave at Mount Horeb" ([Order of Carmelites, Nine Themes in Carmelite Spirituality, 7: Carmel is Elijan](https://www.ocarm.org/en/item/2287)), and "looks to Elijah as our spiritual father" ([Carmelites in Australia and Timor-Leste, Mary and Elijah](https://carmelites.org.au/mary-and-elijah)).
- `7` [Augustine, The City of God, Book XVII, 8](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/120117.htm): on **Solomon as a figure of Christ** [1 Kings 3:12](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1KI03.htm#V12), [1 Kings 6:1](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1KI06.htm#V1), St Augustine holds that the promise to David is fulfilled not in Solomon but in Christ, yet that "even in Solomon there appeared some image of the future event, in that he built the temple, and had peace according to his name", so that "as a shadow of Him that should come, he foreshowed Christ our Lord". Jesus says that the queen of Sheba "came from the ends of the earth to hear the wisdom of Solomon; and behold, someone greater than Solomon is here" [Matthew 12:42](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MAT12.htm#V42), and the **Reformed** commentator Matthew Henry comments on Solomon's riches: "Yet what was he to the King of kings? Where Christ is, by his word and Spirit, 'Behold, a greater than Solomon is there'" ([Matthew Henry, Commentary on the Whole Bible, 1 Kings 10](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/henry/mhc2.iKi.xi.html)).
- `7` [John Calvin, Institutes of the Christian Religion, Book IV, Chapter 1](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/calvin/institutes.vi.ii.html): on **the seven thousand who did not bow to Baal** [1 Kings 19:18](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1KI19.htm#V18), Paul uses God's answer to Elijah to show that "at this present time also there is a remnant according to the election of grace" [Romans 11:2–5](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ROM11.htm#V2) (see [Romans](../new-testament/romans.md)). The **Reformed** theologian John Calvin teaches from it that "God wondrously preserves his Church, while placing it as it were in concealment. Thus it was said to Elijah, 'Yet I have left me seven thousand in Israel'" (IV.1.2).
- `6` [Sergew Hable Selassie, The Establishment of the Ethiopian Church](https://www.ethiopianorthodox.org/english/ethiopian/prechristian.html) (Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church): on **the queen of Sheba** [1 Kings 10:1–13](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1KI10.htm#V1), the **Ethiopian Orthodox** tradition, recorded in the *Kebre Negest* ("The Glory of the king"), tells that she bore Solomon a son, Menelik, who "came home accompanied by many Israelites, the sons of Levites and bringing with him the Ark of the Covenant". Christine Mitchell notes that "The Ethiopian Orthodox Church teaches that their son was the first great king of Ethiopia, Menelik I", although "the biblical texts clearly state that the Queen of Sheba returned to her own land after her meeting with Solomon" ([Bible Odyssey, Christine Mitchell, Queen of Sheba](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/queen-of-sheba/), [1 Kings 10:13](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1KI10.htm#V13)).

## Open questions

Questions on which the churches or scholars disagree, for example about the book's authorship or the meaning of a passage. These guidelines don't pick a side.

- `7` [USCCB, Introduction to the Books of Kings](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/1kings/0) (New American Bible, Revised Edition): **who wrote the books of Kings, and when?** The books refer their readers to older works, such as "the book of the acts of Solomon" and "the book of the chronicles of the kings of Israel" [1 Kings 11:41](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1KI11.htm#V41), [1 Kings 14:19](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1KI14.htm#V19). The Talmud names Jeremiah as the author ([Sefaria, Bava Batra 15a:2](https://www.sefaria.org/Bava_Batra.15a.2)), and the ESV Global Study Bible holds that the author "is unknown" ([ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to 1–2 Kings](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-1-2-kings/)). The USCCB introduction holds that "There may have been an edition sometime late in the reign of Josiah (640–609 B.C.), but in the form we have it the work comes from the time of the exile", and that the Deuteronomistic History "underwent a complex process of editorial revision whose stages are disputed by scholars". Steven McKenzie sets out the theories: Martin Noth's single exilic author, Frank Moore Cross's edition under Josiah updated after the exile, and Rudolf Smend's later layers; some scholars deny that the history was ever "an original, continuous work", while Thomas Römer has proposed a compromise, a work "produced by a school of court scribes who maintained a library of scrolls that were revised under Josiah, in the exile, and then in the Persian period" ([Bible Odyssey, Steven L. McKenzie, Deuteronomistic History](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/deuteronomistic-history/)).
- `7` [Bible Odyssey, Sarah Malena, Solomon](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/solomon/): **how much of Solomon's glory is history?** The ESV Global Study Bible holds that "Solomon's reign marked the high point of Israel's power and wealth in biblical times", and dates it to about 971–931 BC ([ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to 1–2 Kings](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-1-2-kings/)). Malena notes that "we do not have any evidence of Solomon from his own day (mid-tenth century BCE)", but holds that "there is good reason to believe that the legendary Solomon grew from a historical core", and that the arrangement of his story in 1 Kings 1–11 "prioritizes themes over chronological or historical accuracy". Christine Hayes explains that "archaeologists have found that Jerusalem was a small town" until the end of the eighth century, that "there are very few material remains that attest to a fabulous empire on a scale that's suggested by the biblical text", and that archaeologists "differ radically" over the dating of the gateways excavated at Hazor, Megiddo and Gezer, the cities named in [1 Kings 9:15](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1KI09.htm#V15) ([Christine Hayes, The Deuteronomistic History: Response to Catastrophe (1 and 2 Kings), Open Yale Courses](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-14)).
- `7` [USCCB, 1 Kings, chapter 22](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/1kings/22) (New American Bible, Revised Edition): **does God send a lying spirit?** In Micaiah's vision the LORD asks, "Who will entice Ahab?", and a spirit offers to "be a lying spirit in the mouth of all his prophets" [1 Kings 22:19–23](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1KI22.htm#V19). The note in the New American Bible reads the vision as revealing "God's plan to trick Ahab to his death", so that Micaiah "virtually dares Ahab to walk into the trap with his eyes open". Matthew Henry reads it instead as showing "That there are malicious and lying spirits" which seek "to put lies into the mouths of prophets", and that "It is not without the divine permission that the devil deceives men, and even thereby God serves his own purposes" ([Matthew Henry, Commentary on the Whole Bible, 1 Kings 22](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/henry/mhc2.iKi.xxiii.html)).
- `6` [ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to 1–2 Kings](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-1-2-kings/): **when did the kings reign?** The ESV Global Study Bible dates the monarchy to 970–586 BC and the northern kingdom to 931–722 BC. The New American Bible's notes hold instead that "The problem of the chronology of the kings of Israel and Judah has never been convincingly resolved; it is complicated by the fact that the ancient Greek translation sometimes has different lengths of reign and different accession dates" ([USCCB, 1 Kings, chapter 22](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/1kings/22)).

## Tutorials and further reading

- `7` [Christine Hayes, The Deuteronomistic History: Response to Catastrophe (1 and 2 Kings)](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-14) (Open Yale Courses, Introduction to the Old Testament, lecture 14): a university lecture, with a transcript, on Solomon, the division of the kingdom, the fall of Israel and Judah, and how the Deuteronomistic historian explained them.
