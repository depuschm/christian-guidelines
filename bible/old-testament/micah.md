# Micah

The Book of Micah "consists of a collection of speeches, proclamations of punishment and of salvation, attributed to the prophet Micah" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Micah](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/micah/0)). Its heading names "Micah of Morasheth" and places his word "in the days of Jotham, Ahaz, and Hezekiah, kings of Judah", concerning Samaria and Jerusalem ([Micah 1:1](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MIC01.htm#V1)), in the second half of the eighth century BC; "Micah was a contemporary of the prophet Isaiah", and "Unlike Isaiah, who was a native of the holy city, Micah was an outsider from the countryside" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Micah](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/micah/0)). Christine Hayes calls him "the last of the eighth-century prophets", who "seems to have been a rural prophet who spoke for the poor farmers": "The greedy landowners, the dishonest merchants, the aristocracy, they're all targets of his denunciations", and where Isaiah trusted that God would protect Jerusalem, Micah "ridicules the idea of the inviolability of Zion" ([Christine Hayes, Introduction to the Old Testament, Lecture 18, Open Yale Courses](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-18)). His name is a shortened form of Micaiah, "who is like Yah[weh]?" ([Bible Odyssey, Micah (HarperCollins Bible Dictionary)](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/dictionary/micah/)), and the book ends: "Who is a God like you, who pardons iniquity" ([Micah 7:18](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MIC07.htm#V18)). A century later, when Jeremiah was on trial for his life, elders of the land recalled that "Micah the Morashtite prophesied in the days of Hezekiah" that "Zion will be ploughed as a field", and that King Hezekiah did not put him to death but sought the LORD's favour ([Jeremiah 26:17–19](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JER26.htm#V17); [Jeremiah](jeremiah.md)), "The solitary reference to Micah outside the book" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Micah](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/micah/0)).

The book moves twice from judgement to hope: in the first section "chaps. 1–3 consist almost entirely of prophecies of punishment, and chaps. 4–5 of prophecies of salvation", and the second "also moves from prophecies of punishment (6:1–7:6) to confidence in God’s salvation (7:7–20)" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Micah](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/micah/0)). The ESV Global Study Bible names its theme as "judgment and forgiveness": "The Lord, the Judge who scatters his people for their sins, is also the Shepherd-King who in covenant faithfulness gathers, protects, and forgives them" ([ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to Micah](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-micah/)). The USCCB introduction adds that "Just how much of the Book of Micah can be traced to the eighth-century prophet is uncertain"; that is one of the open questions below.

Micah is one of the twelve short prophetic books that the Hebrew Bible counts as one, "the Twelve": "Micah (“Micha”) is one of 12 books of Minor Prophets (“Trei Asar)”, marked by their shortness" ([Sefaria, Micah](https://www.sefaria.org/Micah)), and the Talmud counts "the Twelve Prophets" as one book among those written by "The members of the Great Assembly" ([Sefaria, Bava Batra 15a:2](https://www.sefaria.org/Bava_Batra.15a.2)); Sirach already praises "the twelve prophets" ([Sirach 49:10](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/SIR49.htm#V10); [deuterocanon](../README.md#deuterocanon)). Micah is "the sixth of the Twelve Minor Prophets", as in the WEBBE's order ([Orthodox Church in America, Lives of all saints commemorated on August 14](https://www.oca.org/saints/all-lives/2026/08/14)); in the Greek Septuagint it stands third, after Hosea and Amos ([NETS, The Twelve Prophets, translated by George E. Howard](https://ccat.sas.upenn.edu/nets/edition/32-twelve-nets.pdf)). The Orthodox Church in America notes that "The book of Micah dates from approximately the same period and is very similar in content to Isaiah" ([Orthodox Church in America, The Orthodox Faith: Old Testament, Prophets](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/doctrine-scripture/old-testament/prophets); [Isaiah](isaiah.md)). Every church reads Micah among the prophets. In Jewish practice the book's closing words lie behind *tashlich*, "the ritual of symbolically casting our sins into the sea" at the New Year: the medieval rabbi known as the Maharil is quoted in the Shulchan Aruch as saying that it was customary to go to a river and recite the verse from Micah that ends "hurling our sins into the depths of the sea" ([Danielle Upbin, Tashlich's Origin Story, My Jewish Learning](https://www.myjewishlearning.com/article/tashlichs-origin-story/); [Micah 7:19](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MIC07.htm#V19)).

## How the churches read it

Where the churches, or their interpreters, read the book differently, or where it has played a part in their history. Each entry starts with its source's score.

- `7` [USCCB, Readings for the Fourth Sunday of Advent (22 December 2024)](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/readings/122224.cfm): **Bethlehem and the birth of Christ.** Matthew's Gospel has the chief priests and scribes answer Herod's question about where the Christ would be born with this prophecy ([Matthew 2:4–6](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MAT02.htm#V4); [Matthew](../new-testament/matthew.md)). In the **Catholic** lectionary, Micah 5:1–4a in the New American Bible's numbering ([Micah 5:2–5](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MIC05.htm#V2) in the WEBBE) is the first reading on the Fourth Sunday of Advent in Year C, with Mary's visit to Elizabeth ([Luke 1:39–45](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/LUK01.htm#V39)). The New American Bible notes that "Salvation will come through a 'messiah,' an anointed ruler", and that "The Gospel of Matthew reports that the chief priests and scribes cite this passage as the ancient promise of a messiah in the line of David to be born in Bethlehem" ([USCCB, Micah, chapter 5, with notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/micah/5)). The Revised Common Lectionary, used by many **Protestant** churches, reads Micah 5:2–5a on the same Sunday ([Vanderbilt Divinity Library, Revised Common Lectionary: Sunday citations](https://lectionary.library.vanderbilt.edu/sunday-citations/)). In **Orthodox** teaching, "In Micah is found the prophecy of the Savior’s birth in Bethlehem (5.2–4)" ([Orthodox Church in America, The Orthodox Faith: Old Testament, Prophets](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/doctrine-scripture/old-testament/prophets)), and Micah 5:2–4 is among the readings for the eve of the Nativity ([Orthodox Church in America, Scripture Readings for 24 December 2026](https://www.oca.org/readings/daily/2026/12/24)).
- `7` [Church of England, Common Worship: The Liturgy of Good Friday](https://www.churchofengland.org/liturgy/common-worship/seasonal-material/passiontide/liturgy-good-friday): **"My people, what have I done to you?" on Good Friday.** God's complaint in [Micah 6:3](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MIC06.htm#V3) became the refrain of the Reproaches, sung during the veneration of the cross. The **Catholic** Encyclopedia of 1910 describes the Improperia as "the reproaches which in the liturgy of the Office of Good Friday the Saviour is made to utter against the Jews", rendered by the choir "during the Adoration of the Cross" and beginning with the "Popule meus" of Micah 6:3; "Definite references to it are found in documents of the ninth and tenth centuries" ([Catholic Encyclopedia (1910), Improperia](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/07703a.htm)). The **Anglican** Common Worship gives a revised text, "O my people, O my Church, what have I done to you, or in what have I offended you?", and notes that where the traditional form is sung, "it is important to remember that Jesus’ words are to be understood as applying to the present Church, rather than to his own contemporaries": "‘Israel’ and ‘my people’ stand for the Church, and we are to hear the Reproaches as directed to our own hardness of heart and failure of discipleship."
- `7` [USCCB, Micah, chapter 6, with notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/micah/6) (New American Bible, Revised Edition): **what the LORD requires.** The **Catholic** New American Bible notes that 6:6–8 "borrows from a priestly liturgical pattern", in which worshippers asked a priest what offering to bring, and explains the answer: "To do justice refers to human behavior in relationship to others. To love goodness refers to the kind of love and concern which is at the heart of the covenant between the Lord and Israel; it is persistently faithful. To walk humbly with your God means to listen carefully to the revealed will of God." The Catholic weekday lectionary reads [Micah 6:1–4](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MIC06.htm#V1) and [Micah 6:6–8](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MIC06.htm#V6) with Jesus' words on the sign of Jonah ([Matthew 12:38–42](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MAT12.htm#V38); [USCCB, Readings for Monday of the Sixteenth Week in Ordinary Time (20 July 2026)](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/readings/072026.cfm)), and the Revised Common Lectionary reads Micah 6:1–8 on the Fourth Sunday after the Epiphany in Year A ([Vanderbilt Divinity Library, Revised Common Lectionary: Sunday citations](https://lectionary.library.vanderbilt.edu/sunday-citations/)). For the **Reformed** tradition, Calvin read the questions of verses 6–7 as the voice of people who "ever think that God can be pacified by outward rites and frivolous performances" ([John Calvin, Commentary on Micah 6:6–8](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/calvin/calcom28.iv.6.vi.html)), and held that "God did not command sacrifices, as though they were of themselves of any worth; but he intended to lead the ancient people by such exercises to repentance and faith" ([John Calvin, Commentary on Micah, Lecture 94](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/calvin/calcom28.iv.6.viii.html)). These guidelines cite 6:8 for `JUST-3` in [Justice and the poor](../../general/justice.md).
- `7` [Orthodox Church in America, Lives of all saints commemorated on August 14](https://www.oca.org/saints/all-lives/2026/08/14): **the Orthodox commemoration of the prophet.** The **Orthodox** Church commemorates the Prophet Micah on 14 August as "a native of the city of Moresheth" and "a contemporary of the Prophet Isaiah". "To him belongs a prophecy about the birth of the Savior of the world", which it quotes in a translation ending "His goings forth were from the beginning, even from eternity" ([Micah 5:2](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MIC05.htm#V2)); it adds that "the Jews evidently were afraid to kill the Prophet Micah" ([Jeremiah 26:18–19](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JER26.htm#V18)), and that "His relics were discovered in the fourth century after the Birth of Christ".

## Open questions

Questions on which the churches or scholars disagree. These guidelines don't pick a side.

- `7` [USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Micah](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/micah/0): **how much of the book comes from Micah himself?** The USCCB introduction notes that "Tradition considers all of the words to be the recorded speeches of Micah, and some contemporary commentators agree. On the other hand, some modern scholars have thought of Micah as exclusively a prophet of doom, and therefore attributed as few as three of the seven chapters to him"; some material reflects "not the period of the Assyrian threat to Judah in the eighth century but the Babylonian exile in the sixth century B.C. and later. This is particularly evident in chap. 7". Its note on 7:8–20 holds that most of these verses "probably arose in the exilic or postexilic periods" ([USCCB, Micah, chapter 7, with notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/micah/7)). Christine Hayes notes that some scholars treat the hopeful passages as "interpolations by a later editor", but holds that "a shift in theme or tone is never a solid basis for assuming interpolation"; "Anachronism is a very good guide to interpolation", as in the references to Babylon and to the rebuilding of the walls of Jerusalem ([Christine Hayes, Introduction to the Old Testament, Lecture 18, Open Yale Courses](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-18); [Micah 4:10](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MIC04.htm#V10), [Micah 7:11](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MIC07.htm#V11)). Albin Van Hoonacker, in the Catholic Encyclopedia of 1911, recorded "a general consensus of opinion to attribute to the Prophet Micheas the authorship" of chapters 1–3, found the arguments for a later date of chapters 4–5 "not convincing", and concluded of chapters 6–7 that "it seems unnecessary to deny to the Prophet Micheas the authorship of these two chapters" ([Catholic Encyclopedia (1911), Book of Micheas](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/10278a.htm)).
- `7` [USCCB, Micah, chapter 4, with notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/micah/4) (New American Bible, Revised Edition): **who first spoke the vision of peace?** [Micah 4:1–3](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MIC04.htm#V1) and [Isaiah 2:2–4](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ISA02.htm#V2) are almost word for word the same ([Isaiah](isaiah.md)). The New American Bible notes that "It is not known if Micah or an editor of the book picked up the announcement from his contemporary Isaiah or if Isaiah borrowed it from Micah. Perhaps both Isaiah and Micah depended upon another, more ancient tradition", and that Joel "transforms the promise into a call to war": "Beat your ploughshares into swords" ([Joel 3:9–10](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JOL03.htm#V9)). The USCCB introduction takes the shared passage as a sign that the book's composition "involved a complex editorial process" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Micah](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/micah/0)), and Hayes counts it among the hopeful passages that some scholars assign to a later editor: "it's true that certain parts we see again in Isaiah" ([Christine Hayes, Introduction to the Old Testament, Lecture 18, Open Yale Courses](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-18)). Van Hoonacker held instead that chapters 4–5 "probably date from shortly after the fall of Samaria in 722 B.C." ([Catholic Encyclopedia (1911), Book of Micheas](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/10278a.htm)).
- `7` [USCCB, Micah, chapter 5, with notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/micah/5) (New American Bible, Revised Edition): **what did the promise to Bethlehem mean, and what does "from of old" mean?** The churches read [Micah 5:2](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MIC05.htm#V2) as fulfilled in the birth of Jesus in Bethlehem, as Matthew's Gospel does ([Matthew 2:4–6](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MAT02.htm#V4)). The New American Bible reads it first in its own time: "The Book of Micah shares with Isaiah the expectation that God will deliver Israel through a king in the line of David. Bethlehem-Ephrathah is the home of the Davidic line", and "she who is to give birth" in the next verse is "sometimes understood as a reference to Isaiah's Emmanuel oracle"; the ESV Global Study Bible describes "This deliverer, functioning as a new David" ([ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to Micah](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-micah/)). Jewish interpretation also reads the verse of the Messiah: Rashi explains "from you shall emerge for Me—the Messiah, son of David" ([Sefaria, Rashi on Micah 5:1](https://www.sefaria.org/Rashi_on_Micah.5.1.3)). Readers differ on the closing words, "whose goings out are from of old, from ancient times": the Orthodox Church in America translates "His goings forth were from the beginning, even from eternity" ([Orthodox Church in America, Lives of all saints commemorated on August 14](https://www.oca.org/saints/all-lives/2026/08/14)), while Calvin wrote: "Some, I know, pertinaciously maintain, that the Prophet speaks here of the eternal existence of Christ; and as for myself I willingly own that the divinity of Christ is here proved to us; but as this will never be allowed by the Jews, I prefer taking the words simply as they are, — that Christ will not come forth unexpectedly from Bethlehem, as though God had previously determined nothing respecting him" ([John Calvin, Commentary on Micah, Lecture 91](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/calvin/calcom28.iv.5.iii.html)).
