# Judges

Judges tells the story of Israel between the death of Joshua and the rise of the kings ([Judges 1:1](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JDG01.htm#V1)). The introduction to the New American Bible explains that the Hebrew word translated "Judges" "refers not to specialized judicial officers or magistrates but to leaders in general", and that the period runs from the death of Joshua ([Joshua 24:29–31](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JOS24.htm#V29)) to Samuel, "who was also the last judge" ([1 Samuel 7:15–17](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1SA07.htm#V15)). After an introduction that describes "a cyclical pattern of infidelity, oppression, 'crying out,' and deliverance", the book tells of thirteen leaders: six "major judges", Othniel, Ehud, Deborah, Gideon, Jephthah and Samson, whose stories are told at length, six "minor judges" who appear in brief notices, and Abimelech, whose story is "a cautionary tale of royal ambition"; it ends with two stories that "illustrate the religious and political disorder that prevailed at the time when, as yet, 'there was no king in Israel'" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Judges](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/judges/0)). Its closing words are: "In those days there was no king in Israel. Everyone did that which was right in his own eyes" ([Judges 21:25](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JDG21.htm#V25)).

The book names no author. A tradition recorded in the Babylonian Talmud holds that "Samuel wrote his own book, the book of Judges, and the book of Ruth" ([Sefaria, Bava Batra 14b:12](https://www.sefaria.org/Bava_Batra.14b.12)). Modern scholars read Judges as part of a longer work: "Some scholars argue that the books of Deuteronomy through Kings were compiled as a single literary work, which we call the Deuteronomistic History", though how and when it was put together is debated ([Bible Odyssey, Steven L. McKenzie, Deuteronomistic History](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/deuteronomistic-history/)). The USCCB introduction holds that the Deuteronomistic historian wrote the book's introduction and the comments that frame its stories, and "drew the stories of the judges themselves from older sources, which could have existed in written form but derive ultimately from oral tradition" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Judges](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/judges/0)).

Judges is also Jewish Scripture. Under its Hebrew name *Shoftim* it is "the second book of the Prophets", telling of "cycles of sin, foreign oppression, repentance, and redemption through leaders appointed by God", and it "ends by highlighting the chaos of the period with stories about theft, idolatry, rape, murder, and civil war" ([Sefaria, Judges](https://www.sefaria.org/Judges)). Every church reads it among the historical books of its Old Testament, which follow the Law; the Orthodox Church in America's account notes that during this period "the Israelites were often unfaithful to God and given to evil" ([Orthodox Church in America, The Orthodox Faith: Old Testament, History](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/doctrine-scripture/old-testament/history)).

## How the churches read it

Where the churches, or their interpreters, read the book differently, or where it has played a part in their history. Each entry starts with its source's score.

- `7` [Ambrose, On the Holy Spirit, Book I, Introduction](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/34021.htm) (New Advent): on **Gideon and the fleece** ([Judges 6:36–40](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JDG06.htm#V36), [Judges 7:2–8](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JDG07.htm#V2)), the Church Father Ambrose of Milan reads the story as a figure of Christ: "Holy Gideon then saw the mystery beforehand." He chose three hundred men "so as to show that the world should be freed from the incursion of worse enemies, not by the multitude of their number, but by the mystery of the cross", and the dew on the fleece and then on all the ground foretells that the Scriptures "promised us this rain upon the whole earth, to water the world with the dew of the Divine Spirit at the coming of the Saviour". In **Orthodox** hymnography the fleece is a figure of the Virgin Mary, who bore Christ: the canon of the Akathist hymn sings, "Rejoice, living fleece wet with dew, which Gideon saw of old, O Virgin" ([St Jonah Orthodox Church, Akathist Canon, Ode 6](https://saintjonah.org/akathists-canons-and-services/akathist-for-lent/akathist-canon/)).
- `7` [United States Conference of Catholic Bishops, Readings for Saturday of the Third Week of Advent (19 December 2026)](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/readings/121926.cfm): in the **Catholic Church** the announcement of Samson's birth ([Judges 13:2–7](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JDG13.htm#V2), [Judges 13:24–25](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JDG13.htm#V24)) is read at Mass on 19 December, in the last days of Advent, together with the announcement of the birth of John the Baptist to Zechariah and Elizabeth, who also had no children ([Luke 1:5–25](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/LUK01.htm#V5)).
- `7` [Free Methodist Church USA, A Biblical Understanding of Women in Church Leadership](https://fmcusa.org/wp-content/uploads/FMCUSA_A-Biblical-Understanding-of-Women-in-Church-Leadership.pdf) (2024): on **Deborah** ([Judges 4:4–9](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JDG04.htm#V4)), the churches that ordain women cite her as an example. The **Free Methodist** position paper, which "affirms women in ministry and their ordination", holds that "the Lord called and empowered women to be leaders of His people" and that "Deborah (Judges 4:4–5) was a prophet and a judge with the authority to decide disputes among the people, both men and women". Teachers who hold the **complementarian** view, that "the primary responsibility of men in God's order" is "to lead and teach", read her differently: John Piper and Wayne Grudem see in Deborah "a living indictment of the weakness of Barak and other men in Israel who should have been more courageous leaders", and add that "The period of the judges is an especially precarious foundation for building a vision of God's ideal for leadership" ([John Piper and Wayne Grudem, 50 Crucial Questions, question 27](https://document.desiringgod.org/50-crucial-questions-about-manhood-and-womanhood-en.pdf)). Who the churches ordain is set out on the [1 Timothy](../new-testament/1-timothy.md) page.
- `6` [John Calvin, Commentary on Hebrews 11:32–34](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/calvin/calcom44.xvii.x.html) (Christian Classics Ethereal Library): on **the judges as examples of faith** ([Hebrews 11:32–34](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/HEB11.htm#V32)), the **Reformed** reformer writes that Hebrews "ascribes all that was praiseworthy in them to faith; though there was not one of them whose faith did not halt". Gideon hesitated, Barak "at first trembled", Samson "inconsiderately betrayed the safety of the whole people", and Jephthah, "hasty in making a foolish vow, and too obstinate in performing it, marred the finest victory by the cruel death of his own daughter"; "yet faith, though halting and imperfect, is still approved by God." The [Hebrews](../new-testament/hebrews.md) page covers the chapter as a whole.

## Open questions

Questions on which the churches or scholars disagree. These guidelines don't pick a side.

- `7` [USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Judges](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/judges/0) (New American Bible, Revised Edition): **who wrote Judges, and when?** The Talmud names Samuel ([Sefaria, Bava Batra 14b:12](https://www.sefaria.org/Bava_Batra.14b.12)), and the Catholic Encyclopedia of 1910 still argued that "there is no more probable writer thereof than Samuel", who used earlier documents ([Catholic Encyclopedia, Judges, The Book of](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/08547a.htm)). The USCCB introduction gives the book's framework to the Deuteronomistic historian, who drew on older stories. Steven McKenzie describes how Martin Noth proposed that Deuteronomy to Kings "were originally an extended history of Israel written by a single author/compiler", how others proposed several editions, how "Some scholars began advocating a return to a pre-Noth position", and Thomas Römer's view of a "school of court scribes" whose scrolls "were revised under Josiah, in the exile, and then in the Persian period" ([Bible Odyssey, Steven L. McKenzie, Deuteronomistic History](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/deuteronomistic-history/)). Even the song of Deborah is dated differently: "Some scholars think that Deborah's song was written well before the rest of the book of Judges", while "others believe that it was written during the time of the Babylonian exile" ([Bible Odyssey, Jennifer L. Koosed, Deborah](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/deborah/)).
- `7` [Ambrose, On the Duties of the Clergy, Book III, 12](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/34013.htm) (New Advent): **did Jephthah sacrifice his daughter?** The text says she asked to "bewail my virginity" and that he "did with her according to his vow which he had vowed. She was a virgin" ([Judges 11:37–39](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JDG11.htm#V37)). Ambrose reads it as a sacrifice: "it was a wretched necessity which could only be solved by the death of his child", and he warns that "It is better to make no vow than to vow what God does not wish to be paid to Him". The HarperCollins Bible Dictionary also says that "Jephthah sacrificed his daughter to fulfill a vow" ([Bible Odyssey, Jephthah (HarperCollins Bible Dictionary)](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/dictionary/jephthah/)). The medieval Jewish commentator David Kimhi (Radak), following his father, reads the vow as "it will be for God" or a burnt offering, and concludes from her mourning her virginity, and from the text's silence about a burnt offering, that "she was celibate/separated, and this is what he had vowed -- that she should be for God" ([Sefaria, Radak on Judges 11:31](https://www.sefaria.org/Radak_on_Judges.11.31)).
- `7` [Bible Odyssey, Rhiannon Graybill, The Levite's Concubine](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/the-levites-concubine/): **how should the violence against women in chapters 19–21 be read?** The story "begins with rape and murder, proceeds to dismemberment, and ends with warfare, mass kidnapping, and genocide" ([Judges 19:22–30](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JDG19.htm#V22), [Judges 21:10–23](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JDG21.htm#V10)). Graybill argues that "the rape of the concubine is framed as a harm done to her husband, not to the woman herself", that "The text is relatively uninterested in male responsibility", and that "One act of rape and murder has become many"; her bibliography includes Phyllis Trible's chapter on the story in *Texts of Terror* (1984). Tamara Cohn Eskenazi notes that the Hebrew Bible "recognizes violent treatment of women as a sign of a system gone awry", and that the households in this story and in [Genesis 19:1–11](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/GEN19.htm#V1) "are hardly considered exemplary by the texts' authors, which may suggest some criticism of readiness to sacrifice women" ([Bible Odyssey, Tamara Cohn Eskenazi, Women and Violence in the Hebrew Bible](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/women-and-violence-in-the-hebrew-bible/)), and the USCCB introduction reads the closing stories as illustrating the "disorder" of a time with no king.
