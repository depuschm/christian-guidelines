# Joel

The Book of Joel is a short book of prophecy. "In the two speeches that make up this book, Joel uses an agricultural crisis to measure his audience's knowledge of its God, warn them of a worse disaster if they ignore his preaching, and express his conviction that all faithful Judahites would someday enjoy a secure future" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Joel](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/joel/0)). A plague of locusts and a drought have ruined the fields, the vineyards and the offerings of the temple ([Joel 1:2–20](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JOL01.htm#V2)), and the prophet reads them as a warning of "the day of the LORD". "The dominant theme of the book is the 'day of the Lord'", a time "when the presence of the Lord brings judgment and/or deliverance and blessing, depending on the circumstances" ([ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to Joel](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-joel/)). James D. Nogalski notes that "The Hebrew Bible does not portray the day of the Lord as a single event at the end of time as popularized in some Christian theological traditions"; it "describes or threatens divine intervention in distinct historical events" ([James D. Nogalski, Day of the Lord in the Hebrew Bible, Bible Odyssey](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/day-of-the-lord-in-the-hebrew-bible/)). The book calls the people to fast and return to God, promises rain and harvest, then looks further ahead: from [Joel 2:28](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JOL02.htm#V28), "the book moves to a different kind of genre", and becomes "one of the first biblical passages that looks like the end-time (apocalyptic) literature" of Daniel and Revelation ([Enter the Bible, Introductory Issues in Joel](https://enterthebible.org/courses/joel/lessons/introductory-issues-in-joel/)).

The book opens with "The LORD's word that came to Joel, the son of Pethuel" ([Joel 1:1](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JOL01.htm#V1)), and says nothing more about him. The Catholic Encyclopedia notes that "Nothing is known of his life", and that his many words to the priests suggest "that Joel himself was of priestly descent" ([Catholic Encyclopedia (1910), Joel](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/08419a.htm)); the ESV Global Study Bible calls him "a prophet from Judah (perhaps Jerusalem)". The book gives no date, and when it was written is one of the open questions below.

Joel is one of the twelve minor prophets, whom Sirach already names together as "the twelve prophets" ([Sirach 49:10](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/SIR49.htm#V10)). In the Hebrew Bible the twelve form one book: the Talmud explains that Hosea's prophecy "is written together with those of Haggai, Zechariah, and Malachi in one book of the Twelve Prophets", and that "Were it written separately, since it is small it would be lost" ([Sefaria, Bava Batra 14b:9](https://www.sefaria.org/Bava_Batra.14b.9)). Joel is "the second of 12 books of Minor Prophets ('Trei Asar')" in the Hebrew order ([Sefaria, Joel](https://www.sefaria.org/Joel)); the Greek Septuagint places it fourth, after Hosea, Amos and Micah ([NETS, The Twelve Prophets](https://ccat.sas.upenn.edu/nets/edition/32-twelve-nets.pdf)). In the synagogue, on the Sabbath between Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur, "Ashkenazi Jews read Hosea 14:2-10 and Joel 2:15-27" ([My Jewish Learning, What Is Shabbat Shuvah?](https://www.myjewishlearning.com/article/shabbat-shuvah/)). In the New Testament, Peter's sermon at Pentecost quotes the promise of the Spirit ([Acts 2:16–21](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ACT02.htm#V16)), and Paul quotes its last line, "Whoever will call on the name of the Lord will be saved" ([Romans 10:13](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ROM10.htm#V13)). Every church reads Joel.

## Outline

The outline follows the divisions of the HarperCollins Bible Dictionary ([Bible Odyssey, Joel, The Book of (HarperCollins Bible Dictionary)](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/dictionary/joel-the-book-of/)). The WEBBE, like the Greek and Latin Bibles, divides Joel into three chapters; the Hebrew text and the New American Bible have four, so that WEBBE 2:28–32 is their 3:1–5 and WEBBE chapter 3 is their chapter 4 ([USCCB, Joel, chapter 3, with notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/joel/3); [USCCB, Joel, chapter 4, with notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/joel/4)). The Catholic Encyclopedia notes the same: "the second and third chapters of the Massoretic text forming one chapter, the second in the Septuagint and Vulgate" ([Catholic Encyclopedia (1910), Joel](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/08419a.htm)).

## How the churches read it

Where the churches, or their interpreters, read the book differently, or where it has played a part in their history. Each entry starts with its source's score.

- `7` [Church of England, Book of Common Prayer: The First Day of Lent, commonly called Ash Wednesday](https://www.churchofengland.org/liturgy/book-of-common-prayer/collects-epistles-and-gospels/ash-wednesday): **"Tear your heart": Joel at the start of Lent.** In the **Anglican** Book of Common Prayer the Epistle for Ash Wednesday is [Joel 2:12–17](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JOL02.htm#V12), "rend your heart, and not your garments", read with Jesus' teaching on fasting in secret ([Matthew 6:16–21](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MAT06.htm#V16)). The **Catholic** lectionary reads [Joel 2:12–18](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JOL02.htm#V12) as the first reading on Ash Wednesday, with [2 Corinthians 5:20–6:2](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2CO05.htm#V20) and [Matthew 6:1–6](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MAT06.htm#V1), [Matthew 6:16–18](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MAT06.htm#V16) ([USCCB, Readings for Ash Wednesday, 18 February 2026](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/readings/021826.cfm)), and the Catholic Encyclopedia notes that "The passages from 1:13 sq., and 2:17, are still used in the Liturgy of the Church during Lent" ([Catholic Encyclopedia (1910), Joel](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/08419a.htm)). The Revised Common Lectionary, used by many **Protestant** churches, reads [Joel 2:1–2](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JOL02.htm#V1) and [Joel 2:12–17](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JOL02.htm#V12) on Ash Wednesday in each of its three years ([Vanderbilt Divinity Library, Revised Common Lectionary: Sunday citations](https://lectionary.library.vanderbilt.edu/sunday-citations/)). On these verses the **Reformed** reformer John Calvin taught that "men cannot be restored to the right ways except they entertain a hope of God's mercy", and so the prophet "represents God as propitious and merciful, that he might thus kindly allure the people to repentance" ([John Calvin, Commentary on Joel 2:12–13](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/calvin/calcom27.iii.iii.v.html)).
- `7` [Catechism of the Catholic Church, 702–716](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P22.HTM): **the Spirit poured out at Pentecost.** Peter reads Pentecost as the fulfilment of Joel ([Acts 2:16–21](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ACT02.htm#V16); [Acts](../new-testament/acts.md)). The **Catholic** Catechism counts Joel's promise among "The prophetic texts that directly concern the sending of the Holy Spirit", and teaches that "St. Peter will proclaim their fulfillment on the morning of Pentecost"; elsewhere it teaches that "This fullness of the Spirit was not to remain uniquely the Messiah's, but was to be communicated to the whole messianic people", citing Joel ([Catechism of the Catholic Church, 1286–1292](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P3Q.HTM)). In the **Orthodox** Church, [Joel 2:23–32](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JOL02.htm#V23) is one of the Old Testament readings of Pentecost ([Orthodox Church in America, Scripture Readings for 31 May 2026, Pentecost](https://www.oca.org/readings/daily/2026/05/31)). Calvin wrote that "this prophecy must be referred to the advent of Christ"; to the objection that not every Christian prophesies, he answered that "the Prophet speaks comparatively": "the Spirit of God, who was given only to few under the law, was poured out upon all flesh" ([John Calvin, Commentary on Joel 2:28](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/calvin/calcom27.iii.iii.xxiii.html)).
- `7` [Wolfgang Vondey, Pentecostal Theology, 3.3: Spirit baptism](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Christianity/PentecostalTheology#section3.3) (St Andrews Encyclopaedia of Theology): **Joel and Spirit baptism.** In **Pentecostal** teaching, the moment when the disciples "were all filled with the Holy Spirit" at Pentecost "is interpreted as the fulfillment of God's promise of the Spirit (v. 16–21; Joel 2:28)", and is "extended as eschatological gift to all followers of Christ". The Pentecostal Assemblies of Canada teach that Jesus "continues to baptize in the Holy Spirit those who are believers", and that "This experience is available for everyone, male and female, of every age, status, and ethnicity", citing [Joel 2:28–29](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JOL02.htm#V28) and [Acts 2:17–18](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ACT02.htm#V17) ([Pentecostal Assemblies of Canada, Statement of Essential Truths and Positions and Practices](https://paoc.org/docs/default-source/fellowship-services-documents/constitutions/2026/2026-statement-of-essential-truths-and-positions-and-practices.pdf?sfvrsn=9e0cc06a_4)). See also [The Pentecostal and charismatic churches](../../traditions/pentecostal.md).
- `7` [Orthodox Church in America, Lives of the Saints: Prophet Joel](https://www.oca.org/saints/lives/2026/10/19/102997-prophet-joel): **the Prophet Joel in the Orthodox Church.** The **Orthodox** Church commemorates Joel on 19 October. Its life of the prophet says that he "predicted the desolation of Jerusalem" and "prophesied that the Holy Spirit would be poured out upon all people, through the Savior of the world", and that the hymnographer Anatolius, in the Praises at Matins on the Sunday after the Nativity, reads [Joel 2:30](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JOL02.htm#V30) of Christ's birth: "the blood refers to the Incarnation, the fire to the Divinity, and the pillars of smoke to the Holy Spirit".

## Open questions

Questions on which the churches or scholars disagree. These guidelines don't pick a side.

- `7` [USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Joel](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/joel/0): **when was Joel written?** The book gives no date, and "Scholars have suggested a range of dates for its composition from the ninth to the fourth centuries BCE", "making Joel possibly one of the earliest prophetic books or one of the latest" ([Enter the Bible, Introductory Issues in Joel](https://enterthebible.org/courses/joel/lessons/introductory-issues-in-joel/)). The Catholic Encyclopedia reports that "The early commentators, in agreement with Jerome, placed the era of composition in the eighth century B.C.", as a contemporary of Hosea and Amos, because Joel stands between them in the Hebrew order and the book does not name the Assyrians; others placed it in the reign of Josiah, or after the exile; it concludes that "None of the chronological hypotheses concerning Joel can claim to possess convincing proof" ([Catholic Encyclopedia (1910), Joel](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/08419a.htm)). The Orthodox Church in America's life of the prophet dates him to 800 BC ([Orthodox Church in America, Lives of the Saints: Prophet Joel](https://www.oca.org/saints/lives/2026/10/19/102997-prophet-joel)). The USCCB introduction holds that "internal evidence favors a postexilic date for its composition, probably 450–400 B.C.", pointing to "Joel's reliance on an established, possibly written, prophetic tradition; the existence of an organized temple liturgy; the dominance of priests and the absence of a king; and vocabulary characteristic of later material like Chronicles and Zechariah". The ESV Global Study Bible agrees that "Most scholars date the book after the exile to Babylon (586 B.C.)" ([ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to Joel](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-joel/)).
- `7` [USCCB, Joel, chapter 2, with notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/joel/2) (New American Bible, Revised Edition): **are the locusts insects, an army, or a picture of the end?** In chapter 1 the plague is described as locusts and drought; in chapter 2 the invaders are described as an army, "like a strong people set in battle array", that climbs "the wall like warriors" ([Joel 2:4–7](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JOL02.htm#V4)). The New American Bible reads the "northerner" of [Joel 2:20](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JOL02.htm#V20) as "the locusts, pictured as an invading army", and holds that Joel "combines the imagery of the locust invasion (chap. 1) with language from the holy war tradition in order to describe the Lord leading a heavenly army against the enemy, in this case, Jerusalem". The Catholic Encyclopedia notes that the swarm "has so frequently received a symbolical interpretation", but holds that it "is no apocalyptic picture; neither is it a description of the progress of a hostile army", since "a hostile army is used to typify an actual swarm of locusts" ([Catholic Encyclopedia (1910), Joel](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/08419a.htm)). Calvin read the plague as a punishment that had already happened, "four barren years, suddenly produced by insects", and held that those who think "that a future punishment is denounced" "mistake and pervert the real meaning of the Prophet" ([John Calvin, Commentary on Joel 1:2–4](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/calvin/calcom27.iii.ii.iv.html)). Enter the Bible holds that "The first part of Joel seems to be preoccupied with a real, historical, tragic event", and that from 2:28 the book turns to the end-time ([Enter the Bible, Introductory Issues in Joel](https://enterthebible.org/courses/joel/lessons/introductory-issues-in-joel/)).
- `7` [Free Methodist Church USA, A Biblical Understanding of Women in Church Leadership](https://fmcusa.org/wp-content/uploads/FMCUSA_A-Biblical-Understanding-of-Women-in-Church-Leadership.pdf): **"your sons and your daughters will prophesy": does this bear on who may preach and lead?** The **Free Methodist** Church, which holds "an understanding of Scripture that affirms women in ministry and their ordination", reads the gift of the Spirit at Pentecost as "a fulfillment of Joel's prophecy (Joel 2:28–29) for all believers", and notes that "in the early church from Pentecost onward women spoke in worship as the Spirit gave them ability". The Pentecostal Assemblies of Canada cite Joel for a Spirit baptism "available for everyone, male and female", and teach that "The Spirit also empowers leaders, both female and male" ([Pentecostal Assemblies of Canada, Statement of Essential Truths and Positions and Practices](https://paoc.org/docs/default-source/fellowship-services-documents/constitutions/2026/2026-statement-of-essential-truths-and-positions-and-practices.pdf?sfvrsn=9e0cc06a_4)). Calvin wrote that the prophet "does not exclude women", but also that "the Prophet speaks not here of the public office of teaching" ([John Calvin, Commentary on Joel 2:28](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/calvin/calcom27.iii.iii.xxiii.html)). The complementarian authors John Piper and Wayne Grudem accept "the freedom of women to prophesy publicly as described in Acts 2:17", but hold that "Prophecy in the early church did not correspond to the sermon today or to a formal exposition of Scripture", and that "the regular Bible teaching ministry was the responsibility of the elder-teachers. This latter role is the one Paul assigns uniquely to men" ([John Piper and Wayne Grudem, 50 Crucial Questions, Desiring God](https://document.desiringgod.org/50-crucial-questions-about-manhood-and-womanhood-en.pdf)). The churches' positions on ordination are set out in the open questions of [1 Timothy](../new-testament/1-timothy.md#open-questions).
- `6` [Enter the Bible, Theological Themes in Joel](https://enterthebible.org/courses/joel/lessons/theological-themes-in-joel/) (Luther Seminary): **was the plague a punishment, and for what?** Enter the Bible notes that "Joel, as people do in our day, sees the locusts as a punishment from God, though he never states clearly what sin might have caused it", and asks of natural disasters today: "Did God have a part in sending the disaster? How do we know that?" It suggests reading the call to repent as a call to "return" to God and to the practices of faith, "not necessarily to repent of sin". Calvin held that the plague was so unusual that "it was impossible to ascribe what they had seen with their eyes to chance or to earthly causes, but that it was a miracle" sent to rouse the people ([John Calvin, Commentary on Joel 1:2–4](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/calvin/calcom27.iii.ii.iv.html)); the USCCB introduction speaks of a people "unaware that this crisis is the work of the Lord in their midst" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Joel](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/joel/0)). Jesus, asked about eighteen people killed when a tower fell, answered: "do you think that they were worse offenders than all the men who dwell in Jerusalem? I tell you, no, but, unless you repent, you will all perish in the same way" ([Luke 13:4–5](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/LUK13.htm#V4)). Why God allows suffering is one of the open questions in [Suffering and hope](../../general/suffering.md#open-questions).
