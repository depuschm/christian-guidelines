# Esther

Esther "tells a story of the deliverance of the Jewish people". At the court of the Persian emperor Ahasuerus, "loosely based on Xerxes, 485–464 B.C.", his minister Haman pursues "a personal vendetta against the Jews by having a royal decree issued ordering their destruction". The threat is averted by "two Jews, Esther and Mordecai", and the deliverance "is commemorated by the inauguration of the Jewish festival of Purim". The introduction to the New American Bible adds that "The book confronts the modern reader with important themes, the evils of genocide and racism" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Esther](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/esther/0)). The festival takes its name from *pur*, "the lot", which Haman cast to choose the day of the massacre ([Esther 3:7](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/EST03.htm#V7), [Esther 9:24–26](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/EST09.htm#V24)).

"One striking feature of the Hebrew version of the Book of Esther is that no divine names or titles are employed here; God is not mentioned at all", although "there are indirect references to divine activity (for example, in 4:14)". The book has come down in "two versions: an older Hebrew version, and a Greek version based on a text similar to the Hebrew, but with additions and alterations", and "The Greek additions to Esther have many explicit references to God" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Esther](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/esther/0)). This page describes the Hebrew book, which every church reads. Catholic and Orthodox Bibles also include the Greek additions ([which books each church reads](../README.md#which-books-each-church-reads)), which these guidelines describe with the [deuterocanon](../README.md#deuterocanon).

The book names no author. The Babylonian Talmud says that "The members of the Great Assembly" wrote "the Scroll of Esther" ([Sefaria, Bava Batra 15a](https://www.sefaria.org/Bava_Batra.15a.2)). The Reformed commentator Matthew Henry thought that Mordecai, who "wrote these things" to the Jews ([Esther 9:20](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/EST09.htm#V20)), "was the penman of the whole book" ([Matthew Henry, Commentary on the Whole Bible, Esther: Introduction](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/henry/mhc2.Esth.i.html)); the ESV Global Study Bible calls the book "an anonymous work" and suggests that the author may have been "someone like Mordecai" ([ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to Esther](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-esther/)). The USCCB introduction holds that "The book was probably written in the third or second century B.C." ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Esther](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/esther/0)), and Christine Hayes that "it was probably written in the fourth century" ([Christine Hayes, Introduction to the Old Testament, Lecture 24, Open Yale Courses](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-24)). Whether the story is history is one of the open questions below.

In Christian Bibles, Esther stands among the historical books of the Old Testament ([Orthodox Church in America, The Orthodox Faith: Old Testament, History](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/doctrine-scripture/old-testament/history)). It is also Jewish Scripture: "one of the five megillot (scrolls), part of the section of the Hebrew Bible called Writings", and it "is read publicly in the evening and morning of Purim" ([Sefaria, Esther](https://www.sefaria.org/Esther)).

## How the churches read it

Where the churches, or their interpreters, read the book differently, or where it has played a part in their history. Each entry starts with its source's score.

- `7` [Athanasius, Letter 39](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/2806039.htm) (New Advent): **Esther in the early church's canon.** The church father Athanasius of Alexandria listed Esther not among the books of the canon but among "other books besides these not indeed included in the Canon, but appointed by the Fathers to be read by those who newly join us", with Wisdom, Sirach, Judith and Tobit. The bishop Melito of Sardis sent his "brother Onesimus" a list of "the books of the Old Testament" that has no Esther at all ([Eusebius, Church History, Book IV, 26](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/250104.htm)). The USCCB introduction notes that "the Book of Esther was questioned by some early Christians, even St. Jerome", and Luther Seminary's Enter the Bible that "Some parts of the early Eastern Church did not include Esther in the canon" and that "No part of Esther was found among the writings at Qumran", the Dead Sea Scrolls ([Enter the Bible, Introductory Issues in Esther](https://enterthebible.org/courses/esther/lessons/introductory-issues-in-esther/)). For the **Catholic Church**, the Council of Trent counted Esther among the books of the Old Testament ([Council of Trent, Session IV](https://history.hanover.edu/texts/trent/ct04.html)), "the whole book, including the Greek additions" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Esther](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/esther/0)).
- `7` [Church of England, Articles of Religion, VI](https://www.churchofengland.org/liturgy/book-of-common-prayer/articles-religion#VI): **the Greek additions.** Every church now reads the Hebrew book of Esther as Scripture. They differ over the Greek additions, which Catholic and Orthodox Bibles include. The **Church of England** counts "The Book of Esther" among the canonical books, and puts "The rest of the Book of Esther" among the books the church reads "for example of life and instruction of manners; but yet doth it not apply them to establish any doctrine". The **Reformed** Westminster Confession lists Esther in the canon and holds that the books called Apocrypha "are no part of the canon of the Scripture" ([Westminster Confession of Faith, 1.2–1.3](https://www.opc.org/wcf.html)), and Matthew Henry notes that the "apocryphal addition" is "not in the Hebrew, nor was ever received by the Jews into the canon" ([Matthew Henry, Commentary on the Whole Bible, Esther: Introduction](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/henry/mhc2.Esth.i.html)).
- `7` [Catechism of the Catholic Church, 54–64](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__PG.HTM): **Esther and Mary.** The **Catholic** Catechism names Esther among the "holy women" who "kept alive the hope of Israel's salvation", and adds: "the purest figure among them is Mary" (paragraph 64). At Mass on Thursday of the first week of Lent, the Catholic Church reads Esther's prayer from the Greek additions, "Help me, who am alone and have no help but you", with the words of Jesus "Ask, and it will be given you" ([USCCB, Readings for Thursday of the First Week of Lent, 26 February 2026](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/readings/022626.cfm); [Matthew 7:7–12](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MAT07.htm#V7)).
- `7` [Orthodox Church in America, Sunday before the Nativity](https://www.oca.org/saints/lives/2026/12/20/81-sunday-before-the-nativity): on the Sunday before Christmas, the **Orthodox** Church commemorates the righteous of the Old Testament, among them "the righteous Esther, who delivered Israel from death".
- `6` [Matthew Henry, Commentary on the Whole Bible, Esther: Introduction](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/henry/mhc2.Esth.i.html) (Christian Classics Ethereal Library): **God's hidden providence.** Many Protestant readers find God at work in the book's coincidences. The **Reformed** commentator Matthew Henry writes that "though the name of God be not in it, the finger of God is, directing many minute events for the bringing about of his people's deliverance". The evangelical ESV Global Study Bible holds that the book "shows clearly that, even when God is most hidden, he is still working to protect his chosen people", and that "Christians are not obliged to observe the Feast of Purim, but they are to take to heart the truth that God providentially watches over his own" ([ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to Esther](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-esther/)).
- `6` [Martin Luther, Table Talk: Of God's Word](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/luther/tabletalk.v.i.html) (Christian Classics Ethereal Library): **Luther's criticism.** In the *Table Talk*, informal conversations with his students and colleagues that they wrote down, the reformer Martin Luther is recorded as saying: "I am so great an enemy to the second book of the Maccabees, and to Esther, that I wish they had not come to us at all, for they have too many heathen unnaturalities." This is one reformer's view in conversation; Lutheran churches read Esther among the 39 books of the Old Testament ([which books each church reads](../README.md#which-books-each-church-reads)).

## Open questions

Questions on which the churches or scholars disagree. These guidelines don't pick a side.

- `7` [Martien A. Halvorson-Taylor, Esther](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/esther/) (Bible Odyssey): **is Esther history, or a story?** Halvorson-Taylor writes that "Mainstream biblical scholars do not regard Esther as an historical figure, because of a number of historical improbabilities in her book and the lack of external evidence corroborating her existence", and that they judge her book "to be a Jewish novel in miniature". The USCCB introduction calls the book "a free composition, not a historical document", pointing to its literary motifs, exaggerations and ironies ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Esther](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/esther/0)), and Christine Hayes calls it "a short novella" ([Christine Hayes, Introduction to the Old Testament, Lecture 24, Open Yale Courses](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-24)). The ESV Global Study Bible, by contrast, reads it as "an entertaining historical story", set "during the reign of King Ahasuerus, better known by his Greek name, Xerxes I (486–464 B.C.)" ([ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to Esther](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-esther/)).
- `7` [Elsie R. Stern, Where Is God in Esther?](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/where-is-god-in-esther/) (Bible Odyssey): **why does the Hebrew book never name God?** Stern notes that Esther is "one of only two books in the Hebrew Bible that never mention God (the other is the Song of Songs)", and that "For traditional Jewish commentators, the absence of God's name did not mean that God was absent from the book". The Talmud finds Esther hinted at in God's words "I will surely hide my face" ([Sefaria, Chullin 139b](https://www.sefaria.org/Chullin.139b.12); [Deuteronomy 31:17–18](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/DEU31.htm#V17)), and Enter the Bible notes that "rabbinical literature considers 'another quarter' to be a reference to God" ([Enter the Bible, Theological Themes in Esther](https://enterthebible.org/courses/esther/lessons/theological-themes-in-esther/); [Esther 4:14](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/EST04.htm#V14)); Christian readings of hidden providence are set out above. Stern herself suggests another reading: the authors "might have been asserting that God is absent in the story and that the rescue of the Jews was the result of precarious coincidence, not divine providence".
- `6` [Enter the Bible, Theological Themes in Esther](https://enterthebible.org/courses/esther/lessons/theological-themes-in-esther/) (Luther Seminary): **how should the fighting of chapter 9 be read?** Diane Jacobson and Kathryn Schifferdecker call "the excessive retributive violence apparent in the end" perhaps "the most troubling aspect" of the book. "One can read this theme as part of the exaggeration of the book, never intended to be taken literally, a theme that provides hope to an often-oppressed people in the language of satire and fantasy. Or one can note that when the reader comes to celebrate the very violence feared from the enemy, then the finger is ironically pointed back at the reader." Matthew Henry read the chapter as lawful self-defence under the king's second decree: the Jews "stood upon their defence, offering violence to none, but bidding defiance to all", and "being so supported, they strove lawfully" ([Matthew Henry, Commentary on the Whole Bible, Esther 9](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/henry/mhc2.Esth.x.html)). Enter the Bible also notes that the Jews' refusal to take plunder recalls "Saul's fatal error of taking plunder from Agag", the forebear of Haman the Agagite ([Enter the Bible, Introductory Issues in Esther](https://enterthebible.org/courses/esther/lessons/introductory-issues-in-esther/); [1 Samuel 15:1–35](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1SA15.htm#V1), [Esther 9:10](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/EST09.htm#V10)).

## Tutorials and further reading

- `7` [Christine Hayes, Alternative Visions: Esther, Ruth, and Jonah](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-24) (Open Yale Courses, Introduction to the Old Testament, lecture 24): a university lecture, with a transcript, whose first chapter reads Esther as a story in which the Jews "are saved not by divine intervention but entirely through their own efforts".

- `6` [Leonard Greenspoon, Humor in the Book of Esther](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/humor-in-the-book-of-esther/) (Bible Odyssey): the reversals and ironies of the story, and why "everything Haman plans against his enemies ultimately befalls him".
- `6` [BibleProject, Esther](https://bibleproject.com/videos/esther/): an animated overview of the book, read as the story of a God who is "at work behind the scenes".
