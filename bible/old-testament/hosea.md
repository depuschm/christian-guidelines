# Hosea

The Book of Hosea collects the oracles of a prophet of the eighth century BC. "Hosea, a prophet from the Northern Kingdom, preached in his homeland, which he addresses as Israel, Jacob or, frequently, Ephraim", from the last prosperous years of Jeroboam II through the assassinations of several kings and the advance of Assyria; his "long ministry (ca. 750–725) seems to have ended before the capture of Samaria in 722/721". "The only information the text provides us about the life of Hosea concerns his marriage", and the book presents it as "a symbol of the covenant between the Lord and Israel": "first love, separation, reunion". Hosea charges Israel with breaking the covenant by trusting in its own military strength, by making treaties with Assyria and Egypt, and "by running after the Baals, the gods of fertility"; Assyria will destroy the kingdom, "but God's love will have the last word" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Hosea](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/hosea/0)). Christine Hayes divides the book into two sections, the marriage and its meaning in chapters 1–3 and the oracles of chapters 4–14, and notes that it "is considered by many to be the most difficult of the prophetic books. The Hebrew is very difficult" ([Christine Hayes, Introduction to the Old Testament, Lecture 17, Open Yale Courses](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-17)). The Greek translation of the Septuagint, the Old Testament of the Orthodox churches, "is quite different from the MT in many verses" in Hosea ([NETS, The Twelve Prophets, translated by George E. Howard](https://ccat.sas.upenn.edu/nets/edition/32-twelve-nets.pdf)).

The book opens with "The LORD's word that came to Hosea the son of Beeri, in the days of Uzziah, Jotham, Ahaz, and Hezekiah, kings of Judah, and in the days of Jeroboam the son of Joash, king of Israel" ([Hosea 1:1](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/HOS01.htm#V1)). The New American Bible holds that this heading "is from a Judean editor, who lists the kings of Judah in the south first, even though Hosea preached in the Northern Kingdom" ([USCCB, Hosea, chapter 1, with notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/hosea/1)), and the ESV Global Study Bible dates Hosea's preaching to "c. 753–722 B.C." ([ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to Hosea](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-hosea/)). The Talmud counts Hosea the first of "four prophets who prophesied in that period", "Hosea and Isaiah, Amos and Micah" ([Sefaria, Bava Batra 14b:8](https://www.sefaria.org/Bava_Batra.14b.8)); Hayes pairs him with [Amos](amos.md) as the prophets of the northern kingdom in the Assyrian crisis. How the book was edited is one of the open questions below.

Hosea is the first of the twelve shorter prophetic books, which Jews and Christians read in the same order from Hosea to Malachi. In Jewish tradition the twelve form one book: the Talmud explains that Hosea's "prophecy is written together with those of Haggai, Zechariah, and Malachi in one book of the Twelve Prophets", because "Were it written separately, since it is small it would be lost" ([Sefaria, Bava Batra 14b:9](https://www.sefaria.org/Bava_Batra.14b.9)), and it holds that "The members of the Great Assembly wrote" the Twelve Prophets ([Sefaria, Bava Batra 15a:2](https://www.sefaria.org/Bava_Batra.15a.2)). Sirach, in the [deuterocanon](../README.md#deuterocanon), already praises "the twelve prophets" together ([Sirach 49:10](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/SIR49.htm#V10)). Sefaria describes Hosea (*Hoshea*) as "the first of 12 books of Minor Prophets ('Trei Asar'), marked by their shortness" ([Sefaria, Hosea](https://www.sefaria.org/Hosea)), and the Septuagint, which orders the first six books differently, also puts it first: Hosea, Amos, Micah, Joel, Obadiah and Jonah ([NETS, The Twelve Prophets](https://ccat.sas.upenn.edu/nets/edition/32-twelve-nets.pdf)). In the synagogue, the book's last chapter is read on the Sabbath between Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur, Shabbat Shuvah, "the Sabbath of return": "Ashkenazi Jews read Hosea 14:2-10 and Joel 2:15-27, while Sephardic Jews read Hosea 14:2-10 and Micah 7:18-20" ([My Jewish Learning, What Is Shabbat Shuvah?](https://www.myjewishlearning.com/article/shabbat-shuvah/)), in the WEBBE [Hosea 14:1–9](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/HOS14.htm#V1). Every church reads Hosea, and the New Testament quotes it often: Jesus twice ([Matthew 9:13](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MAT09.htm#V13), [Matthew 12:7](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MAT12.htm#V7)), and Matthew, Paul and Peter ([Matthew 2:15](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MAT02.htm#V15), [Romans 9:25–26](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ROM09.htm#V25), [1 Corinthians 15:54–55](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1CO15.htm#V54), [1 Peter 2:10](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1PE02.htm#V10)).

## How the churches read it

Where the churches, or their interpreters, read the book differently, or where it has played a part in their history. Each entry starts with its source's score.

- `8` [Pope Francis, Misericordiae Vultus, 20–21](https://www.vatican.va/content/francesco/en/bulls/documents/papa-francesco_bolla_20150411_misericordiae-vultus.html) (2015): **"I desire mercy, and not sacrifice."** Proclaiming a jubilee of mercy, the pope wrote of Jesus' appeal to [Hosea 6:6](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/HOS06.htm#V6) that "Jesus affirms that, from that time onward, the rule of life for his disciples must place mercy at the centre", and that "The experience of the prophet Hosea can help us see the way in which mercy surpasses justice", quoting "How can I give you up, O Ephraim!" ([Hosea 11:8–9](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/HOS11.htm#V8)). The **Catholic** Catechism, teaching on true sacrifice, notes that "Jesus recalls the words of the prophet Hosea: 'I desire mercy, and not sacrifice'" ([Catechism of the Catholic Church, 2095–2109](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P7D.HTM)). The **Reformed** reformer John Calvin called the verse "a remarkable passage; the Son of God has twice quoted it", and read Jesus' use of it to mean "that God is not worshipped by external ceremonies, but when men forgive and bear with one another" ([John Calvin, Commentary on Hosea 6:6–7](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/calvin/calcom26.xiii.ix.html)).
- `7` [Catechism of the Catholic Church, 512–560](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P1L.HTM): **"Out of Egypt I called my son."** Matthew writes that the holy family stayed in Egypt "that it might be fulfilled which was spoken by the Lord through the prophet, saying, 'Out of Egypt I called my son'" ([Matthew 2:15](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MAT02.htm#V15), [Hosea 11:1](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/HOS11.htm#V1)). The **Catholic** Catechism, citing both verses, teaches that "Jesus' departure from Egypt recalls the exodus and presents him as the definitive liberator of God's people"; the New American Bible notes that "Hosea dates the real beginning of Israel from the time of the exodus. Mt 2:15 applies this text to the return of Jesus from Egypt" ([USCCB, Hosea, chapter 11, with notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/hosea/11)). Calvin, for the **Reformed** tradition, rejected the view that Matthew made "only a comparison" as "too frigid": "Christ cannot be separated from his Church", and "It was then the full nativity of the Church, when Christ came forth from Egypt to redeem his Church"; "Matthew therefore most fitly accommodates this passage to Christ" ([John Calvin, Commentary on Hosea 11:1](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/calvin/calcom26.xviii.i.html)).
- `7` [Orthodox Church in America, Lives of all saints commemorated on October 17 (Prophet Hosea)](https://www.oca.org/saints/all-lives/2026/10/17): **Hosea's prophecies of Christ.** The **Orthodox** Church commemorates "The Holy Prophet Hosea the first of the minor prophets" on 17 October. Its life of the prophet holds that he "foretold the end of sacrificial offerings and of the priesthood of Aaron" ([Hosea 3:4–5](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/HOS03.htm#V4)), and that "Hosea spoke also about Christ, how He would return from out of Egypt", "that He would be resurrected on the third day" ([Hosea 6:2](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/HOS06.htm#V2), [1 Corinthians 15:4](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1CO15.htm#V4)), "and that He would conquer death" ([Hosea 13:14](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/HOS13.htm#V14), [1 Corinthians 15:54–55](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1CO15.htm#V54)).
- `7` [Catechism of the Catholic Church, 199–231](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P16.HTM): **God the husband, and Christ and the church.** The **Catholic** Catechism, citing Hosea 2 and 11, teaches that "God's love for Israel is compared to a father's love for his son", that "God loves his people more than a bridegroom his beloved", and that "his love will be victorious over even the worst infidelities". The New American Bible notes that "Hosea began the Old Testament tradition of describing the relation between the Lord and Israel in terms of marriage", and that "The New Testament uses the marriage imagery to describe the union between Christ and the Church" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Hosea](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/hosea/0); [Mark 2:19–20](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MRK02.htm#V19), [Ephesians 5:25–32](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/EPH05.htm#V25); see [Ephesians](../new-testament/ephesians.md)). Irenaeus of Lyons, in the second century, held that Hosea's marriage foretold how "from men of this stamp it will be God's good pleasure to take out a Church which shall be sanctified by fellowship with His Son, just as that woman was sanctified by intercourse with the prophet" ([Irenaeus, Against Heresies, IV.20.12](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0103420.htm)). The **Reformed** commentator Matthew Henry read the betrothal of 2:19–20 the same way: "Believing souls are espoused to Christ", and "The gospel-church is the bride, the Lamb's wife" ([Matthew Henry, Commentary on Hosea 2](https://www.ccel.org/h/henry/mhc2/MHC28002.HTM); [2 Corinthians 11:2](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2CO11.htm#V2)).
- `6` [USCCB, Readings for the Solemnity of the Most Sacred Heart of Jesus, 7 June 2024](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/readings/060724.cfm): **Hosea in the lectionaries.** In the **Catholic** Church, Hosea's words on God's love for his child ([Hosea 11:1–9](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/HOS11.htm#V1)) were the first reading on the feast of the Sacred Heart of Jesus in 2024. In the Revised Common Lectionary, used by many **Protestant** churches, [Hosea 1:2–10](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/HOS01.htm#V2), [Hosea 2:14–20](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/HOS02.htm#V14), [Hosea 5:15–6:6](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/HOS05.htm#V15) and [Hosea 11:1–11](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/HOS11.htm#V1) are Sunday readings ([Vanderbilt Divinity Library, Revised Common Lectionary: Sunday citations](https://lectionary.library.vanderbilt.edu/sunday-citations/)).

## Open questions

Questions on which the churches or scholars disagree. These guidelines don't pick a side.

- `7` [USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Hosea](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/hosea/0): **was Hosea's marriage a real event?** The New American Bible holds that "Even if we cannot reconstruct what happened exactly, the text as it now stands speaks of three moments in the relationship", and notes that "a woman of prostitution" "does not necessarily mean that Gomer was a prostitute when Hosea married her"; "Prostitution here may refer to Gomer's participation in the worship of other gods" ([USCCB, Hosea, chapter 1, with notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/hosea/1)). Many read the marriage as a real event. Irenaeus wrote that the prophets foretold the future "also in actual works", and "For this reason did Hosea the prophet take a wife of whoredoms, prophesying by means of the action" ([Irenaeus, Against Heresies, IV.20.12](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0103420.htm)); Thomas Aquinas, discussing whether God can change the natural law, answered that "intercourse with any woman, by the command of God, is neither adultery nor fornication" ([Thomas Aquinas, Summa Theologiae, I-II, Question 94, Article 5](https://www.newadvent.org/summa/2094.htm#article5)); and the Talmud tells the story of the marriage, in which Hosea, told to send his wife away, answers "I have sons from her and I am unable to dismiss her or to divorce her", and God answers that "so too, I am still attached to the Jewish people" ([Sefaria, Pesachim 87a:13](https://www.sefaria.org/Pesachim.87a.13), [Sefaria, Pesachim 87b:4–5](https://www.sefaria.org/Pesachim.87b.4)). Others read it as a vision or a parable. Calvin judged that "Their opinion, therefore, is not probable, who think that the Prophet had taken such a wife as is here described", reported that "almost all the Hebrews agree in this opinion, that the Prophet did not actually marry a wife, but that he was bidden to do this in a vision", and held it "probable" that Hosea simply proclaimed it as a representation, as if God set him "on a stage" ([John Calvin, Commentary on Hosea 1:2](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/calvin/calcom26.viii.iii.html)); Matthew Henry, who allowed that the marriage might have been real, noted that "most commentators think that it was done in vision, or that it is no more than a parable" ([Matthew Henry, Commentary on Hosea 1](https://www.ccel.org/h/henry/mhc2/MHC28001.HTM)).
- `7` [Christine Hayes, Introduction to the Old Testament, Lecture 17, Open Yale Courses](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-17): **how should readers take the marriage metaphor and the threats against the wife?** Chapter 2 threatens the unfaithful wife: "lest I strip her naked, and make her bare as in the day that she was born", and "Now I will uncover her lewdness in the sight of her lovers" ([Hosea 2:3](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/HOS02.htm#V3), [Hosea 2:10](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/HOS02.htm#V10)). Hayes calls it "the almost schizophrenic chapter 2", with "this sustained violent, very violent account of the faithless wife" and yet "a very gentle, very loving portrait of reconciliation", and reads its alternation as "the struggle of a lover who's torn between his jealous wrath and his undying love". Stuart Macwilliam notes that the way the prophets use the metaphor has "caused unease for some readers and outright condemnation from others": "Some scholars attempt to rehabilitate the text, pointing out, for instance, the scattered references to reconciliation. However, feminist scholars have led the way in exposing the damage caused by a metaphor that associates man with God and woman with lust and betrayal" ([Stuart Macwilliam, Marriage Metaphors in the Prophets, Bible Odyssey](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/marriage-metaphors-in-the-prophets/)). Deborah Rooke argues that "The metaphor reflects the male-female power dynamics that would have existed in a marriage" ([Deborah Rooke, The Woman-Adulterer Motif, Bible Odyssey](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/the-woman-adulterer-motif/)). The New American Bible explains the stripping by the law of its time, "it was the husband's responsibility to provide food and clothing for his wife", so that "because of her adultery, he takes back his support" ([USCCB, Hosea, chapter 2, with notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/hosea/2)), and the Catholic Catechism reads the chapters as showing that God's "love will be victorious over even the worst infidelities" ([Catechism of the Catholic Church, 199–231](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P16.HTM)).
- `7` [USCCB, Hosea, chapter 1, with notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/hosea/1): **how much of the book is Hosea's own?** The New American Bible holds that the heading "is from a Judean editor", and that the promise to save Judah in [Hosea 1:7](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/HOS01.htm#V7) was "Probably written by a later editor when the prophecies of Hosea circulated in the south, after the dissolution of the Northern Kingdom had occurred"; Hayes notes that "Some see that verse as perhaps a later interpolation into Hosea; it has such a positive assessment of the southern kingdom" ([Christine Hayes, Introduction to the Old Testament, Lecture 17, Open Yale Courses](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-17)). The ESV Global Study Bible presents the book as Hosea's preaching to Israel "during the latter half of the eighth century B.C." ([ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to Hosea](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-hosea/)), and the Talmud holds that the Twelve Prophets were written down by the members of the Great Assembly ([Sefaria, Bava Batra 15a:2](https://www.sefaria.org/Bava_Batra.15a.2)). The text itself is hard: the New American Bible notes that the Hebrew "is challenging to translate, possibly because the text is corrupt or because it is written in a nonstandard dialect of Hebrew", so that "English translations of the book sometimes offer very different readings of the same passage" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Hosea](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/hosea/0)).
