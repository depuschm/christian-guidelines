# Nahum

"A revelation about Nineveh. The book of the vision of Nahum the Elkoshite" ([Nahum 1:1](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/NAM01.htm#V1)). The book is a set of poems on the fall of Nineveh, the capital of the Assyrian empire, which fell to an alliance of Medes and Babylonians in 612 BC. The New American Bible introduces it: "Shortly before the fall of Nineveh, the capital of Assyria, in 612 B.C., Nahum uttered his prophecy against the hated city", and explains the prophet's "exultant outburst of joy over the impending destruction" by "the savage cruelty of Assyria, which had made it the scourge of the ancient Near East for almost three centuries" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Nahum](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/nahum/0)). Christine Hayes describes it as "really a series of three poems", "and the first one is an acrostic poem, an alphabetical poem" ([Christine Hayes, Introduction to the Old Testament, Lecture 18, Open Yale Courses](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-18); [Nahum 1:2–8](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/NAM01.htm#V2)). Unlike most prophets, Nahum does not call his own people to account: "Unlike most other prophets, Nahum (whose name means 'comfort') does not rebuke or call for reform, but instead praises God and celebrates the downfall of the powerful and oppressive Assyrian empire" ([Sefaria, Nahum](https://www.sefaria.org/Nahum)).

Nothing is known of the prophet beyond the book's title. Matthew Henry wrote that "The name of this prophet signifies a comforter" ([Matthew Henry, Commentary on the Whole Bible, Nahum: Introduction](https://www.ccel.org/h/henry/mhc2/MHC34000.HTM)), and the Orthodox life of the prophet says that his name "means 'God consoles'" ([Orthodox Church in America, Prophet Nahum](https://www.oca.org/saints/lives/2026/12/01/103452-prophet-nahum)). Where Elkosh was is unknown. The book looks back on the fall of No-Amon, the Egyptian city of Thebes ([Nahum 3:8–10](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/NAM03.htm#V8)), so the ESV Global Study Bible reasons: "The Assyrian king Ashurbanipal conquered Thebes around 664 B.C.", Nineveh "fell in 612 B.C.", and "The book was composed, therefore, between 664 and 612 B.C." ([ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to Nahum](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-nahum/)). The Orthodox Church in America dates Nahum, Habakkuk and Zephaniah "in the later part of the seventh century before Christ" ([Orthodox Church in America, The Orthodox Faith: Old Testament, Prophets](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/doctrine-scripture/old-testament/prophets)). Who Nahum was, where Elkosh lay and when he spoke are open questions below.

Nahum is the seventh of the twelve minor prophets ([Bible Odyssey, Nahum (HarperCollins Bible Dictionary)](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/dictionary/nahum/)). In the Hebrew Bible the twelve form "one book of the Twelve Prophets" ([Sefaria, Bava Batra 14b:9](https://www.sefaria.org/Bava_Batra.14b.9)), and "A nearly complete version of the Book of Nahum can be pieced together from manuscripts among the Dead Sea Scrolls", which also include a commentary on it, the Nahum Pesher ([Enter the Bible, Bible in the World: Nahum](https://enterthebible.org/courses/nahum/lessons/bible-in-the-world-nahum/)). The HarperCollins Bible Dictionary notes that Nahum is "so closely connected with the OT book of Habakkuk that the two are sometimes read as a single literary unit" ([Bible Odyssey, Nahum (HarperCollins Bible Dictionary)](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/dictionary/nahum/); [Habakkuk](habakkuk.md)). It is also read beside Jonah: the ESV calls it "a sequel to, and a dramatic contrast with, the book of Jonah", in which Nineveh repented and was spared ([ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to Nahum](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-nahum/); [Jonah 3:10](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JON03.htm#V10); [Jonah](jonah.md)). Both books end with a question about Nineveh ([Jonah 4:11](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JON04.htm#V11), [Nahum 3:19](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/NAM03.htm#V19)).

## How the churches read it

Where the churches, or their interpreters, read the book differently, or where it has played a part in their history. Each entry starts with its source's score.

- `7` [Augustine, City of God, Book XVIII, 31](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/120118.htm) (New Advent): **the messenger of peace and the gospel.** Augustine, a teacher of the **Catholic** and the whole **Western** church, reads the end of Nahum's first chapter of Christ ([Nahum 1:14–15](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/NAM01.htm#V14)): "Let him that remembers the gospel call to mind who has ascended from hell and breathed the Holy Spirit in the face of Judah, that is, of the Jewish disciples". He sees the promise to cut off "the graven and the molten things" fulfilled in "the idols of the false gods, exterminated through the gospel".
- `7` [Orthodox Church in America, Prophet Nahum](https://www.oca.org/saints/lives/2026/12/01/103452-prophet-nahum): **the Orthodox commemoration.** The **Orthodox** Church commemorates "The Holy Prophet Nahum" on 1 December. Its life holds that he "was from the village of Elkosh (Galilee)" and "lived during the seventh century B.C.", and notes that he "differs from most of the prophets in as much as he does not issue any call to repentance, nor does he denounce Israel for infidelity to God". It adds that "The Prophet Nahum and Saint Nahum of Ochrid (December 23) are invoked for people with mental disorders".
- `6` [John Calvin, Commentary on Nahum, Preface](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/calvin/calcom28.v.i.html) (Christian Classics Ethereal Library): **comfort for God's people.** The **Reformed** reformer held that "The time in which Nahum prophesied cannot with certainty be known", and that its "sum" is that the prophet "exhorted the Israelites to patience", "because God had not wholly forsaken them, but would undertake their cause, for they were under his protection". On Jonah he wrote that the Ninevites repented and were spared, but "They afterwards returned to their old ways, as it is usually the case. Hence it was, that God became less disposed to spare them."
- `6` [Matthew Henry, Commentary on the Whole Bible, Nahum 1](https://www.ccel.org/h/henry/mhc2/MHC34001.HTM) (Christian Classics Ethereal Library): **Christ's messengers of peace.** The **Presbyterian** commentator reads the messenger of 1:15 first of the defeat of the Assyrian king Sennacherib, then of the gospel: "These words are also quoted by the apostle, both from Isaiah and Nahum, and applied to the great redemption wrought out for us by our Lord Jesus", and "Christ's ministers are those messengers of good tidings, that preach peace by Jesus Christ" ([Nahum 1:15](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/NAM01.htm#V15), [Romans 10:15](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ROM10.htm#V15)).
- `6` [Enter the Bible, Bible in the World: Nahum](https://enterthebible.org/courses/nahum/lessons/bible-in-the-world-nahum/) (Luther Seminary): **Luther and the medieval readings.** Enter the Bible reports that **Luther** "understood Jonah as preceding Nahum, and the city as repenting but returning to their old ways", writing in the preface to his lectures on Nahum: "but such are the hearts of men that when the punishment ceased, so did the repentance". Luther read the book as an encouragement to trust God, for "the Lord stands by those who are His, shields His own against all attacks of the enemy, be they ever so powerful". It also notes that commentaries on Nahum were written by Cyril of Alexandria, Theodore of Mopsuestia, Theodoret of Cyrus and the Venerable Bede, and that the medieval monk Haimo of Auxerre read the call to keep the feasts of the Church, set free "by the Lord's passion", and Nineveh as a symbol of "the world".
- `6` [USCCB, Readings for Friday of the Eighteenth Week in Ordinary Time (7 August 2026)](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/readings/080726.cfm): **in the lectionaries.** The **Catholic** weekday lectionary reads Nahum once, in its second year: the messenger of peace and the woe on "the bloody city" (Nahum 2:1, 3; 3:1–3, 6–7 in the New American Bible's numbering), with Jesus' call to take up the cross ([Matthew 16:24–28](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MAT16.htm#V24)). The Revised Common Lectionary, used by many **Protestant** churches, has no reading from Nahum: the book is missing from its list of Sunday readings, which includes Micah, Habakkuk and Zephaniah ([Vanderbilt Divinity Library, Revised Common Lectionary: Sunday citations](https://lectionary.library.vanderbilt.edu/sunday-citations/)).

## Open questions

Questions on which the churches or scholars disagree. These guidelines don't pick a side.

- `7` [USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Nahum](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/nahum/0): **how should Christians read a book that rejoices over the destruction of an enemy city?** The New American Bible holds that "Nahum is not a prophet of unrestrained revenge. He asserts God's moral government of the world. Nineveh's doom is evidence that God stands against oppression and the abuse of power", and that its fall was greeted by "the small, oppressed countries as a time of deliverance". Hayes explains the book's joy by the Assyrians' "exceptional brutality" and "inhumanity", so that "for Nahum, the fall of Nineveh is God's vengeance upon Assyria for her barbaric inhumanity" ([Christine Hayes, Introduction to the Old Testament, Lecture 18, Open Yale Courses](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-18)). Enter the Bible calls the judgement "an ethical problem for many modern readers": the book "has been called 'ethically and theologically deficient' and even the work of a false prophet", yet it argues that "it is not judgment for wrath's sake—it is judgment for justice's sake", and asks readers to imagine being the victims of "extreme violence" and "being told that there will be no recompense for your oppressor" ([Enter the Bible, Introductory Issues in Nahum](https://enterthebible.org/courses/nahum/lessons/introductory-issues-in-nahum/)). Matthew Henry reads the fall as just punishment of "a city of blood, in which a great deal of innocent blood is shed by unrighteous war" ([Matthew Henry, Commentary on the Whole Bible, Nahum 3](https://www.ccel.org/h/henry/mhc2/MHC34003.HTM); [Nahum 3:1](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/NAM03.htm#V1)).
- `7` [Christine Hayes, Introduction to the Old Testament, Lecture 18, Open Yale Courses](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-18): **what of the promise that Judah would never again be invaded?** Nahum promises that "the wicked one will no more pass through you" ([Nahum 1:15](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/NAM01.htm#V15)), but Judah was soon subject to Egypt and Babylon. Hayes calls this "a glaring error", and notes that "it wasn't updated, it wasn't repaired in order to protect his prophetic reputation". The New American Bible's note says that "prophets are not always absolutely accurate in the things they foresee", that "Often the prophet speaks in hyperbole", and that such hopes were fulfilled "in the coming of Jesus, and in a way far different than the prophet expected" ([USCCB, Nahum, chapter 2](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/nahum/2)). Enter the Bible observes that the book "with this direct, unconditional promise, has been retained in the canon by both Jewish and Christian communities", and that "we wait in hope for God's kingdom to come" ([Enter the Bible, Theological Themes in Nahum](https://enterthebible.org/courses/nahum/lessons/theological-themes-in-nahum/)). Matthew Henry reads the verse of the defeat of Sennacherib's army and of the gospel ([Matthew Henry, Commentary on the Whole Bible, Nahum 1](https://www.ccel.org/h/henry/mhc2/MHC34001.HTM)).
- `6` [Enter the Bible, Introductory Issues in Nahum](https://enterthebible.org/courses/nahum/lessons/introductory-issues-in-nahum/): **how do Nahum and Jonah fit together?** In Jonah, Nineveh repents and is spared; in Nahum "there is no mention of repentance" and "the judgment is final" ([Jonah 3:10](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JON03.htm#V10), [Nahum 3:19](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/NAM03.htm#V19); [Jonah](jonah.md)). Enter the Bible sets out two readings. If Jonah came first, the message is "a warning against lapsing after one has been forgiven", a reading which "Many interpreters—ancient, medieval, and modern—have aligned the books in this way". If Nahum came first, "even with the vilest offenders, God has options that humans are inclined to resist", and Jonah "functions as a counter-testimony to the relishing of God's judgment". The Aramaic Targum, Josephus, Luther and Calvin all took the first view ([Enter the Bible, Bible in the World: Nahum](https://enterthebible.org/courses/nahum/lessons/bible-in-the-world-nahum/); [John Calvin, Commentary on Nahum, Preface](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/calvin/calcom28.v.i.html)), and so does the ESV Global Study Bible, which holds that Nineveh's repentance "did not last beyond 745 B.C." ([ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to Nahum](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-nahum/)).
- `6` [Julia M. O'Brien, Nineveh, Bible Odyssey](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/nineveh/): **how should readers take the picture of Nineveh as a shamed woman?** O'Brien notes that "The exclusive focus of the book of Nahum is the destruction of Nineveh, depicted as a woman being violated" ([Nahum 3:4–7](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/NAM03.htm#V4)). The New American Bible reads the "mistress" of 2:8 as "either the queen of Nineveh with the ladies of her court, or the city of Nineveh itself, pictured as a noblewoman" ([USCCB, Nahum, chapter 2](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/nahum/2); [Nahum 2:7](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/NAM02.htm#V7)). Matthew Henry reads the image of the harlot as Nineveh's sins and its policy of deceiving the nations ([Matthew Henry, Commentary on the Whole Bible, Nahum 3](https://www.ccel.org/h/henry/mhc2/MHC34003.HTM)). Enter the Bible gives a feminist reading: the picture of a city as a woman "was common" in the ancient Near East, but "Little to no regard is shown for the experience of the one whose body is violated", and "Any text that presents violence against a female body—whether real or metaphorical, individual or collective—as a form of justice requires careful interrogation" ([Enter the Bible, Bible in the World: Nahum](https://enterthebible.org/courses/nahum/lessons/bible-in-the-world-nahum/)).
- `6` [Catholic Encyclopedia, Nahum (1911)](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/10670a.htm) (New Advent): **where was Elkosh, and when did Nahum speak?** The encyclopedia reports four places proposed for Elkosh: Alqush, north of Mosul in Assyria, where a tomb of Nahum is shown, though the tradition "cannot be traced back beyond the sixteenth century"; Capernaum, "the village of Nahum", in Galilee; another place in Galilee, where "St. Jerome located the birthplace of Nahum"; and a place near Beth-Gabre in Judah, which "has been adopted in the Roman Martyrology" and which it says "finds more and more acceptance with modern scholars". The Orthodox life places Elkosh in Galilee ([Orthodox Church in America, Prophet Nahum](https://www.oca.org/saints/lives/2026/12/01/103452-prophet-nahum)). On the date, the encyclopedia sets the limits between the fall of Thebes in "664-663 B.C." and the fall of Nineveh, and reports that "Modern critics appear more and more inclined" to date the prophecy just before the attack on the city. Matthew Henry thought it "most probable that he lived in the time of Hezekiah", decades earlier ([Matthew Henry, Commentary on the Whole Bible, Nahum: Introduction](https://www.ccel.org/h/henry/mhc2/MHC34000.HTM)), and Calvin that "nothing certain" can be known ([John Calvin, Commentary on Nahum, Preface](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/calvin/calcom28.v.i.html)).
