# Zephaniah

Zephaniah is a short book of three chapters on "the day of the LORD". The New American Bible introduces it as a "prophecy of judgment on Judah and Jerusalem" that "emphasizes, perhaps more than any other prophecy, the devastation and death that divine judgment will bring", and names the sins behind it as "Judah's worship of other deities (1:4–9) and its unjust and abusive leadership (3:1–4)" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Zephaniah](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/zephaniah/0); [Zephaniah 1:4–6](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ZEP01.htm#V4), [Zephaniah 3:3–4](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ZEP03.htm#V3)). After the judgement on Judah and on the nations around it, the book turns to a humble remnant and ends in joy: "Sing, daughter of Zion! Shout, Israel!" ([Zephaniah 3:14](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ZEP03.htm#V14)). The ESV Global Study Bible sums up its theme: the coming day "is one of judgment against those who sin against God, and one of blessing for those who follow him" ([ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to Zephaniah](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-zephaniah/)).

The heading names the prophet as "Zephaniah, the son of Cushi, the son of Gedaliah, the son of Amariah, the son of Hezekiah, in the days of Josiah, the son of Amon, king of Judah" ([Zephaniah 1:1](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ZEP01.htm#V1)). Josiah reigned from 640 to 609 BC, and the New American Bible holds that the book's attack on false worship "may indicate that Zephaniah spoke during the height of Assyrian influence in the early years of Josiah's reign, before Josiah launched the religious reforms praised by Israel's historians", which would make him "contemporary with the early prophecy of Jeremiah, with which it shares both language and ideas" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Zephaniah](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/zephaniah/0); [2 Kings 22:1–23:30](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2KI22.htm#V1); [2 Kings](2-kings.md); [Jeremiah 1:1–2](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JER01.htm#V1); [Jeremiah](jeremiah.md)). Christine Hayes notes that "Some of his prophecies seem to date to the time, we think, before Josiah's reforms in 622", and that for Zephaniah, as for Amos, the long-awaited day of the Lord "will not in fact be a day of triumph, but a day of dark destruction and despair" ([Christine Hayes, Introduction to the Old Testament, Lecture 18, Open Yale Courses](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-18); [Amos 5:18–20](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/AMO05.htm#V18); [Amos](amos.md)). The Catholic Encyclopedia of 1912 notes that "The universality of the judgment as well as of the redemption is so forcibly expressed in Sophonias that his book may be regarded as the 'Catholic Epistle' of the Old Testament" ([New Advent, Catholic Encyclopedia (1912), Sophonias (Zephaniah)](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/14146a.htm)). Whether the long heading makes him a descendant of King Hezekiah, and whether the last verses are his own, are open questions below.

Zephaniah is Jewish Scripture: "Zephaniah is one of the 12 books of Minor Prophets ('Trei Asar'), marked by their shortness", and the prophet "calls for reform, justice, and humility" ([Sefaria, Zephaniah](https://www.sefaria.org/Zephaniah)). The Talmud counts the twelve as "one book of the Twelve Prophets" ([Sefaria, Bava Batra 14b:9](https://www.sefaria.org/Bava_Batra.14b.9)). In Christian Bibles Zephaniah is "The ninth of the twelve Minor Prophets", and older Catholic and Orthodox sources call him Sophonias ([New Advent, Catholic Encyclopedia (1912), Sophonias (Zephaniah)](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/14146a.htm); [Orthodox Church in America, Prophet Zephaniah](https://www.oca.org/saints/lives/2026/12/03/103465-prophet-zephaniah)). The New Testament never quotes the book directly, though Enter the Bible notes an echo of [Zephaniah 3:13](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ZEP03.htm#V13) in [Revelation 14:5](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/REV14.htm#V5), "In their mouth was found no lie" ([Enter the Bible, Bible in the World – Zephaniah](https://enterthebible.org/courses/zephaniah/lessons/bible-in-the-world-zephaniah/); [Revelation](../new-testament/revelation.md)).

## How the churches read it

Where the churches, or their interpreters, read the book differently, or where it has played a part in their history. Each entry starts with its source's score.

- `7` [Catechism of the Catholic Church, 722](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P23.HTM): **Mary, the Daughter of Zion.** The **Catholic Church** teaches that "It was quite correct for the angel Gabriel to greet her as the 'Daughter of Zion': 'Rejoice'", with a reference to [Zephaniah 3:14](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ZEP03.htm#V14) ([Luke 1:28](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/LUK01.htm#V28)). The song of 3:14–18 is the first reading on the feast of the Visitation, with Mary's visit to Elizabeth ([USCCB, Readings for the Feast of the Visitation of the Blessed Virgin Mary (31 May 2025)](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/readings/053125.cfm); [Luke 1:39–56](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/LUK01.htm#V39)), and on the Third Sunday of Advent in Year C, with "Rejoice in the Lord always" and John the Baptist's preaching ([USCCB, Readings for the Third Sunday of Advent (12 December 2021)](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/readings/121221.cfm); [Philippians 4:4](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/PHP04.htm#V4), [Luke 3:10–18](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/LUK03.htm#V10); [Luke](../new-testament/luke.md)).
- `7` [Orthodox Church in America, Prophet Zephaniah](https://www.oca.org/saints/lives/2026/12/03/103465-prophet-zephaniah): **the Orthodox commemoration.** The **Orthodox** Church commemorates the prophet on 3 December. Its life holds that he "was a contemporary of the Prophet Jeremiah", "was from the tribe of Simeon", and "lived at the royal court, where he preached repentance and helped King Josiah eliminate idol-worship". The song of [Zephaniah 3:14–19](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ZEP03.htm#V14) is among the readings for the Entry of the Lord into Jerusalem, Palm Sunday, with the prophecy of the king riding on a donkey ([Orthodox Church in America, Scripture Readings for Palm Sunday (5 April 2026)](https://www.oca.org/readings/daily/2026/04/05); [Zechariah 9:9](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ZEC09.htm#V9), [Matthew 21:1–11](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MAT21.htm#V1)).
- `7` [Augustine, City of God, Book XVIII, 33](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/120118.htm) (New Advent): **Christ and the calling of the nations.** Augustine, a teacher of the **Catholic** and the whole **Western** church, heads his chapter "What Jeremiah and Zephaniah Have, by the Prophetic Spirit, Spoken Before Concerning Christ and the Calling of the Nations". He reads [Zephaniah 3:8](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ZEP03.htm#V8) in the Old Latin, "in the day of my resurrection", the worship of "all the isles of the nations" ([Zephaniah 2:11](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ZEP02.htm#V11)) and the humble people left in Israel ([Zephaniah 3:9–13](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ZEP03.htm#V9)) as fulfilled in the church: "These are the remnant of that nation who have believed in Christ" ([Romans 9:27](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ROM09.htm#V27)).
- `6` [New Advent, Catholic Encyclopedia (1908), Dies Irae](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/04787a.htm): **the day of wrath in the Mass for the dead.** The medieval hymn *Dies irae*, "the sequence in requiem Masses", takes its opening words from the Latin of [Zephaniah 1:15](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ZEP01.htm#V15): "The composer found his Biblical text in Soph. (i. 15, 16): 'Dies iræ dies illa. . . dies tubæ et clangoris'". The encyclopedia reports "the conjecture generally entertained by hymnologists, that the Dies Iræ was composed by a Franciscan in the thirteenth century", most often ascribed to Thomas of Celano. Enter the Bible notes that in this way "Zephaniah plays a major role in the Mass for the Dead in the Christian tradition", and that the chant has been quoted by composers from Mozart and Verdi to film scores ([Enter the Bible, Bible in the World – Zephaniah](https://enterthebible.org/courses/zephaniah/lessons/bible-in-the-world-zephaniah/)).
- `6` [John Calvin, Commentary on Zephaniah 3:9](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/calvin/calcom29.iv.iv.x.html) (Christian Classics Ethereal Library): **the nations gathered into the church.** The **Reformed** reformer reads the promise of pure lips as God's answer to the objection that judgement would leave his name nowhere invoked: "he will not only defend his Church in Judea, but will also gather into it nations far and wide, so that his name shall be everywhere celebrated". He adds that "the invocation of God's name is his peculiar work; for men do not pray through the suggestion of the flesh, but when God draws them". He sums up the book as a call to the faithful to "patience, setting before them the hope of favor" ([John Calvin, Commentary on Zephaniah, Preface](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/calvin/calcom29.iv.i.html)).
- `6` [Matthew Henry, Commentary on the Whole Bible, Zephaniah 3](https://www.ccel.org/h/henry/mhc2/MHC36003.HTM) (Christian Classics Ethereal Library): **the gospel in the promises.** The **Presbyterian** commentator holds that the promises of chapter 3 "were to have their full accomplishment in gospel-times and gospel-graces". On "a mighty one who will save" ([Zephaniah 3:17](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ZEP03.htm#V17)) he writes: "He will save. He will be Jesus, will answer the name, for he will save his people from their sins". On the "one shoulder" of 3:9 he writes that "When Christians are unanimous in the service of God the work goes on cheerfully".
- `6` [Enter the Bible, Bible in the World – Zephaniah](https://enterthebible.org/courses/zephaniah/lessons/bible-in-the-world-zephaniah/) (Luther Seminary): **Luther and the Protestant lectionary.** In his lectures on the minor prophets in 1525, Martin Luther, the **Lutheran** reformer, found Zephaniah "quite simple and clear", making "the clearest prophecies about the kingdom of Christ"; he read the judgement on "the face of the earth" as the Babylonian exile of Judah, and compared the "idolatrous priests" with the clergy of his own day ([Zephaniah 1:2–6](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ZEP01.htm#V2)). In the Revised Common Lectionary, used "to varying degrees by the vast majority of mainline Protestant churches in Canada and the United States" ([Vanderbilt Divinity Library, Revised Common Lectionary: FAQ](https://lectionary.library.vanderbilt.edu/faq/)), [Zephaniah 3:14–20](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ZEP03.htm#V14) is read on the Third Sunday of Advent in Year C and at the Easter Vigil, and [Zephaniah 1:7](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ZEP01.htm#V7) and [Zephaniah 1:12–18](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ZEP01.htm#V12) near the end of the church year in Year A ([Vanderbilt Divinity Library, Revised Common Lectionary: Sunday citations](https://lectionary.library.vanderbilt.edu/sunday-citations/)).

## Open questions

Questions on which the churches or scholars disagree. These guidelines don't pick a side.

- `7` [Christine Hayes, Introduction to the Old Testament, Lecture 18, Open Yale Courses](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-18): **are the joyful last verses Zephaniah's own?** Hayes notes that the song of [Zephaniah 3:14–15](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ZEP03.htm#V14) "seems to announce the salvation as happening now", and that "a lot of scholars think that this was Zephaniah's reaction to Josiah and Josiah's reform". The Catholic Encyclopedia of 1912 reports the opposite view, that "The passage most frequently considered an addition of a later date is iii, 14-20, because the tone of a herald of salvation here adopted does not agree with that of the prophecies of the threatening judgment of the two earlier chapters", and answers that "It is, however, the custom of the Prophets after a terrifying warning of the judgments of Jahve to close with a glimpse of the brilliant future", so that "Sophonias, iii, 14-20 cannot be rejected" ([New Advent, Catholic Encyclopedia (1912), Sophonias (Zephaniah)](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/14146a.htm)). The HarperCollins Bible Dictionary's outline marks the last part of the promise, after 3:14–17, "For the exiles in Babylonia", as a "deuteronomic addition" ([Bible Odyssey, Zephaniah, The Book of (HarperCollins Bible Dictionary)](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/dictionary/zephaniah-the-book-of/); [Zephaniah 3:18–20](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ZEP03.htm#V18)).
- `6` [Enter the Bible, Introductory Issues in Zephaniah](https://enterthebible.org/courses/zephaniah/lessons/introductory-issues-in-zephaniah/) (Luther Seminary): **who were Zephaniah's ancestors?** The heading traces him back four generations to a Hezekiah ([Zephaniah 1:1](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ZEP01.htm#V1)). The Jewish Encyclopedia of 1906 holds that "He seems, therefore, to have been a descendant of Hezekiah, King of Judah, since otherwise only the name of his father would have been given" ([Jewish Encyclopedia, Zephaniah (1906)](https://jewishencyclopedia.com/articles/15242-zephaniah)). The Catholic Encyclopedia answers that if King Hezekiah were meant, "the explanatory phrase 'King of Judah' would undoubtedly have been put in apposition to the name" ([New Advent, Catholic Encyclopedia (1912), Sophonias (Zephaniah)](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/14146a.htm)), and Enter the Bible notes that "many, if not most, interpreters now regard a specific connection to King Hezekiah as unprovable". The name of his father, Cushi, "has raised questions about Zephaniah's racial and ethnic identity": "If the reference to Zephaniah as ben Cushi can be interpreted as his hailing from Ethiopia", some scholars "have suggested that Zephaniah demonstrates a keen interest in Ethiopia" ([Zephaniah 2:12](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ZEP02.htm#V12), [Zephaniah 3:10](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ZEP03.htm#V10); [Genesis 10:6](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/GEN10.htm#V6)). "This is a modern tradition, however": earlier Jewish and Christian traditions "suggest that Zephaniah was from Judah, possibly Jerusalem, and possibly even of royal blood" ([Enter the Bible, Bible in the World – Zephaniah](https://enterthebible.org/courses/zephaniah/lessons/bible-in-the-world-zephaniah/)). The Orthodox life of the prophet holds that "He was from the tribe of Simeon" ([Orthodox Church in America, Prophet Zephaniah](https://www.oca.org/saints/lives/2026/12/03/103465-prophet-zephaniah)).
- `6` [Enter the Bible, Theological Themes in Zephaniah](https://enterthebible.org/courses/zephaniah/lessons/theological-themes-in-zephaniah/) (Luther Seminary): **how does the judgement of the whole earth fit with the hope for all peoples?** The book opens with God sweeping away "everything from the surface of the earth" ([Zephaniah 1:2–3](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ZEP01.htm#V2)) and ends with the peoples calling on his name ([Zephaniah 3:9](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ZEP03.htm#V9)). Enter the Bible reads the two together: "In linking the fates of Judah and the nations, the prophet envisions an entire world remade into a faithful worshipping community", and the remnant are "survivors who have gone through the judgment". The ESV Global Study Bible holds that "God will judge the whole earth" and that "God extends blessing and grace to all peoples and nations", and that judgement and blessing come "both in the near future for the prophet and his audience" and "also in the more distant future" ([ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to Zephaniah](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-zephaniah/)). Luther, by contrast, took "the face of the earth" to mean only Judah, so that the judgement foretold the Babylonian exile ([Enter the Bible, Bible in the World – Zephaniah](https://enterthebible.org/courses/zephaniah/lessons/bible-in-the-world-zephaniah/)), while Calvin held that God "would propagate his grace wider, after having cleansed the earth" ([John Calvin, Commentary on Zephaniah 3:9](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/calvin/calcom29.iv.iv.x.html)).
