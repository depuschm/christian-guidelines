# Lamentations

The Book of Lamentations is "a collection of five poems that serve as an anguished response to the destruction of Jerusalem in 587 B.C., after a long siege by the invading Babylonian army". The poems combine laments, funeral dirges and the ancient Near Eastern "city-laments (in which the destruction of a city is mourned)". In chapters 1–4 "the separate stanzas begin with successive letters of the Hebrew alphabet from the first to the last", and the last chapter, "while not strictly an acrostic, nevertheless partially conforms to the pattern in its use of 22 lines, the number of letters in the Hebrew alphabet" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Lamentations](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/lamentations/0)). Chapter 3 extends the pattern "by having succeeding sets of three for each letter of the alphabet". Luther Seminary's Enter the Bible notes that "By using a highly rigid structure that breaks down in the final poem, Lamentations attempts to impose order on chaos, but ultimately the sorrow expressed in the book overwhelms any attempt at an orderly containment". Several voices speak: a narrator, "the female voice of personified (daughter) Zion", and in chapter 3 the "strongman" (Hebrew *geber*); "The voices converge in the first-person plural of Lamentations 5, when the people together cry out to be remembered and restored" ([Enter the Bible, Introductory Issues in Lamentations](https://enterthebible.org/courses/lamentations/lessons/introductory-issues-in-lamentations/)). The book "begins with the Hebrew word *Eicha* (how)", which gives it its Hebrew name ([Shawn Aster, Lamentations, My Jewish Learning](https://www.myjewishlearning.com/article/lamentations/); [Lamentations 1:1](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/LAM01.htm#V1)). The fall of the city is told in prose in [2 Kings 25:1–21](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2KI25.htm#V1).

The Hebrew book names no author. The Talmud holds that "Jeremiah wrote his own book, and the book of Kings, and Lamentations" ([Sefaria, Bava Batra 15a:2](https://www.sefaria.org/Bava_Batra.15a.2)), and the Greek Septuagint opens the book with a note: "And it happened, after Israel was taken captive and Ierousalem was laid waste, Ieremias sat weeping and gave this lament over Ierousalem and said" ([NETS, Lamentations, translated by Peter J. Gentry](https://ccat.sas.upenn.edu/nets/edition/36-lam-nets.pdf)). Chronicles records that "Jeremiah lamented for Josiah", and that such laments "are written in the lamentations" ([2 Chronicles 35:25](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2CH35.htm#V25)); Calvin notes that Jerome took the book to be that lament for Josiah, and rejects the idea: "we see nothing here that is suitable to that event" ([John Calvin, Commentary on Lamentations, Preface](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/calvin/calcom21.iii.i.html)). The USCCB introduction holds instead that the poems are anonymous, "probably used by survivors of the catastrophe of 587 B.C. in a communal expression of grief and mourning" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Lamentations](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/lamentations/0)); who wrote them is one of the open questions below.

Lamentations is Jewish Scripture: "The Book of Lamentations ('Eikhah') is one of the five megillot (scrolls), part of the section of the Hebrew Bible called Writings", and "It is read publicly on the fast of Tisha B'Av" ([Sefaria, Lamentations](https://www.sefaria.org/Lamentations)), which "commemorates two catastrophic events: the destruction of the first and second temples" ([Adele Berlin, Tisha B'Av, Bible Odyssey](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/tisha-bav/)). In Christian Bibles it stands among the prophets, after Jeremiah: "In the Roman Catholic version of the Bible, Lamentations is appended to the book of Jeremiah" ([Shawn Aster, Lamentations, My Jewish Learning](https://www.myjewishlearning.com/article/lamentations/)). The Greek Bible places Jeremiah, Baruch, Lamentations and the Letter of Jeremiah together ([NETS, electronic edition](https://ccat.sas.upenn.edu/nets/edition/)), and in Orthodox Bibles "The books of Baruch and the Letter of Jeremiah from the apocrypha go together with this prophetic book" ([Orthodox Church in America, The Orthodox Faith: Old Testament, Prophets](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/doctrine-scripture/old-testament/prophets); [deuterocanon](../README.md#deuterocanon)). Every church reads Lamentations.

## How the churches read it

Where the churches, or their interpreters, read the book differently, or where it has played a part in their history. Each entry starts with its source's score.

- `7` [Church of England, Common Worship: Passiontide and Holy Week, Seasonal Material](https://www.churchofengland.org/liturgy/common-worship/seasonal-material/passiontide/passiontide-and-holy): **"Is it nothing to you?" in Holy Week.** The **Anglican** seasonal material for Passiontide gives as its first short passage of Scripture "Is it nothing to you, all you who pass by? Look and see if there is any sorrow like my sorrow" ([Lamentations 1:12](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/LAM01.htm#V12)), with Jesus' saying about the grain of wheat that dies and Paul's "May I never boast of anything except the cross of our Lord Jesus Christ" ([John 12:24](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JHN12.htm#V24), [Galatians 6:14](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/GAL06.htm#V14)). In the Revised Common Lectionary, used by most mainline **Protestant** churches in Canada and the United States ([Vanderbilt Divinity Library, Revised Common Lectionary: FAQ](https://lectionary.library.vanderbilt.edu/faq/)), [Lamentations 3:1–9](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/LAM03.htm#V1) and [Lamentations 3:19–24](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/LAM03.htm#V19) are read on Holy Saturday in each of its three years ([Vanderbilt Divinity Library, Revised Common Lectionary: Sunday citations](https://lectionary.library.vanderbilt.edu/sunday-citations/)). The **Catholic** New American Bible notes that "passages from chap. 3 are often incorporated into Christian services for Holy Thursday or Good Friday", but that "the Church has otherwise tended to neglect the book" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Lamentations](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/lamentations/0)).
- `7` [Catechism of the Catholic Church, 1430–1433](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P4A.HTM): **"Turn us to yourself."** The **Catholic** Catechism quotes the book's last prayer ([Lamentations 5:21](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/LAM05.htm#V21)) to teach that "Conversion is first of all a work of the grace of God who makes our hearts return to him: 'Restore us to thyself, O LORD, that we may be restored!'"
- `7` [Orthodox Church in America, The Orthodox Faith: Holy Saturday](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/worship/the-church-year/holy-saturday): **the Orthodox "Lamentations" of Holy Saturday.** At the **Orthodox** Matins of Holy Saturday, sung on Friday night, "the entire Psalm 119 is read with a verse praising the dead Saviour chanted between each of its lines", and "The Praises, as the verses are called, glorify God as 'the Resurrection and the Life'" ([Psalm 119](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/PSA119.htm)). This part of the service "includes what are called the Lamentations of the Theotokos", in which Christ's mother "laments and grieves over His lifeless Body" ([OrthoChristian, The Lamentations Service for Holy Saturday Matins](https://orthochristian.com/78602.html)). These Lamentations are hymns of the Lenten Triodion, not readings from the biblical book.
- `6` [Catholic Encyclopedia (1912), Tenebræ](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/14506a.htm): **Tenebrae.** In the Western night office of the last three days of Holy Week, called Tenebrae from the putting out of the candles, the readings were "taken from the Lamentations of Jeremias". The **Catholic** encyclopedia notes that these readings "remain now as when we first hear of them in the eighth century", that the office was "treated as a sort of funeral service, or dirge, commemorating the death of Jesus Christ", and that its darkness "seems to have been designedly chosen to mark the Church's desolation".
- `6` [John Calvin, Commentary on Lamentations, Preface](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/calvin/calcom21.iii.i.html) (Christian Classics Ethereal Library): **hope among the ruins.** The **Reformed** reformer held that Jeremiah wrote the book after the city fell, "to speak first of God's judgments; secondly, to exhort the people to repentance; thirdly, to encourage them to hope; and lastly, to open the door for prayer to God". Though the temple was destroyed and "the Covenant of God appeared as made void", Calvin wrote, "yet hope still remained, provided the people sought God in true repentance and faith".
- `6` [Hymnary.org, Great Is Thy Faithfulness](https://hymnary.org/text/great_is_thy_faithfulness_o_god_my_fathe): **"Great is your faithfulness" in hymnody.** Thomas O. Chisholm wrote the hymn "Great is thy faithfulness, O God my Father" in 1923, drawing on [Lamentations 3:22–23](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/LAM03.htm#V22); Hymnary.org notes that "he found truth in the words he encountered in Lamentations 3:22-23", and lists it in **Protestant** and **Anglican** hymnals such as the Baptist Hymnal (1991), the African Methodist Episcopal Church Hymnal, The United Methodist Hymnal and Ancient and Modern.

## Open questions

Questions on which the churches or scholars disagree. These guidelines don't pick a side.

- `7` [USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Lamentations](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/lamentations/0): **who wrote Lamentations?** Jewish and Christian tradition names Jeremiah: the Talmud holds that "Jeremiah wrote his own book, and the book of Kings, and Lamentations" ([Sefaria, Bava Batra 15a:2](https://www.sefaria.org/Bava_Batra.15a.2)), the Septuagint opens the book with the words "Ieremias sat weeping and gave this lament over Ierousalem" ([NETS, Lamentations, translated by Peter J. Gentry](https://ccat.sas.upenn.edu/nets/edition/36-lam-nets.pdf)), and Calvin held that "Jeremiah, after the city was destroyed and the Temple burnt, bewailed the miserable state of his own nation" ([John Calvin, Commentary on Lamentations, Preface](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/calvin/calcom21.iii.i.html)). Christine Hayes notes that "The Bible itself doesn't make this claim; it's an old tradition", which may have arisen because Jeremiah "reveals the most to us about his personal suffering and grief", and "because he was present as an eyewitness at the destruction" ([Christine Hayes, Introduction to the Old Testament, Lecture 20, Open Yale Courses](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-20)). The USCCB introduction holds that authorship by Jeremiah "is unlikely": "The Hebrew text of the book does not mention Jeremiah at all, and it is difficult to square some of the content of the poetry with what one finds in the Book of Jeremiah itself"; the poems "may have been composed separately and grouped together later", as "anonymous compositions probably used by survivors of the catastrophe of 587 B.C." The ESV Global Study Bible holds that "The author of this literary masterpiece is unknown", and that the book "was likely written between 586 and 516 B.C., with an early date being more probable" ([ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to Lamentations](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-lamentations/)).
- `7` [USCCB, Lamentations, chapter 5, with notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/lamentations/5) (New American Bible, Revised Edition): **does the book end in hope?** The last verse can be read in more than one way. The WEBBE has "But you have utterly rejected us. You are very angry against us" ([Lamentations 5:22](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/LAM05.htm#V22)); the NRSVUE "unless you have utterly rejected us and are angry with us beyond measure" ([Bible Gateway, Lamentations 1–5 (NRSVUE)](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Lamentations%201-5&version=NRSVUE)); and the New American Bible "For now you have indeed rejected us and utterly turned your wrath against us", with the note that "Unlike most of the laments found in the Book of Psalms, the Book of Lamentations never moves from lament to thanksgiving. It ends with this question still unanswered by God". The ESV Global Study Bible holds that "Hope, not despair, is the central theme in Lamentations" ([ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to Lamentations](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-lamentations/)). Enter the Bible notes that "Christian interpreters have often sought to make the middle of Lamentations 3 function as the answer to the book's laments", but that "The book does not end in doxology as is frequently claimed for the lament psalms", and "The possibility that God has permanently rejected the people remains open" ([Enter the Bible, Theological Themes in Lamentations](https://enterthebible.org/courses/lamentations/lessons/theological-themes-in-lamentations/)). In Jewish reading, the Midrash has Rabbi Shimon ben Lakish say of the last verse, "If it is despising, there is no hope; if it is anger, there is hope, as anyone who is angry will ultimately be assuaged" ([Sefaria, Eikhah Rabbah 5:22](https://www.sefaria.org/Eikhah_Rabbah.5.22)); Rashi explains that "Because he concludes with words of reproof, he had to repeat the preceding verse again" ([Sefaria, Rashi on Lamentations 5:22](https://www.sefaria.org/Rashi_on_Lamentations.5.22.2)), and in the synagogue the reading builds to a climax "sung aloud by the entire congregation: 'Turn us to you, O Lord, and we will return. Renew our days as of old'" ([Irving Greenberg, The Book of Lamentations, My Jewish Learning](https://www.myjewishlearning.com/article/the-book-of-lamentations/)).
- `7` [Christine Hayes, Introduction to the Old Testament, Lecture 20, Open Yale Courses](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-20): **how should readers take the picture of God as an enemy?** Hayes notes that the poet "does adopt the standard Deuteronomistic interpretation of events which infers sin from suffering", but that the descriptions of God's wrath "are some of the most powerful and most violent poetry in the Hebrew Bible. They tend to divert attention, in fact, from the people's guilt and focus attention on their suffering", with "an emphasis not so much on Israel's guilt, but on Israel's tremendous suffering, God's hardheartedness". Shawn Aster reads it otherwise: "The description of God as enemy is part of the book's attempt to justify the destruction", and "Lamentations does not remonstrate with God, or accuse Him of injustice, so much as it asks for His mercy" ([Shawn Aster, Lamentations, My Jewish Learning](https://www.myjewishlearning.com/article/lamentations/)). Calvin held that "the Church intimates that God was the author of that sorrow which she deplored", and that "it is necessary to know this, lest men should be carried away into excesses in their mourning" ([John Calvin, Commentary on Lamentations 1:12](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/calvin/calcom21.iii.ii.xvii.html)). Enter the Bible adds that the picture of Zion as an unfaithful wife punished by her husband "risks perpetuating and justifying male abuse of women", that "There is no easy way to 'redeem' the metaphor", and that it "may have been broken in the lament tradition which voices Israel's demands to God in a tone that never would have been permitted for an adulterous wife in an ancient patriarchal society" ([Enter the Bible, Introductory Issues in Lamentations](https://enterthebible.org/courses/lamentations/lessons/introductory-issues-in-lamentations/)).
- `6` [Enter the Bible, Introductory Issues in Lamentations](https://enterthebible.org/courses/lamentations/lessons/introductory-issues-in-lamentations/) (Luther Seminary): **has God already answered, or is the speaker still pleading?** "Modern Bible translations translate the verbs in these verses in the past tense", as the WEBBE does: "You heard my voice", "You came near in the day that I called on you" ([Lamentations 3:56–63](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/LAM03.htm#V56)). "Several recent commentaries have understood the perfect Hebrew verb forms in vv. 56-62 not as past tense but as expressions of will", so that the speaker "pleads with God to hear, see, and come". "Both translations are grammatically defensible. How one understands the overall flow of the book and the theological tensions it articulates shapes the interpreter's decision on this point."
