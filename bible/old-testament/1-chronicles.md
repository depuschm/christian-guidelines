# 1 Chronicles

1 Chronicles retells the story of Israel from Adam to the death of David. Its first nine chapters are genealogies. They run from "Adam, Seth, Enosh" through Abraham and the twelve sons of Israel to the people who lived in Jerusalem after the exile ([1 Chronicles 1:1–4](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1CH01.htm#V1), [1 Chronicles 2:1–2](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1CH02.htm#V1), [1 Chronicles 9:1–3](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1CH09.htm#V1)). The rest of the book is about David: all Israel makes him king, he brings the ark of God to Jerusalem with the singing of the Levites, and he prepares everything for the temple that his son Solomon will build ([1 Chronicles 11:1–3](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1CH11.htm#V1), [1 Chronicles 15:16](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1CH15.htm#V16), [1 Chronicles 22:5](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1CH22.htm#V5)). The introduction to the New American Bible explains that the Chronicler "aimed to establish and defend the legitimate claims of the Davidic monarchy in Israel's history, and to underscore the status of Jerusalem and its divinely established Temple worship as the center of religious life for the Jewish people". He "was much more interested in David's religious and cultic influence than in his political power" ([USCCB, Introduction to 1 Chronicles](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/1chronicles/0)). Christine Hayes notes that Chronicles leaves out "a lot of material that sheds a poor light on Israel's kings", such as the story of David and Bathsheba. She calls it "an inner-biblical interpretation. It is the Bible interpreting itself" ([Christine Hayes, The Restoration: 1 and 2 Chronicles, Ezra and Nehemiah, Open Yale Courses](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-22)).

1 and 2 Chronicles were one book in the Hebrew Bible. Sefaria calls 1 Chronicles "the first half of the last book of the Hebrew Bible" ([Sefaria, I Chronicles](https://www.sefaria.org/I_Chronicles)), and the Talmud's order of the Writings ends with "Ezra and Chronicles" ([Talmud, Bava Batra 14b:11](https://www.sefaria.org/Bava_Batra.14b.11)). Christian Bibles count Chronicles among the historical books, after Kings. In the Septuagint, the Greek Bible whose listing the Orthodox Church generally follows, "the Chronicles are called Paralipomena which means 'that which has been left out'", indicating their purpose as being "to fill in what has been excluded from the earlier historical books of the Bible" ([Orthodox Church in America, The Orthodox Faith: Old Testament, History](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/doctrine-scripture/old-testament/history)). The USCCB introduction adds that the books "are much more than a supplement to Samuel and Kings". The story continues in [2 Chronicles](2-chronicles.md), from Solomon to the exile and the decree of Cyrus.

The book names no author. The Talmud holds that "Ezra wrote his own book and the genealogy of the book of Chronicles until his period" ([Talmud, Bava Batra 15a:2](https://www.sefaria.org/Bava_Batra.15a.2)). Scholars speak instead of "the Chronicler", "the conventional designation for the anonymous author", and the USCCB introduction places him at "the end of the fifth century B.C." ([USCCB, Introduction to 1 Chronicles](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/1chronicles/0)). The debate is set out under open questions below.

## How the churches read it

Where the churches, or their interpreters, read the book differently, or where it has played a part in their history. Each entry starts with its source's score.

- `7` [Jerome, Letter 53, to Paulinus (New Advent)](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/3001053.htm): on **the genealogies and the gospel** ([1 Chronicles 1:1–9:44](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1CH01.htm#V1)), Jerome wrote in 394 that "The book of things omitted or epitome of the old dispensation is of such importance and value that without it any one who should claim to himself a knowledge of the scriptures would make himself a laughing stock in his own eyes", and that "Every name used in it, nay even the conjunction of the words, serves to throw light on narratives passed over in the books of Kings and upon questions suggested by the gospel" (8). The gospels give Jesus' own genealogy, Matthew's from Abraham through David, Luke's back to "Adam, the son of God" ([Matthew 1:1–17](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MAT01.htm#V1), [Luke 3:23–38](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/LUK03.htm#V23)). The evangelical ESV Global Study Bible reads the Chronicler's genealogies, which trace Israel's ancestry "back to Adam", as "a striking reminder that Israel was at the center of God's purpose from the very beginning of creation" ([ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to 1–2 Chronicles](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-1-2-chronicles/)).
- `7` [John Paul II, General Audience of 6 June 2001](https://www.vatican.va/content/john-paul-ii/en/audiences/2001/documents/hf_jp-ii_aud_20010606.html): on **David's blessing and the offering** ([1 Chronicles 29:10–14](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1CH29.htm#V10)), Pope John Paul II, commenting on the canticle for the **Catholic** faithful, taught that "David expresses the profound truth that all is grace", and that its praise and thanksgiving belong with "an authentic spirituality of 'offering' that the Christian liturgy makes us live, above all, in the celebration of the Eucharist", in which the priest offers bread and wine received from God; he found "similar sentiments in the Byzantine Divine Liturgy". In the **Church of England**'s Common Worship, the first of the prayers at the preparation of the table uses words from these verses: "Yours, Lord, is the greatness, the power, the glory, the splendour, and the majesty; for everything in heaven and on earth is yours", with the response "All things come from you, and of your own do we give you" ([Church of England, Common Worship: Prayers at the Preparation of the Table](https://www.churchofengland.org/liturgy/common-worship/holy-communion-order-one/prayers-preparation-table)).
- `6` [Matthew Henry, Commentary on the Whole Bible, 1 Chronicles 29 (Christian Classics Ethereal Library)](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/henry/mhc2.iCh.xxx.html): on **David's blessing and the end of the Lord's Prayer** ([1 Chronicles 29:11](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1CH29.htm#V11), [Matthew 6:13](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MAT06.htm#V13)), Matthew Henry, a nonconformist pastor of a Presbyterian congregation ([Christian Classics Ethereal Library, Matthew Henry](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/henry)), wrote that "Our Lord's prayer ends with a doxology much like this which David here begins with— for thine is the kingdom, the power, and the glory." Kaufmann Kohler, in the Jewish Encyclopedia, held that "The doxology added in Matthew, following a number of manuscripts, is a portion of I Chron. xxix. 11, and was the liturgical chant with which the Lord's Prayer was concluded in the Church", and that in Jewish worship "the whole verse" is chanted "at the opening of the Ark of the Law" ([Jewish Encyclopedia, Lord's Prayer, The (1906)](https://jewishencyclopedia.com/articles/10112-lords-prayer-the)). The **Catholic** Catechism teaches that the final doxology "takes up again, by inclusion, the first three petitions to our Father", "now proclaimed as adoration and thanksgiving, as in the liturgy of heaven" ([Catechism of the Catholic Church, 2855](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__PAD.HTM)).

## Open questions

Questions on which the churches or scholars disagree, for example about the book's authorship or the meaning of a passage. These guidelines don't pick a side.

- `7` [Christine Hayes, The Restoration: 1 and 2 Chronicles, Ezra and Nehemiah](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-22) (Open Yale Courses): **how does Chronicles relate to Samuel and Kings, and how far is it history?** Hayes calls Chronicles "a second account of the history of Israel" that echoes Samuel and Kings but has "more of a priestly bias": "you won't find the story of David and Bathsheba", and the Chronicler is "less interested in David's political genius" than in "his role in establishing Jerusalem as a religious capital, in planning a temple, in organizing the music for temple worship". At the point where 2 Samuel tells of Bathsheba, 1 Chronicles goes straight from David staying in Jerusalem to the capture of Rabbah ([1 Chronicles 20:1](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1CH20.htm#V1), [2 Samuel 11:1](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2SA11.htm#V1), [2 Samuel 12:26–29](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2SA12.htm#V26)). The USCCB introduction describes biblical history as "primarily interpretative", "less concerned with reporting in precise detail all the facts of a situation than with drawing out the meaning of those facts", and holds that the elaborate ritual which the book credits to David "in point of fact, only gradually evolved in the Second Temple period"; it also notes that the Chronicler's work "contains early preexilic material not found in the Books of Kings" ([USCCB, Introduction to 1 Chronicles](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/1chronicles/0)). The ESV Global Study Bible holds that the Chronicler "had scribal training and access to the temple records", and that he "presented an interpretation of Israel's past, drawing mainly on the books of Samuel and Kings" ([ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to 1–2 Chronicles](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-1-2-chronicles/)).
- `7` [Talmud, Bava Batra 15a:2 (Sefaria)](https://www.sefaria.org/Bava_Batra.15a.2): **who wrote Chronicles, and when?** The Talmud holds that "Ezra wrote his own book and the genealogy of the book of Chronicles until his period". The Orthodox Church in America's account says that the books "may well have been compiled by Ezra, although scholars consider them as the work of third century authors, perhaps the same who wrote Ezra and Nehemiah" ([Orthodox Church in America, The Orthodox Faith: Old Testament, History](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/doctrine-scripture/old-testament/history)). The ESV Global Study Bible holds that the author "was either a priest or Levite who was employed in the service of the temple during the Persian period (539–332 B.C.)" ([ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to 1–2 Chronicles](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-1-2-chronicles/)), and the USCCB introduction dates the work to "the end of the fifth century B.C." Hayes notes that Chronicles, Ezra and Nehemiah "may derive from the same author", and that all four books "were probably edited in the late fifth century BCE, maybe close to the fourth century" ([Christine Hayes, The Restoration: 1 and 2 Chronicles, Ezra and Nehemiah, Open Yale Courses](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-22)).
- `6` [Robin A. Leaver, Bach and the Bible (Christian History)](https://christianhistoryinstitute.org/magazine/article/bach-and-the-bible): **is David's ordering of temple music a pattern for the church's music?** 1 Chronicles shows David appointing Levites to sing and play "with harps, with stringed instruments, and with cymbals" ([1 Chronicles 25:1–8](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1CH25.htm#V1)). Leaver reports that Johann Sebastian Bach wrote in the margin of his Bible, an edition by Abraham Calov with Luther's comments, beside chapter 25: "NB. This chapter is the true foundation of all God-pleasing church music", and beside 28:21, "Splendid proof that, besides other arrangements of the service of worship, music too was instituted by the Spirit of God through David". In the **Lutheran** Church–Missouri Synod's magazine, R. Reed Lessing explains that the chapter describes the musicians "in prophetic terms", so that "by means of instrumental music, the Lord prophetically proclaimed His Word to Israel" ([R. Reed Lessing, J. S. Bach: A Musician—and a Lover of Lists! (The Lutheran Witness, 2009)](https://witness.lcms.org/2009/j-s-bach-a-musicianand-a-lover-of-lists-3-2009/)). The **Reformed** reformer John Calvin held instead that cymbals, harps and "all that kind of music" belonged to "the puerile instruction of the law: I speak of the stated service of the temple", and that in the church's assemblies "musical instruments in celebrating the praises of God would be no more suitable than the burning of incense, the lighting up of lamps, and the restoration of the other shadows of the law" ([John Calvin, Commentary on Psalm 33:1–4 (Christian Classics Ethereal Library)](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/calvin/calcom08.xxxix.i.html)).

## Tutorials and further reading

- `7` [Christine Hayes, The Restoration: 1 and 2 Chronicles, Ezra and Nehemiah](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-22) (Open Yale Courses, Introduction to the Old Testament, lecture 22): a university lecture, with a transcript; after finishing the psalms and the Song of Songs, it turns to the return from exile and to Chronicles as a second telling of Israel's history.
- `6` [BibleProject, 1 & 2 Chronicles](https://bibleproject.com/videos/chronicles/): an animated overview of Chronicles, "the last book in the Hebrew Bible", and its interest in "David, Solomon, God's temple, and the kings of Judah".
