# Job

The Book of Job is "an exquisite dramatic treatment of the problem of the suffering of the innocent". A prose prologue tells how a blameless man is tested: in the heavenly council the satan questions Job's sincerity, and "the Lord gives leave for a series of catastrophes to afflict Job". Three friends come to console him, and a long poetic dialogue follows, in which the friends "insist that his plight can only be a punishment for personal wrongdoing" and Job "challenges God to respond". A young bystander, Elihu, then speaks, and the LORD answers Job out of the whirlwind, "not by explaining divine justice, but by cataloguing the wonders of creation"; in a prose epilogue "the Lord restores Job's fortune" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Job](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/job/0)). The New American Bible notes that "the satan" means literally "adversary": "Here a member of the heavenly court, 'the accuser'", and "In later biblical traditions this character will be developed as the devil" ([USCCB, Job, chapter 1, with notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/job/1)); the WEBBE renders it as the name Satan ([Job 1:6–12](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JOB01.htm#V6)). Carol Newsom notes that the prose story "portrays the 'patient' Job, but in the rest of the book Job is an outspoken rebel" ([Carol Newsom, Job, Bible Odyssey](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/job/)).

The book names no author. The Babylonian Talmud records the tradition that "Moses wrote his own book, i.e., the Torah, and the portion of Balaam in the Torah, and the book of Job" ([Sefaria, Bava Batra 14b](https://www.sefaria.org/Bava_Batra.14b.12)). The USCCB introduction holds that "The author or authors of the book are unknown; it was probably composed some time between the seventh and fifth centuries B.C." ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Job](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/job/0)), and Orthodox teaching notes that the book is "usually dated sometime at the period of exile" ([Orthodox Church in America, The Orthodox Faith: Old Testament, Wisdom](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/doctrine-scripture/old-testament/wisdom)). Christine Hayes suggests that the prose frame "was probably a standard Ancient Near Eastern folktale of great antiquity", which "the Israelite author has used … for his own purposes" ([Christine Hayes, Introduction to the Old Testament, Lecture 20, Open Yale Courses](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-20)). Job himself is "apparently not an Israelite" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Job](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/job/0)); the prophet Ezekiel names him with Noah and Daniel as a righteous man ([Ezekiel 14:14](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/EZK14.htm#V14)). When the book was written, and whether Job lived, are among the open questions below.

Job is Jewish Scripture: "Job ('Iyov') is the third book of the section in the Hebrew Bible called Writings" ([Sefaria, Job](https://www.sefaria.org/Job)). In Christian Bibles it comes first among the wisdom books, with Psalms, Proverbs, Ecclesiastes and the Song of Songs, and, in Catholic and Orthodox Bibles, Sirach and the Wisdom of Solomon ([Orthodox Church in America, The Orthodox Faith: Old Testament, Wisdom](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/doctrine-scripture/old-testament/wisdom); [deuterocanon](../README.md#deuterocanon)). Every church reads it.

## How the churches read it

Where the churches, or their interpreters, read the book differently, or where it has played a part in their history. Each entry starts with its source's score.

- `8` [Salvifici doloris, 10–11](https://www.vatican.va/content/john-paul-ii/en/apost_letters/1984/documents/hf_jp-ii_apl_11021984_salvifici-doloris.html) (John Paul II, 1984): **innocent suffering.** In his apostolic letter on suffering, John Paul II writes that "In the Book of Job the question has found its most vivid expression". Job's friends hold that suffering "always strikes a man as punishment for a crime", but Job "challenges the truth of the principle that identifies suffering with punishment for sin", and God himself "recognizes that Job is not guilty". "His suffering is the suffering of someone who is innocent and it must be accepted as a mystery." The **Catholic** letter adds that the book "is not the last word on this subject in Revelation. In a certain way it is a foretelling of the Passion of Christ." The churches' wider teaching on suffering is set out in [Suffering, death and hope](../../general/suffering.md).
- `7` [Orthodox Church in America, Righteous Job the Long-Suffering](https://www.oca.org/saints/lives/2026/05/06/101301-righteous-job-the-long-suffering): **Job in Holy Week.** The **Orthodox** Church commemorates Job on 6 May, and "reads the book of Job … during Holy Week, drawing a parallel between Job and Christ as righteous men who suffered through no fault of their own". The Septuagint ends with the words that Job "will rise again with those whom the Lord raises up", which are read on Great and Holy Friday. *The Orthodox Faith* explains that the readings come "on the first days of Holy Week" because they deal with "the problem of suffering, which is brought to its ultimate completion in Christ" ([Orthodox Church in America, The Orthodox Faith: Old Testament, Wisdom](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/doctrine-scripture/old-testament/wisdom)).
- `7` [Church of England, Book of Common Prayer: The Order for the Burial of the Dead](https://www.churchofengland.org/liturgy/book-of-common-prayer/burial-dead): **Job at funerals.** The **Anglican** burial service opens, after Jesus' words "I am the resurrection and the life", with Job's: "I know that my Redeemer liveth, and that he shall stand at the latter day upon the earth", and "The Lord gave, and the Lord hath taken away; blessed be the name of the Lord" ([Job 19:25–27](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JOB19.htm#V25), [Job 1:21](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JOB01.htm#V21)). In the **Catholic** Church, a reading from Job 19 ([Job 19:23–27](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JOB19.htm#V23)) is one of the Old Testament readings in Masses for the Dead, under the heading "I know that my Vindicator lives" ([USCCB, Masses for the Dead: Readings from the Old Testament](https://www.usccb.org/prayer-and-worship/sacraments-and-sacramentals/bereavement-and-funerals/old-testament-readings)).
- `6` [Gregory the Great, Moralia on Job, Book One](https://faculty.georgetown.edu/jod/texts/moralia1.html) (a draft translation on the Georgetown University pages of James J. O'Donnell): **Job as a figure of Christ.** Pope Gregory the Great's commentary, "one of the most popular works of scriptural exegesis in the middle ages" ([James J. O'Donnell, Gregory the Great](https://faculty.georgetown.edu/jod/gregory.html)), reads the book in three senses: "First we lay the foundations of historical fact; then we lift up the mind to the citadel of faith through allegory; finally through the exposition of the moral sense we dress the edifice in its colored raiment". For Gregory, Job "should proclaim the Redeemer in his voice and resemble him in his way of life. His sufferings foretold Christ's passion".
- `6` [Matthew Henry, Commentary on the Whole Bible, Job 19](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/henry/mhc3.Job.xx.html) (Christian Classics Ethereal Library): **Job's confession of faith.** The **Reformed** commentator Matthew Henry calls [Job 19:25–27](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JOB19.htm#V25) "Job's creed, or confession of faith": "Job was taught of God to believe in a living Redeemer, and to look for the resurrection of the dead and the life of the world to come", and not "a deliverance from his trouble or a revival of his happiness in this world, as some would understand him".
- `6` [Tod Linafelt, The Patience of Job](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/the-patience-of-job/) (Bible Odyssey): **the patience of Job.** The letter of James holds Job up as an example: "You have heard of the perseverance of Job and have seen the Lord in the outcome" ([James 5:11](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JAS05.htm#V11); see [James](../new-testament/james.md)). The 1910 *Catholic Encyclopedia* calls Job "to us an example of patience" and the book "a book of consolation for all sufferers" ([Catholic Encyclopedia (1910), Job](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/08413a.htm)). Linafelt notes that the phrase "the patience of Job" comes from the King James Version, and that "Most biblical scholars agree that the Greek word (*hupomone*) translated by the KJV as 'patience' probably means something more like 'endurance' or even 'persistence'", which "makes much more sense of the character of Job".

## Open questions

Questions on which the churches or scholars disagree. These guidelines don't pick a side.

- `7` [USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Job](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/job/0): **does the book answer why the innocent suffer?** The USCCB introduction holds that "the lessons that the book teaches are not transparent", and that the book "does not definitively answer the problem of the suffering of the innocent, but challenges readers to come to their own understanding". Newsom finds it "difficult to say whether Job is simply subdued or genuinely satisfied by God's reply" ([Carol Newsom, Job, Bible Odyssey](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/job/)). For Christine Hayes, "God refuses to be seen as a moral accountant", and the book "provides no answer in the sense of an explanation or a justification of suffering and injustice" ([Christine Hayes, Introduction to the Old Testament, Lecture 20, Open Yale Courses](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-20)). John Paul II reads Job's suffering as "a mystery" and "a foretelling of the Passion of Christ" ([Salvifici doloris, 11](https://www.vatican.va/content/john-paul-ii/en/apost_letters/1984/documents/hf_jp-ii_apl_11021984_salvifici-doloris.html)), and Enter the Bible asks, "Is God with us, even if we cannot understand everything? Most believers who have read Job have concluded that the answer is yes" ([Enter the Bible, Introductory Issues in Job](https://enterthebible.org/courses/job/lessons/introductory-issues-in-job/)). The wider question of why God allows suffering is set out in [Suffering, death and hope](../../general/suffering.md#open-questions).
- `7` [Christine Hayes, Introduction to the Old Testament, Lecture 20, Open Yale Courses](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-20): **who is "the satan"?** Hayes holds that "The satan is certainly not the devil", but "a member of the divine counsel", "a kind of prosecuting attorney", and that "it's only in later Jewish, and especially Christian thought, that the term loses the definite article … and becomes a proper name, Satan". Adam Porter writes that in Job "the noun has a definite article ('the satan') and should be translated as a title ('the accuser')" ([Adam Porter, Satan, Bible Odyssey](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/satan/)), and the New American Bible notes that "In later biblical traditions this character will be developed as the devil" ([USCCB, Job, chapter 1, with notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/job/1)). The churches' readings name him Satan: John Paul II writes that "God permitted this testing as a result of Satan's provocation" ([Salvifici doloris, 11](https://www.vatican.va/content/john-paul-ii/en/apost_letters/1984/documents/hf_jp-ii_apl_11021984_salvifici-doloris.html)), the Orthodox life of Job that "God allowed Satan to afflict Job" ([Orthodox Church in America, Righteous Job the Long-Suffering](https://www.oca.org/saints/lives/2026/05/06/101301-righteous-job-the-long-suffering)), and Matthew Henry that "the devil, having a great enmity to Job for his eminent piety, begged and obtained leave to torment him", though he allows that the scene in heaven may be "parabolical" ([Matthew Henry, Commentary on the Whole Bible, Job 1](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/henry/mhc3.Job.ii.html)).
- `7` [USCCB, Job, chapter 19, with notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/job/19) (New American Bible, Revised Edition): **does "I know that my Redeemer lives" speak of the resurrection?** The New American Bible translates "Vindicator", and notes that "The meaning of this passage is obscure because the original text has been poorly preserved and the ancient versions do not agree among themselves". Job "asserts three times that he shall see a future vindicator (Hebrew *goel*), but he leaves the time and manner of this vindication undefined. The Vulgate translation has Job indicating a belief in resurrection after death, but the Hebrew and the other ancient versions are less specific." The 1910 *Catholic Encyclopedia* calls the passage Job's "famous declaration of the resurrection of the body", and holds that "the opinion of all early commentators" supports that reading ([Catholic Encyclopedia (1910), Job](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/08413a.htm)); Matthew Henry reads it as faith in "a living Redeemer" and "the resurrection of the dead" ([Matthew Henry, Commentary on the Whole Bible, Job 19](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/henry/mhc3.Job.xx.html)); and the churches' funeral services read it as a word of hope (see above).
- `7` [USCCB, Job, chapter 42, with notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/job/42) (New American Bible, Revised Edition): **what does Job repent of?** The WEBBE reads "Therefore I abhor myself, and repent in dust and ashes", and the NRSVUE "therefore I despise myself and repent in dust and ashes" ([Bible Gateway, Job 42 (NRSVUE)](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Job%2042&version=NRSVUE); [Job 42:6](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JOB42.htm#V6)). The New American Bible translates "Therefore I disown what I have said, and repent in dust and ashes", and calls it "A difficult verse. Some doubt, in view of God's commendation in v. 7, that Job does in fact express repentance, and alternative translations are often given"; "dust and ashes" "can refer to the human condition … or to Job's ash heap". Hayes asks, "What is he repenting of? Certainly not of sin", and reads it as Job's giving up the idea "that led him to ascribe wickedness to God" ([Christine Hayes, Introduction to the Old Testament, Lecture 20, Open Yale Courses](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-20)).
- `7` [Sefaria, Bava Batra 15a](https://www.sefaria.org/Bava_Batra.15a.15): **did Job live, and when was the book written?** In the Talmud, one sage said that "Job never existed and was never created; there was never such a person as Job. Rather, his story was a parable", and Rabbi Shmuel bar Naḥmani answered from the book's first verse ([Job 1:1](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JOB01.htm#V1)); other sages placed Job in the time of Moses, or among the exiles who returned from Babylon. The 1910 *Catholic Encyclopedia* notes that "Catholic commentators, however, almost without exception, hold Job to have actually existed", and that "All the Fathers considered Job an historical person" ([Catholic Encyclopedia (1910), Job](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/08413a.htm)); Matthew Henry held the book to be "for the substance of it, a true history, and not a romance, though the dialogues are poetical", and "of equal date with the book of Genesis itself" ([Matthew Henry, Commentary on the Whole Bible, Job: Introduction](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/henry/mhc3.Job.i.html)). The USCCB introduction holds that "This is a literary composition, and not a transcript of historical events and conversations", composed between the seventh and fifth centuries BC ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Job](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/job/0)), and Enter the Bible that "Most scholars agree that the Book of Job is not meant to be read as a historical account" ([Enter the Bible, Introductory Issues in Job](https://enterthebible.org/courses/job/lessons/introductory-issues-in-job/)).
- `6` [Enter the Bible, Introductory Issues in Job](https://enterthebible.org/courses/job/lessons/introductory-issues-in-job/) (Luther Seminary): **were Elihu's speeches and the happy ending part of the first book?** "Those who see Elihu as an original part of the book think that he helps prepare Job for his audience with God", but "The majority opinion is that the Elihu speeches were added to the book at a later time". In 1910 the *Catholic Encyclopedia* noted that "nearly all Protestant commentators regard them as a later insertion, while the great majority of Catholic investigators rightly defend them as belonging to the original work", and that the prologue and epilogue "are regarded by many as not parts of the original work", while itself holding the prologue "absolutely essential" ([Catholic Encyclopedia (1910), Job](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/08413a.htm)). Readers also weigh Elihu differently: the HarperCollins Bible Dictionary holds that he "merely repeats in other words the views of Job's three friends" ([Bible Odyssey, Elihu](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/dictionary/elihu/)), while Hayes notes that he "does hint, however, that not all suffering is punitive". On the ending, Hayes writes: "For some, the happy ending seems anticlimactic", but "in a way I think the ending is superbly fitting" ([Christine Hayes, Introduction to the Old Testament, Lecture 20, Open Yale Courses](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-20)).

## Tutorials and further reading

- `7` [Christine Hayes, Responses to Suffering and Evil: Lamentations and Wisdom Literature](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-20) (Open Yale Courses, Introduction to the Old Testament, lecture 20): a university lecture, with a transcript, whose last chapters read the prose prologue, the speech cycles and God's response in Job.
- `7` [Carol Newsom, Job](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/job/) (Bible Odyssey): a short introduction to "one of the best-known but also one of the most perplexing books of the Bible", the accuser, the trial Job wants with God, and the speeches from the whirlwind.
- `6` [James L. Crenshaw, Theodicy in the Hebrew Bible](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/theodicy-in-the-hebrew-bible/) (Bible Odyssey): the answers the Hebrew Bible gives to why God allows evil, in which "The best-known biblical theodicy is the book of Job".
- `6` [Enter the Bible, Theological Themes in Job](https://enterthebible.org/courses/job/lessons/theological-themes-in-job/) (Luther Seminary): the friends' answers, creation, the crisis of faith, God's presence and lament in Job.
- `6` [BibleProject, Job](https://bibleproject.com/videos/job/): an animated overview of the book, which concludes that "While we do not always know why we suffer, we can bring our pain and grief to God and trust his wisdom".
