# 2 Kings

2 Kings continues the story of the kings of Israel and Judah from where [1 Kings](1-kings.md) leaves it. It opens with the prophet Elijah taken up "by a whirlwind into heaven" and his disciple Elisha receiving his mantle ([2 Kings 2:1–15](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2KI02.htm#V1)), tells many stories of Elisha's deeds, and follows the two kingdoms to their end: Samaria falls to Assyria and Israel is carried into exile ([2 Kings 17:5–6](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2KI17.htm#V5)), and later Jerusalem falls to Babylon, the temple is burnt and the people of Judah are taken away ([2 Kings 25:8–11](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2KI25.htm#V8)). Between the two falls come the reforming kings Hezekiah and Josiah. The introduction to the New American Bible calls the story of Elisha's succession to Elijah "the structural center of the two books", and explains that the books judge each king "on theological grounds, with no allowance made for political or economic successes or failures" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Books of Kings](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/1kings/0)). The evangelical ESV Global Study Bible sums up their theme: "Israel suffers again and again because of its great sinfulness", "Yet there is still hope for the nation, because God's chosen family of kings has not come to an end" ([ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to 1–2 Kings](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-1-2-kings/)). The book ends with the captive king Jehoiachin released from prison in Babylon ([2 Kings 25:27–30](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2KI25.htm#V27)).

1 and 2 Kings were one book in the Hebrew Bible. Sefaria describes Kings II, *Melakhim Bet*, as "the second part of the fourth book of the Prophets" ([Sefaria, II Kings](https://www.sefaria.org/II_Kings)). Christian Bibles count it among the historical books, and in the Orthodox Church's listing, "which is generally that of the Septuagint", "1 and 2 Kings are called 3 and 4 Kings" ([Orthodox Church in America, The Orthodox Faith: Old Testament, History](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/doctrine-scripture/old-testament/history)).

The book names no author, though it refers its readers to older records such as "the book of the chronicles of the kings of Israel" ([2 Kings 1:18](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2KI01.htm#V18)). The Talmud holds that "Jeremiah wrote his own book, and the book of Kings, and Lamentations" ([Sefaria, Bava Batra 15a:2](https://www.sefaria.org/Bava_Batra.15a.2)). The ESV Global Study Bible holds that "The author or authors of these two books is unknown" ([ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to 1–2 Kings](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-1-2-kings/)). The USCCB introduction reads Kings as "the last part of a work commonly known as the Deuteronomistic History", which "underwent a complex process of editorial revision whose stages are disputed by scholars": "There may have been an edition sometime late in the reign of Josiah (640–609 B.C.), but in the form we have it the work comes from the time of the exile" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Books of Kings](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/1kings/0)). The debate is set out under open questions below.

## How the churches read it

Where the churches, or their interpreters, read the book differently, or where it has played a part in their history. Each entry starts with its source's score.

- `7` [Ambrose, On the Mysteries, 16–18](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/3405.htm) (New Advent): on **Naaman's washing in the Jordan** ([2 Kings 5:9–14](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2KI05.htm#V9)), the church father Ambrose of Milan, explaining the rites of baptism, reads the story as a figure of it: Naaman, once cleansed, "understood that it is not of the waters but of grace that a man is cleansed", and the captive girl stands for "the Church of God". The **Orthodox** Church reads Naaman's healing, with Elijah's and Elisha's crossings of the Jordan, among the Old Testament readings on the eve of Theophany, the feast of Christ's baptism ([Orthodox Church in America, Scripture Readings for 5 January 2026](https://www.oca.org/readings/daily/2026/01/05)), and the **Catholic** lectionary sets the story beside Jesus' words about Naaman in Luke 4 on Monday of the third week of Lent ([USCCB, Readings for Monday of the Third Week of Lent, 9 March 2026](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/readings/030926.cfm)). Matthew Henry, a **Nonconformist** pastor of a Presbyterian congregation in England ([Christian Classics Ethereal Library, Matthew Henry](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/henry)), reads the washing as "a sign of the cure, and a trial of his obedience", and the command as a picture of the gospel's call: "It is but, 'Believe, and be saved'—'Repent, and be pardoned'—'Wash, and be clean'" ([Matthew Henry, Commentary on 2 Kings 5](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/henry/mhc2.iiKi.vi.html)).
- `7` [Orthodox Church in America, Holy Glorious Prophet Elijah](https://www.oca.org/saints/lives/2026/07/20/102060-holy-glorious-prophet-elijah): on **Elijah taken up into heaven** ([2 Kings 2:11](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2KI02.htm#V11)) and the promise that Elijah will be sent "before the great and terrible day of the LORD" ([Malachi 4:5](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MAL04.htm#V5)). The **Orthodox** Church teaches that Elijah "was taken up alive into Heaven in a fiery chariot", and that "According to the Tradition of Holy Church, the Prophet Elijah will be the Forerunner of the Dread Second Coming of Christ". Jesus says of John the Baptist, "Elijah has come already" ([Matthew 17:10–13](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MAT17.htm#V10)), and the **Catholic** Catechism teaches that "John is 'Elijah (who) must come'" and that "John completes the cycle of prophets begun by Elijah" ([Catechism of the Catholic Church, 718–719](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P23.HTM)). In the **Reformed** tradition, Matthew Henry sees in Elijah's translation "a type and figure of the ascension of Christ and the opening of the kingdom of heaven to all believers" ([Matthew Henry, Commentary on 2 Kings 2](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/henry/mhc2.iiKi.iii.html)).
- `7` [Cyril of Jerusalem, Catechetical Lecture 18, 16](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/310118.htm) (New Advent): on **Elisha's bones** ([2 Kings 13:20–21](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2KI13.htm#V20)), the church father Cyril of Jerusalem teaches that the dead man was raised "to show, that even though the soul is not present, a virtue resides in the body of the saints", and Sirach, in Catholic and Orthodox Bibles, says that Elisha's "works were also marvellous in death" ([Sirach 48:13–14](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/SIR48.htm#V13)). The **Orthodox** Church recalls that when Julian the Apostate ordered the relics of Elisha burnt, "the holy relics were preserved by believers" ([Orthodox Church in America, Prophet Elisha](https://www.oca.org/saints/lives/2026/06/14/101718-prophet-elisha)), and the **Catholic** Roman Catechism, drawn up after the Council of Trent, draws from the passage the lesson that God works through "the sacred ashes, the bones, and other relics of the saints" ([Catholic Encyclopedia (1911), Relics](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/12734a.htm)). The **Church of England**'s Articles of Religion reject the "Worshipping, and Adoration, as well of Images as of Reliques" as "grounded upon no warranty of Scripture" ([Church of England, Articles of Religion, XXII](https://www.churchofengland.org/liturgy/book-of-common-prayer/articles-religion#XXII)).
- `6` [Matthew Henry, Commentary on 2 Kings 22](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/henry/mhc2.iiKi.xxiii.html) (Christian Classics Ethereal Library): on **the book of the law found under Josiah** ([2 Kings 22:8–13](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2KI22.htm#V8)), the **Protestant** commentator Matthew Henry links the story with the Reformation. He suggests that the book had been "carelessly mislaid and neglected, thrown by into a corner (as some throw their Bibles)", and draws the lesson that "It is a sign that God has mercy in store for a people when he magnifies his law among them", adding that the translation of the Scriptures into the people's languages "was the glory, strength, and joy of the Reformation". Most modern scholars link the book found with Deuteronomy (see [Deuteronomy](deuteronomy.md)).

## Open questions

Questions on which the churches or scholars disagree, for example about the book's authorship or the meaning of a passage. These guidelines don't pick a side.

- `7` [USCCB, Introduction to the Books of Kings](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/1kings/0) (New American Bible, Revised Edition): **who wrote the books of Kings, and when?** The Talmud names Jeremiah ([Sefaria, Bava Batra 15a:2](https://www.sefaria.org/Bava_Batra.15a.2)); the ESV Global Study Bible leaves the author unknown ([ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to 1–2 Kings](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-1-2-kings/)). The USCCB introduction holds that the books draw on "older sources", reach their present form "from the time of the exile", and belong to a Deuteronomistic History whose stages of editing "are disputed by scholars". Steven McKenzie explains that Martin Noth proposed in 1943 a single author writing in the exile; that Frank Moore Cross proposed a first edition with King Josiah as "its hero", updated after "Josiah's unexpected death" to explain the exile; and that Thomas Römer has reached "a sort of consensus" with a school of scribes whose scrolls "were revised under Josiah, in the exile, and then in the Persian period" ([Bible Odyssey, Steven L. McKenzie, Deuteronomistic History](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/deuteronomistic-history/)). Christine Hayes explains that the final form "must be post exilic", "but there are of course various layers within that larger work that we can't really date with precision" ([Christine Hayes, The Deuteronomistic History: Response to Catastrophe (1 and 2 Kings), Open Yale Courses](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-14)). The Orthodox Church in America notes that "It is mostly the case that the historical books of the Bible were written well after the events described in them actually took place" ([Orthodox Church in America, The Orthodox Faith: Old Testament, History](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/doctrine-scripture/old-testament/history)).
- `7` [Bible Odyssey, Shuichi Hasegawa, Jehu's Rebellion](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/jehus-rebellion/): **how far do records from outside the Bible confirm the book's account?** Several inscriptions name the same kings. The Mesha Stela of Moab, Erasmus Gass writes, is "an external source confirming the historicity of several details in the biblical account of an Omride king's reign" ([Bible Odyssey, Erasmus Gass, The Mesha Stela](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/the-mesha-stela/), [2 Kings 3:4–5](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2KI03.htm#V4)), and Sennacherib's annals describe his siege of Hezekiah's Jerusalem, saying that he shut Hezekiah up "like a bird in a cage" ([Christine Hayes, Open Yale Courses, lecture 14](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-14), [2 Kings 18:13–19:37](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2KI18.htm#V13)). Other records differ from the book: Hasegawa writes that the main lines of Jehu's coup "are thought to reflect a real event", but that research "has also cast doubt on the historical authenticity of aspects of the biblical account", since an Aramaic inscription from Tel Dan says that the Syrian king Hazael, not Jehu, killed Joram and Ahaziah ([2 Kings 9:24–28](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2KI09.htm#V24)). Joe Price notes that the Bible's writers judged Hezekiah "very favorably", whereas "many modern critical histories" conclude that Judah's borders shrank under him ([Bible Odyssey, Joe Price, Hezekiah](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/hezekiah/)). The USCCB introduction holds that the books "contain history", but that "historical accuracy is sometimes sacrificed to the demands of compelling characterization and dramatic tension" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Books of Kings](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/1kings/0)), while the ESV Global Study Bible dates the two kingdoms precisely, Israel from 931 to 722 BC and Judah to 586 BC ([ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to 1–2 Kings](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-1-2-kings/)).
- `6` [USCCB, 2 Kings, chapter 2](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/2kings/2) (New American Bible, Revised Edition): **how should the story of Elisha and the bears be read?** When youths mock Elisha at Bethel, "Go up, you baldy!", he curses them "in the LORD's name", and two bears maul forty-two of them ([2 Kings 2:23–25](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/2KI02.htm#V23)). The note in the New American Bible says that "This story probably was told to warn children of the importance of respect for prophets", and Christine Hayes counts the Elisha stories among "highly folkloristic" tales that were later set into the history of the kings ([Christine Hayes, Hebrew Prophecy: The Non-Literary Prophets, Open Yale Courses](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-15)). Matthew Henry holds instead that Elisha acted "not in personal revenge for the indignity done to himself, but as the mouth of divine justice", and that "The prophet must be justified, for he did it by divine impulse" ([Matthew Henry, Commentary on 2 Kings 2](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/henry/mhc2.iiKi.iii.html)). Sarah Cook reads the curse as God's way of showing that Elisha "enjoys just as much prestige and power as the prophet who came before him" ([Bible Odyssey, Sarah Cook, Elisha](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/elisha/)).

## Tutorials and further reading

- `7` [Christine Hayes, The Deuteronomistic History: Response to Catastrophe (1 and 2 Kings)](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-14) (Open Yale Courses, Introduction to the Old Testament, lecture 14): a university lecture, with a transcript, on the two covenants with Moses and with David, the division of the kingdom, the falls of Samaria and Jerusalem, and how the Deuteronomistic historian explained them.
