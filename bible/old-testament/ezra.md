# Ezra

Ezra tells how the Jews came home from exile in Babylon and rebuilt their life around the temple and the law. It opens with the decree of Cyrus, king of Persia, who lets the exiles return and rebuild "the LORD's house" in Jerusalem ([Ezra 1:1–4](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/EZR01.htm#V1)). The returning exiles set up the altar, lay the foundations of the temple, and finish it despite years of opposition ([Ezra 3:1–13](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/EZR03.htm#V1), [Ezra 6:13–22](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/EZR06.htm#V13)). Then Ezra, "a skilled scribe in the law of Moses", leads a second group from Babylon and confronts the marriages that many of the returned exiles had made with women of the surrounding peoples ([Ezra 7:6](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/EZR07.htm#V6), [Ezra 9:1–2](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/EZR09.htm#V1)). The introduction to the New American Bible divides the book into "The Return from Exile" (1:1–6:22) and "The Work of Ezra" (7:1–10:44), and calls Ezra and Nehemiah together "our most important literary source for the formation of the Jewish religious community in the province of Judah after the Babylonian exile" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Ezra](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/ezra/0)). Christine Hayes explains that Cyrus's policy of letting captives return and rebuild their temples is also told in the Cyrus Cylinder, and that the returning exiles came to what was now the Persian province of Yehud ([Christine Hayes, The Restoration: 1 and 2 Chronicles, Ezra and Nehemiah, Open Yale Courses](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-22)).

The book is a compilation. The Catholic Encyclopedia recognises in it "the personal memoirs of Esdras (vii, 27-ix, 15)", lists "very likely taken from public documents", and "Aramaic writings (iv, 7-vi, 18; vii, 12-26)" ([Ezra 7:27–9:15](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/EZR07.htm#V27), [Ezra 4:7–6:18](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/EZR04.htm#V7), [Ezra 7:12–26](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/EZR07.htm#V12)) ([Catholic Encyclopedia (1910), Esdras](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/05535a.htm)). The USCCB introduction notes "several dislocations of large sections" in Ezra and Nehemiah, "so that the chronological or logical sequence is disrupted": "The major instance is Ezra's public reading of the law in Neh 8", which is told on the [Nehemiah](nehemiah.md) page ([Nehemiah 8:1–12](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/NEH08.htm#V1)).

Ezra and Nehemiah were one book in the Hebrew Bible. Sefaria describes Ezra as "one of the books of the section in the Hebrew Bible called Writings", which "originally included the book of Nehemiah as well" ([Sefaria, Ezra](https://www.sefaria.org/Ezra)), and the USCCB introduction explains that "in ancient times the two books were put under the one name, Ezra". Their names in Christian Bibles are confusing. The Catholic Encyclopedia explains that "Esdras A of the Septuagint is III Esdras of St. Jerome, whereas the Greek Esdras B corresponds to I and II Esdras of the Vulgate, which were originally united into one book", and that Protestant writers, after the Geneva Bible, call I and II Esdras of the Vulgate "Ezra and Nehemiah". The Church of England's Articles of Religion, which follow the Latin names, list "The First Book of Esdras" and "The Second Book of Esdras", that is Ezra and Nehemiah, among the canonical books, and "The Third Book of Esdras" and "The Fourth Book of Esdras" among the books the church reads "for example of life and instruction of manners" ([Church of England, Articles of Religion, VI](https://www.churchofengland.org/liturgy/book-of-common-prayer/articles-religion#VI)). The Orthodox Church in America lists "1 and 2 Esdras" after Ezra, Nehemiah and Esther among the books that "are considered by the Orthodox as genuine parts of the Bible" ([Orthodox Church in America, The Orthodox Faith: Old Testament, History](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/doctrine-scripture/old-testament/history)); 1 Esdras is the Greek Esdras A, and both books are listed with the [deuterocanon](../README.md#deuterocanon). The book names no author: the Talmud says that "Ezra wrote his own book and the genealogy of the book of Chronicles until his period" ([Sefaria, Bava Batra 15a:2](https://www.sefaria.org/Bava_Batra.15a.2)), while modern scholars debate whether the book comes from the author of Chronicles; see open questions below.

## How the churches read it

Where the churches, or their interpreters, read the book differently, or where it has played a part in their history. Each entry starts with its source's score.

- `7` [Irenaeus, Against Heresies, III.21.2](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0103321.htm): on **Ezra and the Scriptures** ([Ezra 7:6](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/EZR07.htm#V6), [Ezra 7:10](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/EZR07.htm#V10)), the church father Irenaeus taught that when "the Scriptures had been corrupted" during the captivity, God "inspired Esdras the priest, of the tribe of Levi, to recast all the words of the former prophets, and to re-establish with the people the Mosaic legislation". The **Catholic** Encyclopedia treats this as tradition grown around a historical figure: "According to Jewish traditions, he restored from memory — an achievement little short of miraculous — all the books of the Old Testament", and "legend blended with history" ([Catholic Encyclopedia (1910), Esdras](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/05535a.htm)). The USCCB introduction adds that the Talmud "regards him as a second Moses", and that the apocryphal 2 Esdras "transforms him into a prophet and visionary" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Ezra](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/ezra/0)). The **Reformed** commentator Matthew Henry, a Presbyterian minister ([Christian Classics Ethereal Library, Matthew Henry](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/henry)), reports that "The Jews say that he collected and collated all the copies of the law he could find out", and so "made up the canon of the Old Testament"; "If he was raised up of God, and qualified and inclined to do this, all generations have reason to call him blessed" ([Matthew Henry, Commentary on the Whole Bible, Ezra 7](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/henry/mhc2.Ez.viii.html)).
- `7` [USCCB, Ezra, chapter 9](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/ezra/9) (New American Bible, Revised Edition, with notes): on **Ezra's prayer of confession** ([Ezra 9:5–15](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/EZR09.htm#V5)), the **Catholic** notes call it "a communal confession of sin, of a kind characteristic of the Second Temple period", like the prayers in [Nehemiah 9:6–37](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/NEH09.htm#V6) and [Daniel 9:4–19](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/DAN09.htm#V4). In the Catholic weekday lectionary, the start of the prayer, [Ezra 9:5–9](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/EZR09.htm#V5), is read at Mass on the Wednesday of the twenty-fifth week in Ordinary Time, the day after the reading on the dedication of the temple, as on 23 and 24 September 2025 ([USCCB, Readings for 23 September 2025](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/readings/092325.cfm), [USCCB, Readings for 24 September 2025](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/readings/092425.cfm)). Matthew Henry draws from it that "Holy shame is as necessary an ingredient in true and ingenuous repentance as holy sorrow", and that "The sins of others should be our shame, and we should blush for those who do not blush for themselves" ([Matthew Henry, Commentary on the Whole Bible, Ezra 9](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/henry/mhc2.Ez.x.html)).
- `6` [Matthew Henry, Commentary on the Whole Bible, Ezra 1](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/henry/mhc2.Ez.ii.html): on **Cyrus and God's providence** ([Ezra 1:1](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/EZR01.htm#V1)), the **Reformed** commentator Matthew Henry reads "The Lord stirred up the spirit of Cyrus" as a lesson that "The hearts of kings are in the hand of the Lord" and that "God governs the world by his influence on the spirits of men", and calls Cyrus "an eminent type of Christ the Sun of righteousness". The evangelical ESV Global Study Bible names as a key theme of the book that "The Lord works providentially by all means, especially through powerful rulers, to bring about his greater purposes" ([ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to Ezra](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-ezra/)). Augustine dated the end of the captivity, under Cyrus, to "the Completion of Seventy Years" ([Augustine, City of God, XVIII.26](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/120118.htm)), the time Jeremiah had foretold ([Jeremiah 25:11–12](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JER25.htm#V11)).

## Open questions

Questions on which the churches or scholars disagree, for example about the book's authorship or the meaning of a passage. These guidelines don't pick a side.

- `7` [USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Ezra](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/ezra/0) (New American Bible, Revised Edition): **who wrote Ezra, and is it part of one work with Chronicles?** The Talmud says that "Ezra wrote his own book and the genealogy of the book of Chronicles until his period" ([Sefaria, Bava Batra 15a:2](https://www.sefaria.org/Bava_Batra.15a.2)). The ESV Global Study Bible holds that "Ezra 7:27–9:15 clearly comes from Ezra's own hand", and that either Ezra combined it with other material or "a later historian may have collected all the portions"; "Many scholars believe that the same author wrote Ezra, Nehemiah, and 1–2 Chronicles" ([ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to Ezra](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-ezra/)). The USCCB introduction notes that Ezra and Nehemiah were once "generally considered to be the work of one and the same author known as 'the Chronicler'", but that "While some scholars still maintain unity of authorship, others prefer to speak of the influence of a 'Chronistic school'". The Catholic Encyclopedia reports that the Talmud's view, "still admitted by scholars of good standing", is "abandoned by several modern Biblical students", who date the book "later than 330 B.C." ([Catholic Encyclopedia (1910), Esdras](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/05535a.htm)); Christine Hayes's "best guess" is that the books were edited "in the late fifth century BCE, maybe close to the fourth century" ([Christine Hayes, The Restoration: 1 and 2 Chronicles, Ezra and Nehemiah, Open Yale Courses](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-22)).
- `7` [USCCB, Ezra, chapter 7](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/ezra/7) (New American Bible, Revised Edition, with notes): **when did Ezra come to Jerusalem, and did he come before Nehemiah?** Ezra arrives "in the seventh year of Artaxerxes the king" ([Ezra 7:7–8](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/EZR07.htm#V7)), but there were several Persian kings of that name. The notes of the New American Bible hold that "The context suggests the seventh year of Artaxerxes I, therefore, 458 B.C.", and that "The arguments often advanced for 398 B.C., the seventh year of Artaxerxes II, or less often for the thirty-seventh year of Artaxerxes I, that is, 428 B.C., are inconclusive"; the ESV Global Study Bible also dates his return "around 458 B.C." ([ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to Ezra](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-ezra/)). The Catholic Encyclopedia reports that scholars such as Van Hoonacker and Cheyne "maintain that Nehemias's mission preceded that of Esdras", so that Ezra's mission "might have taken place as late as 397 B.C." ([Catholic Encyclopedia (1910), Esdras](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/05535a.htm)). Christine Hayes concludes that "even the experts cannot agree at all on the sequence of events" ([Christine Hayes, The Restoration: 1 and 2 Chronicles, Ezra and Nehemiah, Open Yale Courses](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-22)).
- `7` [Bible Odyssey, Katherine E. Southwood, Intermarriage in Ezra-Nehemiah](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/intermarriage-in-ezra-nehemiah/): **how should the sending away of the foreign wives be read?** Ezra's prayer draws on the law's ban on marriage with the peoples of Canaan, given lest they "turn away your sons from following me" ([Ezra 9:11–12](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/EZR09.htm#V11), [Deuteronomy 7:3–4](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/DEU07.htm#V3)). The Catholic Encyclopedia of 1910 described the marriages as an "abuse", contracted "Regardless of the Law of Moses" ([Catholic Encyclopedia (1910), Esdras](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/05535a.htm)). Matthew Henry wrote that "Shechaniah's counsel, which he was then so clear in, will not hold now": such marriages "ought not to be made, but they are not null", for "Our rule, under the gospel" is Paul's instruction not to leave an unbelieving wife or husband ([Matthew Henry, Commentary on the Whole Bible, Ezra 10](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/henry/mhc2.Ez.xi.html), [1 Corinthians 7:12–14](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1CO07.htm#V12)). Other scholars read the measure as going beyond the law: Christine Hayes calls it "not in fact Pentateuchal law plainly read", "a great innovation on Ezra's part" that "was not universally accepted at all" ([Christine Hayes, The Restoration: 1 and 2 Chronicles, Ezra and Nehemiah, Open Yale Courses](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-22)); the notes of the New American Bible call it a policy "not mandated by any law known to us", which "did not succeed" ([USCCB, Ezra, chapter 10](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/ezra/10)); and Southwood explains it by "a growing ethnic awareness and an increased emphasis on purity", noting that "a lot of other texts in the Hebrew Bible do not object to intermarriage in the way that Ezra and Nehemiah do", such as Moses' marriage to a Cushite woman ([Numbers 12:1](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/NUM12.htm#V1)). Whether the book of Ruth was written as an answer to Ezra and Nehemiah is discussed on the [Ruth](ruth.md#open-questions) page.
