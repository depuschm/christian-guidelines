# Ecclesiastes

Ecclesiastes opens with "The words of the Preacher, the son of David, king in Jerusalem", and with its refrain: "Vanity of vanities, all is vanity" ([Ecclesiastes 1:1–2](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ECC01.htm#V1), [Ecclesiastes 12:8](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ECC12.htm#V8)). "The Hebrew name of this book and of its author, Qoheleth, is actually a title, and it perhaps means 'assembler' (of students, listeners) or 'collector' (of wisdom sayings)"; "Ecclesiastes" is "an approximate translation into Greek of this Hebrew word". The book "comprises an extended reflective essay employing autobiographical narrative, proverbs, parables, and allegories", and its theme word, *hebel*, "has the sense of 'emptiness, futility, absurdity'" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Ecclesiastes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/ecclesiastes/0)). Lisa M. Wolfe notes that the word, usually translated "vanity", is "found thirty-eight times throughout the book"; it is the name of Abel, and elsewhere in the Hebrew Bible it means "idols", "breath" and "worthless". She adds that the book "also includes a sevenfold call to 'seize the day' (*carpe diem*) by eating, drinking, and enjoying one's life and work" ([Lisa M. Wolfe, Vanity of Vanities, Bible Odyssey](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/vanity-of-vanities/)).

The Preacher searches for what lasts "under the sun": in wisdom, pleasure, great works and wealth. He finds that the wise and the fool die alike, that the righteous may perish while the wicked live long, and that no one can find out the work of God ([Ecclesiastes 2:14–16](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ECC02.htm#V14), [Ecclesiastes 7:15](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ECC07.htm#V15), [Ecclesiastes 8:17](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ECC08.htm#V17)). Yet he commends, again and again, eating, drinking and enjoying one's work as "the gift of God" ([Ecclesiastes 3:12–13](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ECC03.htm#V12)). An epilogue speaks of the Preacher in the third person and ends: "Fear God and keep his commandments; for this is the whole duty of man" ([Ecclesiastes 12:9–14](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ECC12.htm#V9)).

Tradition ascribed the book to Solomon, the son of David known for his wisdom ([1 Kings 4:29–34](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1KI04.htm#V29)), although the book never names him. The Babylonian Talmud names "Hezekiah and his colleagues" as the writers of Isaiah, Proverbs, the Song of Songs and Ecclesiastes ([Sefaria, Bava Batra 15a:2](https://www.sefaria.org/Bava_Batra.15a.2)). The ESV Global Study Bible notes that "Some interpreters have concluded that this was Solomon, while others think he was a role-playing writer later than Solomon" ([ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to Ecclesiastes](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-ecclesiastes/)). The USCCB introduction says that "Many would locate Ecclesiastes in the third century B.C." ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Ecclesiastes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/ecclesiastes/0)), and the Orthodox Church in America that the book "is traditionally ascribed to Solomon, the Preacher" while scholars "place the book in the third century before Christ" ([Orthodox Church in America, The Orthodox Faith: Old Testament, Wisdom](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/doctrine-scripture/old-testament/wisdom)). Who wrote it, and when, is one of the open questions below.

Ecclesiastes is in the Old Testament of every church ([which books each church reads](../README.md#which-books-each-church-reads)), among "the Wisdom books", which "include Job, Psalms, Proverbs, Ecclesiastes, and the Song of Solomon" ([Orthodox Church in America, The Orthodox Faith: Old Testament, Wisdom](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/doctrine-scripture/old-testament/wisdom)). It is also Jewish Scripture: *Kohelet* is "one of the five megillot (scrolls), part of the section of the Hebrew Bible called Writings, and is often read publicly on the holiday of Sukkot" ([Sefaria, Ecclesiastes](https://www.sefaria.org/Ecclesiastes)).

## How the churches read it

Where the churches, or their interpreters, read the book differently, or where it has played a part in their history. Each entry starts with its source's score.

- `7` [Catechism of the Catholic Church, 1005–1014](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P2I.HTM): **mortality, and the readings at Mass.** The **Catholic** Catechism quotes "Remember also your Creator in the days of your youth" in its teaching on death: "remembering our mortality helps us realize that we have only a limited time in which to bring our lives to fulfillment" (paragraph 1007; [Ecclesiastes 12:1](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ECC12.htm#V1), [Ecclesiastes 12:7](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ECC12.htm#V7)). At Mass on the Eighteenth Sunday in Ordinary Time in Year C, the Catholic Church reads "Vanity of vanities" with Jesus' warning against greed and his parable of the rich man who stores up his harvest in bigger barns ([USCCB, Readings for the Eighteenth Sunday in Ordinary Time, 3 August 2025](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/readings/080325.cfm); [Ecclesiastes 2:21–23](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ECC02.htm#V21), [Luke 12:13–21](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/LUK12.htm#V13); see [Luke](../new-testament/luke.md)), and on weekdays it reads the poem of the seasons ([USCCB, Readings for Friday of the Twenty-fifth Week in Ordinary Time, 25 September 2026](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/readings/092526.cfm); [Ecclesiastes 3:1–11](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ECC03.htm#V1)).
- `7` [Jerome, Prefaces: Preface to the Commentary on Ecclesiastes](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/3002.htm) (New Advent): **contempt of the world.** The church father Jerome, writing in 388, recalls: "I read Ecclesiastes to the saintly Blesilla, so that I might provoke her to the contempt of this earthly scene, and to count as nothing all that she saw in the world".
- `7` [Paul R. Hinlicky, Luther on Ecclesiastes: Nature in the Light of Grace](https://wordandworld.luthersem.edu/wp-content/uploads/pdfs/36-4_Winter_2023_Word_and_World/Luther%20on%20Ecclesiastes%20Nature%20in%20the%20Light%20of%20Grace.pdf) (Word & World, 2023): **enjoying God's gifts.** The reformer Martin Luther rejected the reading of Ecclesiastes as contempt of the world. Hinlicky shows how Luther, throughout his commentary on the book, singled out the "no less noxious" doctrine of "contempt of the world", when the "world" is taken as the "things which have been created and established by God", and quotes him: "it is foolish and wicked when many preachers inveigh against glory, power, social position, wealth, gold, fame, beauty, or women, thus openly condemning the creation of God". For Luther, Hinlicky writes, "The true 'vanity of vanities' resides not in the temporal gifts of God but in the inconstant and faithless heart of human creatures". Michael J. Chan notes that "Martin Luther stands out in his high regard for Ecclesiastes", and that Luther found "the theological heart of Ecclesiastes in the book's commendations" to enjoy life and work as God's gift ([Michael J. Chan, Futility and Gift: Luther on Ecclesiastes, Word & World](https://wordandworld.luthersem.edu/wp-content/uploads/pdfs/36-4_Winter_2023_Word_and_World/Futility%20and%20Gift%20Luther%20on%20Ecclesiastes.pdf); [Ecclesiastes 2:24–26](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ECC02.htm#V24), [Ecclesiastes 5:18–20](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ECC05.htm#V18)).
- `6` [Gregory of Nyssa, Homilies on Ecclesiastes, from Homily 4](https://earlychurchtexts.com/public/gregoryofnyss_ecclesiastes_slavery.htm) (Early Church Texts, translated by Andrew Maguire): **slavery.** Commenting on the Preacher's boast "I bought male servants and female servants" ([Ecclesiastes 2:7](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ECC02.htm#V7)), the church father Gregory of Nyssa spoke out against slavery itself: "You are condemning to slavery human beings whose nature is free and characterised by free will", and "For how many obols did you value the image of God?", for "God would not make a slave of humankind" ([Genesis 1:26](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/GEN01.htm#V26)). The churches' teaching on slavery is set out on the [Philemon](../new-testament/philemon.md) page.
- `6` [Matthew Henry, Commentary on the Whole Bible, Ecclesiastes: Introduction](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/henry/mhc3.Ec.i.html) (Christian Classics Ethereal Library): **Solomon's recantation.** The **Reformed** commentator Matthew Henry read the book as Solomon's own, written when "he had grown old" and "was, by the grace of God, recovered from his backslidings" ([1 Kings 11:1–13](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1KI11.htm#V1); see [1 Kings](1-kings.md)): "it is a recantation-sermon, in which the preacher sadly laments his own folly and mistake, in promising himself satisfaction in the things of this world".
- `6` [Vanderbilt Divinity Library, Revised Common Lectionary: Sunday citations](https://lectionary.library.vanderbilt.edu/sunday-citations/): **Ecclesiastes in worship.** The Revised Common Lectionary reads the poem of the seasons on New Year's Day every year, and "Vanity of vanities" in the season after Pentecost in Year C ([Ecclesiastes 3:1–13](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ECC03.htm#V1), [Ecclesiastes 1:2](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ECC01.htm#V2), [Ecclesiastes 1:12–14](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ECC01.htm#V12), [Ecclesiastes 2:18–23](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ECC02.htm#V18)). The **United Reformed Church** lists the poem among its readings for funerals ([United Reformed Church, Readings for Funeral Services](https://urc.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/Worship_book_-2026-Readings-for-Funeral.pdf)). The **Orthodox** Church in America notes that of Ecclesiastes, Sirach and the Wisdom of Solomon, "only the Wisdom of Solomon, which is considered to be the last of them written, is read liturgically in the Orthodox Church" ([Orthodox Church in America, The Orthodox Faith: Old Testament, Wisdom](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/doctrine-scripture/old-testament/wisdom); see the [deuterocanon](../README.md#deuterocanon)).

## Open questions

Questions on which the churches or scholars disagree, for example about the book's authorship or the meaning of a passage. These guidelines don't pick a side.

- `7` [Christine Hayes, Biblical Poetry: Psalms and Song of Songs](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-21) (Open Yale Courses, Introduction to the Old Testament, lecture 21): **is the Preacher a sceptic or a believer, and how does the epilogue fit?** Hayes calls the book "a second attack on the optimism and piety of conventional religion", and holds that the closing call to "Fear God and keep his commandments" is "very likely (in my view; people will disagree)" from "a later hand, which was disturbed by the theme of Qohelet's preaching" ([Ecclesiastes 12:13–14](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ECC12.htm#V13)). The USCCB introduction finds "An almost unrelenting skepticism", "somewhat tempered by the resigned conclusions to rejoice in whatever gifts God may give" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Ecclesiastes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/ecclesiastes/0)). The ESV Global Study Bible holds that "The theme of Ecclesiastes is the necessity of fearing God in this fallen, confusing world", and that "the faithful do not despair but cling to God, even when they cannot see what God is doing" ([ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to Ecclesiastes](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-ecclesiastes/)). Wolfe leaves the choice to the reader: whether the call to "seize the day" answers the book's "absurdities", the answer "rests in the mind of the reader" ([Lisa M. Wolfe, Vanity of Vanities, Bible Odyssey](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/vanity-of-vanities/)).
- `7` [Michael J. Chan, Futility and Gift: Luther on Ecclesiastes](https://wordandworld.luthersem.edu/wp-content/uploads/pdfs/36-4_Winter_2023_Word_and_World/Futility%20and%20Gift%20Luther%20on%20Ecclesiastes.pdf) (Word & World, 2023): **what does the book say about death and what comes after?** Chan notes that some readers "are bothered by the book's vision of the afterlife": "death for Ecclesiastes is a dank, dreary place where all are destined to go—human and beast alike" ([Ecclesiastes 3:19–21](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ECC03.htm#V19), [Ecclesiastes 9:5–6](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ECC09.htm#V5)). Christine Hayes holds that the Preacher "rejects the idea of any life after death" ([Christine Hayes, Biblical Poetry: Psalms and Song of Songs, Open Yale Courses](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-21)). The ESV Global Study Bible holds that the Preacher "speaks as one who eagerly awaits the resurrection age" ([ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to Ecclesiastes](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-ecclesiastes/); [Romans 8:20–23](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ROM08.htm#V20)), and the Catholic Catechism quotes "the spirit returns to God who gave it" in its teaching on death ([Catechism of the Catholic Church, 1005–1014](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P2I.HTM); [Ecclesiastes 12:7](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ECC12.htm#V7)).
- `7` [Sefaria, Bava Batra 15a:2](https://www.sefaria.org/Bava_Batra.15a.2): **who wrote the book, and when?** The Talmud names "Hezekiah and his colleagues" as its writers, and Christian tradition ascribed it to Solomon; Matthew Henry held that Solomon wrote it in old age ([Matthew Henry, Commentary on the Whole Bible, Ecclesiastes: Introduction](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/henry/mhc3.Ec.i.html)). The ESV Global Study Bible leaves open whether the Preacher "was Solomon" or "a role-playing writer later than Solomon" ([ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to Ecclesiastes](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-ecclesiastes/)). Christine Hayes holds that the attribution to Solomon "is fictive", since "there are linguistic and literary features that suggest a later, probably or perhaps, a fourth-century date" ([Christine Hayes, Biblical Poetry: Psalms and Song of Songs, Open Yale Courses](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-21)); the USCCB introduction notes that "Many would locate Ecclesiastes in the third century B.C." ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Ecclesiastes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/ecclesiastes/0)).
- `7` [Sefaria, Mishnah Yadayim 3:5](https://www.sefaria.org/Mishnah_Yadayim.3.5): **how did a book with so many tensions come into the Bible?** The Mishnah records a debate among the rabbis over whether Ecclesiastes, like "All the Holy Scriptures", "defile[s] the hands": "Rabbi Judah says: the Song of Songs defiles the hands, but there is a dispute about Kohelet. Rabbi Yose says: Kohelet does not defile the hands". The Talmud reports that "The Sages sought to suppress the book of Ecclesiastes and declare it apocryphal because its statements contradict each other", and kept it "Because its beginning consists of matters of Torah and its end consists of matters of Torah"; one of its examples is what the book says about laughter ([Sefaria, Shabbat 30b:3](https://www.sefaria.org/Shabbat.30b.3); [Ecclesiastes 2:2](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ECC02.htm#V2), [Ecclesiastes 7:3](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ECC07.htm#V3)). Christine Hayes reads the epilogue as a sign of this controversy ([Christine Hayes, Biblical Poetry: Psalms and Song of Songs, Open Yale Courses](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-21)); Michael J. Chan notes that "Many modern Christians" have "an uneasy relationship with the book", and that it is common to hear that "Ecclesiastes is what life looks like apart from Jesus" ([Michael J. Chan, Futility and Gift: Luther on Ecclesiastes, Word & World](https://wordandworld.luthersem.edu/wp-content/uploads/pdfs/36-4_Winter_2023_Word_and_World/Futility%20and%20Gift%20Luther%20on%20Ecclesiastes.pdf)).

## Tutorials and further reading

- `7` [Christine Hayes, Biblical Poetry: Psalms and Song of Songs](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-21) (Open Yale Courses, Introduction to the Old Testament, lecture 21): a university lecture, with a transcript, whose third chapter reads Ecclesiastes as a challenge to "the principle of divine providence or distributive justice", and finds in it the advice to "Accept the reality of death and then enjoy what you can in the short time you have".
- `7` [Lisa M. Wolfe, Vanity of Vanities](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/vanity-of-vanities/) (Bible Odyssey): what the Hebrew word *hevel* means, how translations render it, and how it sits beside the book's call to "seize the day".
- `6` [BibleProject, Ecclesiastes](https://bibleproject.com/videos/ecclesiastes/): an animated overview that reads the book as "the author's response to Proverbs", in which "it's all hevel," meaning "temporary or fleeting".
