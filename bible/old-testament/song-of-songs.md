# Song of Songs

The Song of Songs is "an exquisite collection of love lyrics, arranged to tell a dramatic tale of mutual desire and courtship". The introduction to the New American Bible calls it "an inspired portrayal of ideal human love, a resounding affirmation of the goodness of human sexuality that is applicable to the sacredness and the depth of married union" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Song of Songs](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/songofsongs/0)). Its title is a Hebrew superlative: "the X of Xs" means the highest of its kind, as "king of kings" means "the highest king", so the Song of Songs is the greatest of songs ([USCCB, Song of Songs, chapter 1, with notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/songofsongs/1); [Song of Songs 1:1](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/SNG01.htm#V1)). It is also called the Canticle of Canticles and, in many English Bibles, the WEBBE among them, the Song of Solomon.

The Song is "a dialogue between two lovers that is dominated by the voice of a woman", and it mentions no covenant, temple or religious practice, "perhaps not even a mention of God (depending on how you translate Song 8:6)" ([Martien A. Halvorson-Taylor, Is the Song of Songs Sacred or Secular?, Bible Odyssey](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/is-the-song-of-songs-sacred-or-secular-2/)). The Hebrew text doesn't mark who is speaking. The WEBBE, like the New American Bible, adds headings that name the speakers; the New American Bible explains that its marks follow "an early Christian scribal practice, attested in some Septuagint manuscripts" ([USCCB, Song of Songs, chapter 1, with notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/songofsongs/1)).

The title says the song "is Solomon's", and the Talmud says that "Hezekiah and his colleagues wrote" Isaiah, Proverbs, the Song of Songs and Ecclesiastes ([Sefaria, Bava Batra 15a](https://www.sefaria.org/Bava_Batra.15a.2)). Who wrote it, and when, is one of the open questions below. In Christian Bibles it is counted among the wisdom books, with Job, Psalms, Proverbs and Ecclesiastes ([Orthodox Church in America, The Orthodox Faith: Old Testament, Wisdom](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/doctrine-scripture/old-testament/wisdom)). It is also Jewish Scripture, *Shir Hashirim*, "one of the five megillot (scrolls), part of the section of the Hebrew Bible called Writings"; it "is traditionally understood as a metaphor for the loving relationship between God and the people of Israel, and is often read publicly on Passover or on Shabbat evening" ([Sefaria, Song of Songs](https://www.sefaria.org/Song_of_Songs)). When some rabbis disputed whether the book was Scripture, Rabbi Akiva answered that "all the writings are holy but the Song of Songs is the holy of holies" ([Sefaria, Mishnah Yadayim 3:5](https://www.sefaria.org/Mishnah_Yadayim.3.5)).

The outline follows, with small changes, the HarperCollins Bible Dictionary ([Bible Odyssey, Song of Solomon, The](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/dictionary/song-of-solomon-the/)). ## How the churches read it

Where the churches, or their interpreters, read the book differently, or where it has played a part in their history. Each entry starts with its source's score.

- `8` [Benedict XVI, Deus caritas est](https://www.vatican.va/content/benedict-xvi/en/encyclicals/documents/hf_ben-xvi_enc_20051225_deus-caritas-est.html) (2005), 6 and 10: **human love and love for God.** In his encyclical on love, Pope Benedict XVI writes that "According to the interpretation generally held today, the poems contained in this book were originally love-songs, perhaps intended for a Jewish wedding feast and meant to exalt conjugal love". He finds in the Song's two Hebrew words for love, *dodim* and *ahabà*, a path from a "searching" love to "a love which involves a real discovery of the other". He adds that "the reception of the Song of Songs in the canon of sacred Scripture was soon explained by the idea that these love songs ultimately describe God's relation to man and man's relation to God", so that the book "became, both in Christian and Jewish literature, a source of mystical knowledge and experience". The **Catholic** introduction to the New American Bible likewise reads the Song as an affirmation of human love, and notes that "Christian writers have interpreted the Song in terms of the union between Christ and the Church and of the union between Christ and the individual soul, particularly in the writings of Origen and St. Bernard" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Song of Songs](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/songofsongs/0)).
- `7` [Benedict XVI, General Audience of 2 May 2007: Origen of Alexandria (2)](https://www.vatican.va/content/benedict-xvi/en/audiences/2007/documents/hf_ben-xvi_aud_20070502.html): **Christ and the church, Christ and the soul.** Anselm C. Hagedorn writes that "Christian interpretation since Hippolytus (ca. 170-235 C.E.) and Origen (ca. 185-254 C.E.) follows in Jewish footsteps and reads the Song allegorically", in two strands: "an ecclesiological reading of the Song where the poem describes the love relationship between Christ and his church", and a reading "in which the poem describes the relationship of an individual soul to Christ as bridegroom", which "reaches its climax in the bridal mysticism of Bernard of Clairvaux" ([Anselm C. Hagedorn, Jewish and Christian Interpretation of the Song of Songs, Bible Odyssey](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/jewish-and-christian-interpretation-of-the-song-of-songs/)). Pope Benedict XVI quotes Origen's *Homilies on the Song of Songs*: "I have often felt - God is my witness - that the Bridegroom came to me in the most exalted way. Then he suddenly left, and I was unable to find what I was seeking", words that echo the woman's searches ([Song of Songs 3:1–4](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/SNG03.htm#V1), [Song of Songs 5:6](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/SNG05.htm#V6)). He counts Bernard's "celebrated Sermons on the Song of Songs" among the abbot's "most famous works" ([Benedict XVI, General Audience of 21 October 2009: Saint Bernard of Clairvaux](https://www.vatican.va/content/benedict-xvi/en/audiences/2009/documents/hf_ben-xvi_aud_20091021.html)). The Carmelite John of the Cross's poem *Spiritual Canticle*, on the soul's search for Christ, "loosely follows the narrative of Solomon's Song of Songs and can serve as an allegorical reading thereof in light of the Gospel" ([Christian Classics Ethereal Library, John of the Cross, Spiritual Canticle of the Soul and the Bridegroom Christ](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/john_cross/canticle.html)).
- `7` [USCCB, Readings for Monday of the Fourth Week of Advent, 21 December 2026](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/readings/122126.cfm): **the Song in Catholic worship.** On 21 December, the **Catholic** lectionary gives Song of Songs 2:8–14, "Arise, my beloved, my dove, my beautiful one, and come!", as one of two possible first readings, with the Gospel of Mary's visit to Elizabeth ([Song of Songs 2:8–14](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/SNG02.htm#V8), [Luke 1:39–45](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/LUK01.htm#V39)). On the feast of Saint Mary Magdalene, 22 July, it reads the woman's night search, "I sought him whom my heart loves", with the Gospel of Mary Magdalene seeking Jesus at the tomb ([USCCB, Readings for the Feast of Saint Mary Magdalene, 22 July 2026](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/readings/072226.cfm); [John 20:1–2](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JHN20.htm#V1), [John 20:11–18](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JHN20.htm#V11)). The Catholic Encyclopedia of 1908 notes that "In the Breviary and Missal the Church has repeatedly applied the song to the Mother of God" ([New Advent, Catholic Encyclopedia (1908), Canticle of Canticles](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/03302a.htm)).
- `7` [Church of England, Common Worship: Readings and Psalms for Marriage](https://www.churchofengland.org/liturgy/common-worship/marriage/readings-and-psalms-marriage): **the Song at weddings.** The **Church of England** lists "Song of Solomon 2.10-13; 8.6,7" among the readings for a marriage service ([Song of Songs 2:10–13](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/SNG02.htm#V10), [Song of Songs 8:6–7](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/SNG08.htm#V6)). The **Catholic** Church offers "Song of Songs 2:8-10, 14, 16a; 8:6-7a" among the Old Testament readings at a wedding, and the bishops' commentary says that in the book "The gift of sexuality is affirmed and portrayed without apology" ([USCCB, For Your Marriage, Old Testament Readings](https://www.foryourmarriage.org/old-testament-readings/)). In the **Russian Orthodox** Church, lines from the Song are sung at weddings (see the next entry).
- `6` [Orthodox Church in America, The Orthodox Faith: Old Testament, Wisdom](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/doctrine-scripture/old-testament/wisdom): **the Orthodox reading.** "In Orthodox Church Tradition it is interpreted as a mystical love story between man's soul and God. Christian saints of East and West, such as Gregory of Nyssa and Bernard of Clairvaux, have given such a meaning to the book which is in line with the biblical tradition of viewing the interrelationship of God and His People as that of conjugal love." The book "is never read in the liturgical services of the Orthodox Church, although certain lines from it are traditionally sung in the Russian Orthodox Church when the bride approaches her bridegroom in the church before the celebration of their marriage".
- `6` [Matthew Henry, Commentary on the Whole Bible, Song of Solomon: Introduction](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/henry/mhc3.Song.i.html) (Christian Classics Ethereal Library): **Protestant readings.** The **Reformed** commentator Matthew Henry admits that "here is not the name of God in it; it is never quoted in the New Testament", but holds that "It is an allegory, the letter of which kills those who rest in that and look no further": a nuptial song in which "Christ and the church in general, Christ and particular believers, are here discoursing", and to which "The best key" is Psalm 45 ([Psalm 45:1–17](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/PSA045.htm#V1)). Hagedorn notes that "Like their Catholic counterparts, most Protestant theologians continue to read the Song of Songs allegorically", while Martin Luther "favored a decidedly political interpretation", in which Solomon gives thanks to God "for his divinely established and confirmed kingdom and government" ([Anselm C. Hagedorn, Jewish and Christian Interpretation of the Song of Songs, Bible Odyssey](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/jewish-and-christian-interpretation-of-the-song-of-songs/)). The evangelical ESV Global Study Bible reads it instead as "beautiful poetry expressing romantic love between a young man and a young woman in ancient Israel", and adds that marriage "is a fitting image for God's relationship with his people" ([ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to The Song of Solomon](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-the-song-of-solomon/)).

## Open questions

Questions on which the churches or scholars disagree. These guidelines don't pick a side.

- `7` [Martien A. Halvorson-Taylor, Is the Song of Songs Sacred or Secular?](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/is-the-song-of-songs-sacred-or-secular-2/) (Bible Odyssey): **is the Song a love poem, an allegory, or both?** Anselm C. Hagedorn notes "the prevalence of the allegorical interpretation up to the nineteenth century" among Jewish and Christian readers. He holds that it "was originally a carefully crafted anthology of secular love poems", which "Jewish and Christian tradition transformed" into "a sublime document of religious belief"; he names Theodore of Mopsuestia, who "interprets Song of Songs as a wedding song", among the "dissenting voices" ([Anselm C. Hagedorn, Jewish and Christian Interpretation of the Song of Songs, Bible Odyssey](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/jewish-and-christian-interpretation-of-the-song-of-songs/)). The Catholic Encyclopedia of 1908 records that "At the Œcumenical Council of Constantinople (553), Theodore's view was rejected as heretic", and describes a "typical" reading that keeps the literal sense and gives the song "a second and higher sense" ([New Advent, Catholic Encyclopedia (1908), Canticle of Canticles](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/03302a.htm)). Matthew Henry warns that its letter "kills those who rest in that and look no further" ([Matthew Henry, Commentary on the Whole Bible, Song of Solomon: Introduction](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/henry/mhc3.Song.i.html)). Halvorson-Taylor's own answer to whether it is "sacred scripture or a secular love poem" is "both", and BibleProject says that it "works on two levels" ([BibleProject, Song of Songs](https://bibleproject.com/videos/song-songs/)).
- `7` [USCCB, Song of Songs, chapter 8, with notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/songofsongs/8): **does the Song name God?** In 8:6 love's flames are, in the WEBBE, "a very flame of the LORD"; its footnote gives the Hebrew as *Yah*, the short form of God's name ([Song of Songs 8:6](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/SNG08.htm#V6)). The NRSVUE translates "a raging flame" ([Bible Gateway, Song of Songs 1–8 (NRSVUE)](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Song%20of%20Songs%201-8&version=NRSVUE)). The New American Bible's note says that "the short form (-Yah) of the divine name Yhwh found here may associate love with the Lord, or it may be acting as a superlative—i.e., god-sized flames". André LaCocque calls it "the sole allusion to Yahweh in the poem", but holds that the ending "is a superlative form evoking Yahweh's supreme nature, rather than being an actual reference to the deity" ([André LaCocque, Love in the Song of Songs, Bible Odyssey](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/love-in-the-song-of-songs/)). Elsie R. Stern counts the Song with Esther as the two books of the Hebrew Bible "that never mention God" ([Elsie R. Stern, Where Is God in Esther?, Bible Odyssey](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/where-is-god-in-esther/)).
- `7` [USCCB, Introduction to the Song of Songs](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/songofsongs/0): **who wrote it, and when?** The title names Solomon, but the New American Bible's note says that "The ascription of authorship to Solomon is traditional. The heading may also mean 'for Solomon' or 'about Solomon'" ([USCCB, Song of Songs, chapter 1, with notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/songofsongs/1)), and the Talmud gives the book to "Hezekiah and his colleagues" ([Sefaria, Bava Batra 15a](https://www.sefaria.org/Bava_Batra.15a.2)). The New American Bible's introduction holds that its language and style "suggest a time after the end of the Babylonian exile (538 B.C.) when an unknown poet collected extant love poems, perhaps composing new material", and notes that "Some scholars argue the possibility of female authorship for at least portions of the Song". The ESV Global Study Bible thinks that Solomon "probably was not the writer himself", but that "the book was probably composed during Solomon's time, perhaps under his oversight, between c. 960 and 931 B.C." ([ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to The Song of Solomon](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-the-song-of-solomon/)).
- `7` [Anselm C. Hagedorn, Jewish and Christian Interpretation of the Song of Songs](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/jewish-and-christian-interpretation-of-the-song-of-songs/) (Bible Odyssey): **one poem or a collection?** Hagedorn calls the Song "a carefully crafted anthology", and the New American Bible's introduction "an exquisite collection of love lyrics, arranged to tell a dramatic tale", whose structure "is difficult to analyze" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Song of Songs](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/songofsongs/0)). The ESV Global Study Bible reads it as one "series of exchanges, mostly between the shepherdess and the shepherd", held together by the refrain to the daughters of Jerusalem ([ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to The Song of Solomon](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-the-song-of-solomon/)).
- `6` [ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to The Song of Solomon](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-the-song-of-solomon/): **are the lovers married?** The ESV Global Study Bible reads the Song as the story of a couple "looking forward to their marriage and the pleasure it will bring", with "the marriage bed, which seems to begin in 8:5". The man calls the woman "my bride", and the Song speaks of Solomon's wedding day ([Song of Songs 3:11](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/SNG03.htm#V11), [Song of Songs 4:8–12](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/SNG04.htm#V8)). Martien A. Halvorson-Taylor, by contrast, writes that "the Song rejoices in erotic love and sex outside of marriage" ([Martien A. Halvorson-Taylor, Is the Song of Songs Sacred or Secular?, Bible Odyssey](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/is-the-song-of-songs-sacred-or-secular-2/)). The churches that read it at weddings apply it to marriage (see above).
