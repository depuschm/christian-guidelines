# Psalms

The book of Psalms is a collection of 150 songs and prayers. Sefaria describes Psalms, *Tehillim*, as "the first book of the section in the Hebrew Bible called Writings" and "an anthology of 150 poems attributed to King David and to others", which "includes songs of praise to God, laments of communal or personal tragedy, and expressions of anger, despair, hope, and gratitude" ([Sefaria, Psalms](https://www.sefaria.org/Psalms)). The Catechism of the Catholic Church calls the Psalter "the masterwork of prayer in the Old Testament", and finds its traditional title, "The Praises", "so fitting" ([Catechism of the Catholic Church, 2585–2589](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P91.HTM)). The USCCB introduction holds that "the majority were composed originally precisely for liturgical worship", and that they follow distinct literary forms: hymns of praise, psalms of thanksgiving, royal psalms, wisdom and "torah" psalms, and laments, for "There are more Psalms of lament than of any other type" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Psalms](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/psalms/0)). The ESV Global Study Bible likewise counts the laments "the largest by far, including up to a third of all Psalms" ([ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to The Psalms](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-the-psalms/)). The psalms are gathered into five books, each closing with a blessing of God [Psalm 41:13](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/PSA041.htm#V13), [Psalm 72:18–19](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/PSA072.htm#V18), [Psalm 89:52](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/PSA089.htm#V52), [Psalm 106:48](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/PSA106.htm#V48), a division the ESV Global Study Bible suggests was made "perhaps in imitation of the five books of the Pentateuch"; the whole book ends: "Let everything that has breath praise the LORD!" [Psalm 150:6](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/PSA150.htm#V6).

Many psalms have a heading that names a person: most often David, but also Asaph, the sons of Korah, Solomon and Moses [Psalm 42](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/PSA042.htm), [Psalm 72](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/PSA072.htm), [Psalm 90](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/PSA090.htm). The USCCB introduction holds that "The superscriptions derive from pre-Christian Jewish tradition", and that "Seventy-three Psalms are attributed to David, but there is no sure way of dating any Psalm. Some are preexilic (before 587), and others are postexilic (after 539)". The Talmud teaches that "David wrote the book of Psalms by means of ten elders of previous generations", including psalms of Adam, Melchizedek, Abraham and Moses ([Sefaria, Bava Batra 14b:12](https://www.sefaria.org/Bava_Batra.14b.12)). Christine Hayes explains that "tradition attributes the entire book of Psalms to King David", but that "The superscriptions, however, are in many cases late additions", and calls the book "an anthology of religious expressions deriving from many centuries of Israel's history" ([Christine Hayes, Biblical Poetry: Psalms and Song of Songs, Open Yale Courses](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-21)). The question is set out under open questions below.

The psalms are numbered in two ways. "The Hebrew Psalter numbers 150 songs", the USCCB introduction explains, and "the numbering in the Greek Psalter (which was followed by the Latin Vulgate) is usually one digit behind the Hebrew" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Psalms](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/psalms/0)). This page, like the WEBBE, follows the Hebrew numbering, so that its Psalm 51 is Psalm 50 in the Greek numbering. "The Orthodox Church follows the Septuagint version of the psalter and for this reason the numbers and not seldom the texts of certain psalms are different in Orthodox service books" ([Orthodox Church in America, The Orthodox Faith: Old Testament, Psalms](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/doctrine-scripture/old-testament/psalms)); Walter Drum's article in the Catholic Encyclopedia sets out the two numberings side by side ([Walter Drum, Psalms, Catholic Encyclopedia (1911)](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/12533a.htm)). Greek Psalters also add a Psalm 151, which the WEBBE prints with the heading "This Psalm is a genuine one of David, though extra" [Psalm 151:1](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/PS201.htm#V1). James A. Sanders notes that it "is not in Jewish, Roman Catholic, or Protestant Bibles, though it is in some Eastern Orthodox Bibles", and that its Hebrew originals were found among the Dead Sea Scrolls ([Bible Odyssey, James A. Sanders, Psalm 151](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/psalm-151/)); see the [deuterocanon](../README.md#deuterocanon).

In Jewish worship, "Psalms are prevalent throughout Jewish liturgy and commonly recited as an independent form of prayer" ([Sefaria, Psalms](https://www.sefaria.org/Psalms)). The Hallel, "comprised of six psalms (113-118)", is "a prayer of thanksgiving added to the morning service on festive Jewish holidays", and is recited on Passover, Shavuot, Sukkot and Hanukkah ([My Jewish Learning, What Is Hallel?](https://www.myjewishlearning.com/article/hallel/)). The New Testament quotes the psalms more than almost any other book, and Jesus speaks of "all things which are written in the law of Moses, the prophets, and the psalms concerning me" [Luke 24:44](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/LUK24.htm#V44).

## How the churches read it

Where the churches, or their interpreters, read the book differently, or where it has played a part in their history. Each entry starts with its source's score. The churches' shared teaching on praying the psalms, laments included, is in [Prayer](../../general/prayer.md).

- `7` [General Instruction of the Liturgy of the Hours, 100–109 and 126](https://www.ewtn.com/catholicism/library/general-instruction-on-the-liturgy-of-the-hours-28969) (1971): in the **Catholic Church**, the psalms are the core of the daily prayer of the Liturgy of the Hours: "In the liturgy of the hours the Church in large measure prays through the magnificent songs that the Old Testament authors composed under the inspiration of the Holy Spirit", and "The psalms are distributed over a four-week cycle in such a way that very few psalms are omitted". At Mass, "After the first reading comes the responsorial Psalm, which is an integral part of the Liturgy of the Word" ([General Instruction of the Roman Missal, 61](https://www.vatican.va/roman_curia/congregations/ccdds/documents/rc_con_ccdds_doc_20030317_ordinamento-messale_en.html)). The Catechism teaches that "The Psalter is the book in which the Word of God becomes man's prayer", and that, "Prayed by Christ and fulfilled in him, the Psalms remain essential to the prayer of the Church" ([Catechism of the Catholic Church, 2586–2587](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P91.HTM)).
- `7` [Orthodox Church in America, The Orthodox Faith: Old Testament, Psalms](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/doctrine-scripture/old-testament/psalms): in the **Orthodox Church**, "the entire psalter is divided into twenty sections and is chanted each week in those monasteries and churches which perform the entire liturgical office", and "Various psalms and verses of psalms are used in all liturgical services". Each section, or kathisma, is "a twentieth part of the Psalter, that is, a section conveniently readable in one sitting or session" ([Orthodox Church in America, The Division of the Psalter into Kathismas](https://www.oca.org/liturgics/outlines/the-division-of-the-psalter-into-kathismas)). "The Vespers service in the Church always begins with the chanting of the evening psalm", Psalm 104 (103 in the Greek numbering), "which glorifies God's creation of the world" ([Orthodox Church in America, The Orthodox Faith: Vespers](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/worship/the-daily-cycles-of-prayer/vespers), [Psalm 104:19–24](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/PSA104.htm#V19)), and at Lenten Matins "the reader continues with Psalm 50", the Psalm 51 of the Hebrew numbering ([Orthodox Church in America, The Lenten Office of Daily Matins](https://www.oca.org/files/PDF/Music/Rubrics/Horologion/lenten-office-daily-matins.pdf)).
- `7` [Book of Common Prayer, The Order how the Psalter is appointed to be read](https://www.churchofengland.org/liturgy/book-of-common-prayer/order-how-psalter-appointed-be-read) (Church of England): in the **Anglican** daily office, "The Psalter shall be read through once every Month, as it is there appointed, both for Morning and Evening Prayer", Psalm 119 being divided into twenty-two portions.
- `7` [Augustine, Exposition on Psalm 86](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/1801086.htm): on **the psalms and Christ**, the New Testament reads several psalms of Jesus: he quotes Psalm 110 of himself, cries the first words of Psalm 22 on the cross, and his apostles pray Psalm 2 against the rulers "against the Lord, and against his Christ" [Matthew 22:43–45](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MAT22.htm#V43), [Matthew 27:46](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MAT27.htm#V46), [Acts 4:25–26](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ACT04.htm#V25). St Augustine taught that Christ "both prays for us, and prays in us, and is prayed to by us. He prays for us, as our Priest; He prays in us, as our Head; He is prayed to by us, as our God. Let us therefore recognise in Him our words, and His words in us." **Orthodox** teaching holds that "all of the psalms are understood as having their deepest and most genuine spiritual meaning in terms of Christ and His mission of eternal salvation" ([Orthodox Church in America, The Orthodox Faith: Old Testament, Psalms](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/doctrine-scripture/old-testament/psalms)), and the **Catholic** General Instruction asks those who pray them to be aware of "their full sense (sensus plenus), especially their Messianic sense, which was the reason for the Church's introduction of the psalter into its prayer" ([General Instruction of the Liturgy of the Hours, 109](https://www.ewtn.com/catholicism/library/general-instruction-on-the-liturgy-of-the-hours-28969)). The USCCB introduction notes that "Many of the royal Psalms were given a messianic interpretation by Christians", while "In Jewish tradition they were preserved, even after kingship had disappeared, because they were read in the light of the Davidic covenant" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Psalms](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/psalms/0)).
- `6` [John Calvin, Commentary on Psalms, Volume 1: The Author's Preface](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/calvin/calcom08.vi.html): the **Reformed** theologian John Calvin called the book "An Anatomy of all the Parts of the Soul", "for there is not an emotion of which any one can be conscious that is not here represented as in a mirror". In his preface to the Genevan Psalter he wrote that "we shall find no songs better and more suitable for our purpose than the Psalms of David, dictated to him and made for him by the Holy Spirit" ([Louis F. Benson, John Calvin and the Psalmody of the Reformed Churches, X](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/benson/psalmody.title.c10.html)). At his urging the poets Clément Marot and Théodore de Bèze put the psalms into French verse for the congregation to sing, until in 1562 "the complete Psalter appeared at Geneva", the beginning of the Reformed tradition of singing metrical psalms ([Louis F. Benson, John Calvin and the Psalmody of the Reformed Churches, VI](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/benson/psalmody.c6.html)).
- `6` [The Psalter for Individual & Family Devotions, with Introductions by Martin Luther](https://s3.amazonaws.com/cph-org-assets/media/pdf/124726.pdf) (Concordia Publishing House, sample pages): the **Lutheran** reformer Martin Luther, in his preface to the Psalter, wrote that the Psalter "ought to be a dear and beloved book, if only because it promises Christ's death and resurrection so clearly and so depicts His kingdom and the condition and nature of all Christendom that we may call it a little Bible", and that "everyone, whatever his situation may be, finds psalms and words in it that fit his situation".
- `6` [Walter Drum, Psalms, Catholic Encyclopedia (1911)](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/12533a.htm): on **the seven penitential psalms**, 6, 32, 38, 51, 102, 130 and 143 in the Hebrew numbering, the Catholic Encyclopedia notes that in the Latin rite they "have long been recited, in the above order, as prayers of sorrow for sin", and that "Their recitation during Lent was ordered by Innocent III (1198-1216)". Pope John Paul II called Psalm 51 "the most intense and commonly used penitential psalm", which "The Liturgy of the Hours makes us pray" "at Lauds every Friday" ([John Paul II, General Audience of 24 October 2001](https://www.vatican.va/content/john-paul-ii/en/audiences/2001/documents/hf_jp-ii_aud_20011024.html)). In the **Church of England**, the Book of Common Prayer's service "to be used on the first day of Lent" has the priest and people kneel and say Psalm 51 ([Book of Common Prayer, A Commination](https://www.churchofengland.org/liturgy/book-of-common-prayer/commination)).

## Open questions

Questions on which the churches or scholars disagree, for example about the book's authorship or the meaning of a passage. These guidelines don't pick a side.

- `7` [Reports of the Committee on Song in Worship](https://opc.org/GA/song.html) (Orthodox Presbyterian Church, 1946–1947): **should churches sing only the psalms in worship?** Paul urges Christians to speak to one another "in psalms, hymns, and spiritual songs" [Ephesians 5:19](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/EPH05.htm#V19), [Colossians 3:16](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/COL03.htm#V16). In the Orthodox Presbyterian Church, the majority of a study committee concluded that "the Greek word psalmos in the New Testament appears to have a generic sense wider than that of Old Testament Psalm", and that "The content of song, then, like the content of our prayer, need not be restricted to the very words of Scripture, although it must be assuredly Scriptural in teaching". The minority, John Murray and William Young, held that "The songs of divine worship must therefore be limited to the songs of Scripture, for they alone are inspired", and that "we should confine ourselves to the Book of Psalms"; a later General Assembly prepared a hymnal along the lines of the majority. The Reformed Presbyterian Church of North America holds the other view: "Our musical praise employs God's Word only, thus making use of the divinely inspired Book of Psalms of the Bible", and "we sing without the aid of musical instruments" ([Reformed Presbyterian Church of North America, Convictions](https://reformedpresbyterian.org/convictions)).
- `7` [General Instruction of the Liturgy of the Hours, 131](https://www.ewtn.com/catholicism/library/general-instruction-on-the-liturgy-of-the-hours-28969) (1971): **may Christians pray the cursing psalms?** Some psalms ask God to destroy the psalmist's enemies, as in "Happy shall he be, who takes and dashes your little ones against the rock" [Psalm 137:8–9](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/PSA137.htm#V8), [Psalm 109:6–20](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/PSA109.htm#V6). In the **Catholic** Liturgy of the Hours, "Three psalms (58, 83, and 109) have been omitted from the psalter cycle because of their curses; in the same way, some verses have been omitted from certain psalms". "The reason for the omission is a certain psychological difficulty, even though the psalms of imprecation are in fact used as prayer in the New Testament, for example, Rv 6:10, and in no sense to encourage the use of curses" [Revelation 6:10](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/REV06.htm#V10). The Book of Common Prayer's monthly cycle reads the whole Psalter ([Book of Common Prayer, The Order how the Psalter is appointed to be read](https://www.churchofengland.org/liturgy/book-of-common-prayer/order-how-psalter-appointed-be-read)). **Orthodox** teaching reads such psalms spiritually: "The psalms calling for victory over the enemies in battle refer to the only real Enemy, the devil", and "Babylon thus signifies the realm of Satan" ([Orthodox Church in America, The Orthodox Faith: Old Testament, Psalms](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/doctrine-scripture/old-testament/psalms)). The **Lutheran** pastor Dietrich Bonhoeffer, who wrote that "No section of the Psalter causes us greater difficulty today than the so-called imprecatory psalms", held that the one who prays them "calls for the wrath of God alone" and does not take revenge into his own hands, that "the imprecatory psalm leads to the cross of Jesus and to the love of God which forgives enemies", and that "In this way the crucified Jesus teaches us to pray the imprecatory psalms correctly" ([Dietrich Bonhoeffer, The Enemies, from Psalms: The Prayer Book of the Bible, Renovaré](https://renovare.org/articles/the-enemies), [Romans 12:19](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ROM12.htm#V19)).
- `7` [USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Psalms](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/psalms/0) (New American Bible, Revised Edition): **who wrote the psalms, and what does "of David" mean?** The USCCB introduction holds that "Seventy-three Psalms are attributed to David, but there is no sure way of dating any Psalm", and the ESV Global Study Bible that "David wrote about half of the Psalms" ([ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to The Psalms](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-the-psalms/)). Walter Drum records that "Christian tradition is alike uncertain": St Augustine "thinks that all the Psalms are Davidic", while St Jerome says that "they err who deem all the psalms are David's and not the work of those whose names are superscribed"; in 1910 the Pontifical Biblical Commission decided that "It cannot be prudently denied that David is the chief author of the songs of the Psalter" ([Walter Drum, Psalms, Catholic Encyclopedia (1911)](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/12533a.htm)). Rhonda Burnette-Bletsch explains that the heading *le-david* "has traditionally been interpreted as indicating King David's authorship", but that the Hebrew prefix "is very ambiguous and could legitimately be translated in many different ways (of, dedicated to, in the tradition of)", and that "The Psalter is an anthology compiled over a long period of time (perhaps centuries) and finalized in the 5th or 4th century B.C.E." ([Bible Odyssey, Rhonda Burnette-Bletsch, Psalm 23](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/psalm-23/)). Orthodox teaching notes that the psalms "are traditionally called the 'psalms of David,' although many of them most certainly come from other authors of much later times" ([Orthodox Church in America, The Orthodox Faith: Old Testament, Psalms](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/doctrine-scripture/old-testament/psalms)).

## Tutorials and further reading

- `7` [Christine Hayes, Biblical Poetry: Psalms and Song of Songs](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-21) (Open Yale Courses, Introduction to the Old Testament, lecture 21): a university lecture, with a transcript, on the five books of the Psalter, the superscriptions, the kinds of psalm and their place in Israel's worship.
- `7` [Bible Odyssey, Walter Brueggemann, Psalms in Israel's Worship](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/psalms-in-israels-worship/): the psalms in the temple liturgies and in the life of families, from the "songs of Zion" and the royal psalms to the laments.
- `6` [Bible Odyssey, G. Brooke Lester, Psalms of Lament](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/psalms-of-lament/): what a lament psalm is, its typical parts, and why "the book of Psalms contains more lament psalms than any other genre".
- `6` [Bible Odyssey, Jeffery Leonard, Torah and Psalms](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/torah-and-psalms/): the historical, wisdom and torah psalms, and Psalms 1, 19 and 119 as "quintessential Torah psalms".
- `6` [BibleProject, Psalms](https://bibleproject.com/videos/psalms/): an animated overview of the book as "a carefully designed collection of poetry that communicates key ideas about God's character".
