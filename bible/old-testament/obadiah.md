# Obadiah

"The vision of Obadiah" is the shortest book of the Old Testament: "This book, the shortest among the twelve minor prophets, is a single twenty-one-verse oracle against Edom" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Obadiah](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/obadiah/0); [Obadiah 1](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/OBA01.htm#V1)). Edom was a kingdom south-east of the Dead Sea, and "The biblical traditions consider Edom the brother of Israel/Judah because their respective ancestors are Esau and Jacob, the sons of Isaac and Rebekah" ([Brad Anderson, Edom, Bible Odyssey](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/edom/); [Genesis 25:21–26](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/GEN25.htm#V21); [Genesis](genesis.md)). The New American Bible calls the book "a bitter cry for vengeance against Edom for its pride and its crimes": "Mount Esau in Edom will be occupied and ravaged by the enemy, while Mount Zion will be restored to its former sanctity and security", and the book ends with "The triumphant refrain of Israelite eschatology", "The kingdom is the Lord's!" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Obadiah](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/obadiah/0); [Obadiah 21](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/OBA01.htm#V21)). Its opening verses "are very similar to part of an oracle against Edom in Jer 49" ([Jeremiah 49:9](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JER49.htm#V9), [Jeremiah 49:14–16](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JER49.htm#V14); [Jeremiah](jeremiah.md)).

The book tells us nothing about the prophet but his name, which means "worshiper" or "servant of Yah[weh]" ([Bible Odyssey, Obadiah (HarperCollins Bible Dictionary)](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/dictionary/obadiah/)); Jerome wrote of "Obadiah, whose name means the servant of God" ([Jerome, Letter 53, 8, New Advent](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/3001053.htm)). The New American Bible holds that "Nothing is known of the author", but that the prophecy "indicates a date of composition sometime after the Babylonian destruction of Jerusalem in 587 B.C., when the Edomites apparently took advantage of the helpless people of Judah and Jerusalem" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Obadiah](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/obadiah/0); [Obadiah 11](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/OBA01.htm#V11); [Psalm 137:7](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/PSA137.htm#V7)). The Talmud identifies him with Obadiah, the steward of King Ahab who hid a hundred prophets from Jezebel ([Sefaria, Sanhedrin 39b:13](https://www.sefaria.org/Sanhedrin.39b.13); [1 Kings 18:3–16](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1KI18.htm#V3); [1 Kings](1-kings.md)), and so does the Orthodox Church's life of the prophet ([Orthodox Church in America, Prophet Obadiah (Abdia)](https://www.oca.org/saints/lives/2026/11/19/103326-prophet-obadiah-abdia)). Who he was and when he wrote are open questions below.

Obadiah is Jewish Scripture: "Obadiah ('Ovadiah') is the shortest book in the Hebrew Bible, at just 21 verses, and is the fourth of 12 books of Minor Prophets ('Trei Asar')", and "Since Edom descends from the biblical character Esau, it is common to read Obadiah as the haftorah for parshat Vayishlach, which discusses the final encounter between Jacob and Esau" ([Sefaria, Obadiah](https://www.sefaria.org/Obadiah); [Genesis 32:3–33:16](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/GEN32.htm#V3)). The Talmud counts the twelve minor prophets as "one book of the Twelve Prophets", and explains that Hosea's prophecy is not written as a separate book because "since it is small it would be lost" ([Sefaria, Bava Batra 14b:9](https://www.sefaria.org/Bava_Batra.14b.9)). In the Greek Septuagint, the Old Testament of the Orthodox churches, the book is called Abdias, and its translation is "reasonably close" to the Hebrew ([NETS, The Twelve Prophets, translated by George E. Howard](https://ccat.sas.upenn.edu/nets/edition/32-twelve-nets.pdf)). In Christian Bibles it stands among the twelve minor prophets: the HarperCollins Bible Dictionary calls it "An OT book, the fourth of the twelve Minor Prophets" ([Bible Odyssey, Obadiah, The Book of (HarperCollins Bible Dictionary)](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/dictionary/obadiah-the-book-of/)).

## How the churches read it

Where the churches, or their interpreters, read the book differently, or where it has played a part in their history. Each entry starts with its source's score.

- `7` [Augustine, City of God, Book XVIII, 31](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/120118.htm) (New Advent): **Edom as the nations, and the kingdom of Christ.** Augustine, a teacher of the **Catholic** and the whole **Western** church, notes that Obadiah, "the briefest of all the prophets, speaks against Idumea, that is, the nation of Esau". Taking Idumea "as put for the nations", he reads the promise of safety on Mount Zion and "a Holy One" of Christ ([Obadiah 17](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/OBA01.htm#V17)), and the saviours who go up from Zion of "the believers in Christ from Judea, of whom the apostles are chiefly to be acknowledged", who defend "Mount Esau", which "signifies the Church of the Gentiles", "through the preaching of the gospel" ([Obadiah 21](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/OBA01.htm#V21); [Colossians 1:13](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/COL01.htm#V13)).
- `7` [Orthodox Church in America, Prophet Obadiah (Abdia)](https://www.oca.org/saints/lives/2026/11/19/103326-prophet-obadiah-abdia): **the Orthodox commemoration.** The **Orthodox** Church commemorates "The holy Prophet Obadiah [or Abdia]", "the fourth of the Twelve Minor Prophets", on 19 November. Its life holds that "he lived during the ninth century B.C.", "served as steward of the impious Israelite King Ahab" and sheltered the prophets of the Lord, and that his book "contains predictions about the future salvation of the Gentiles (Vs. 15) and that the Savior would come forth from Sion (Vs. 17)" ([Obadiah 15](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/OBA01.htm#V15), [Obadiah 17](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/OBA01.htm#V17)).
- `7` [Jerome, Letter 53, 8](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/3001053.htm) (New Advent): **Edom as the earthly man.** In his survey of the books of the Bible, Jerome writes that Obadiah "thunders against Edom red with blood and against the creature born of earth. He smites him with the spear of the spirit because of his continual rivalry with his brother Jacob".
- `6` [John Calvin, Commentary on Obadiah, Preface](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/calvin/calcom27.v.i.html) (Christian Classics Ethereal Library): **comfort for God's people.** The **Reformed** reformer held that the book "threatens the Idumeans for the sake of administering consolation to the chosen people", because Israel, in distress, saw Edom "living at ease and beyond the reach of danger". On the last verse he wrote that "God himself really rules in the person of Christ", with the saviours of the verse as ministers under him ([John Calvin, Commentary on Obadiah 21](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/calvin/calcom27.v.ii.xviii.html); [Obadiah 21](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/OBA01.htm#V21)).
- `6` [Matthew Henry, Commentary on the Whole Bible, Obadiah 1](https://www.ccel.org/h/henry/mhc2/MHC31001.HTM) (Christian Classics Ethereal Library): **"much in a little".** The **Presbyterian** commentator calls Obadiah "the shortest of all the books of the Old Testament", yet "not to be passed by, or thought meanly of" ([Matthew Henry, Commentary on the Whole Bible, Obadiah: Introduction](https://www.ccel.org/h/henry/mhc2/MHC31000.HTM)). He reads the saviours of the last verse as "the preachers of the gospel", and the kingdom as "the kingdom of the Messiah" ([Obadiah 21](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/OBA01.htm#V21)).
- `6` [Vanderbilt Divinity Library, Revised Common Lectionary: Sunday citations](https://lectionary.library.vanderbilt.edu/sunday-citations/): **seldom read in worship.** The Revised Common Lectionary, "used to varying degrees by the vast majority of mainline Protestant churches in Canada and the United States" ([Vanderbilt Divinity Library, Revised Common Lectionary: FAQ](https://lectionary.library.vanderbilt.edu/faq/)), has no reading from Obadiah: the book is missing from its list of readings, which includes Joel, Jonah and Habakkuk.

## Open questions

Questions on which the churches or scholars disagree. These guidelines don't pick a side.

- `7` [USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Obadiah](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/obadiah/0): **when was Obadiah written, and how is it related to Jeremiah 49?** The New American Bible dates it "sometime after the Babylonian destruction of Jerusalem in 587 B.C.", and explains the likeness to Jeremiah's oracle against Edom ([Jeremiah 49:7–22](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JER49.htm#V7)) by "suggesting that Israel's prophets drew upon traditional language and idioms in the composition of prophetic speech". The ESV Global Study Bible holds that it "was probably written in the first half of the Babylonian exile, after 586 B.C. (when Babylon destroyed Jerusalem) and before 553 (when Babylon attacked Edom)" ([ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to Obadiah](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-obadiah/)). The Orthodox Church in America places the prophet himself in "the ninth century B.C." ([Orthodox Church in America, Prophet Obadiah (Abdia)](https://www.oca.org/saints/lives/2026/11/19/103326-prophet-obadiah-abdia)), while its survey of the prophets places "perhaps Obadiah" with Haggai, Zechariah and Malachi in "the period of the return of God's People from exile" ([Orthodox Church in America, The Orthodox Faith: Old Testament, Prophets](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/doctrine-scripture/old-testament/prophets)). Calvin held that "they seem to be mistaken who think that Obadiah lived before the time of Isaiah", and that Jeremiah and Obadiah "made use of the same thoughts and nearly of the same words", but "I know not whether Obadiah and Jeremiah were contemporaries" ([John Calvin, Commentary on Obadiah, Preface](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/calvin/calcom27.v.i.html)). The Jewish Encyclopedia of 1906 sets out the possibilities: the likeness "may mean that Jeremiah borrowed from Obadiah, or that the latter borrowed from the former, or that both borrowed from a still earlier prophet", and reports scholars who divide the book into parts from different periods ([Jewish Encyclopedia, Obadiah, Book of (1906)](https://jewishencyclopedia.com/articles/11642-obadiah-book-of)).
- `7` [Sefaria, Sanhedrin 39b:13](https://www.sefaria.org/Sanhedrin.39b.13): **who was Obadiah?** The Talmud identifies the prophet with the Obadiah of King Ahab's household, and asks "For what reason did Obadiah merit prophecy? It is because he concealed one hundred prophets in a cave" ([1 Kings 18:3–4](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/1KI18.htm#V3)). It also records the view, in the name of Rabbi Meir, that "Obadiah was an Edomite convert", and therefore prophesied about Edom ([Sefaria, Sanhedrin 39b:15](https://www.sefaria.org/Sanhedrin.39b.15)). The Orthodox life of the prophet also makes him Ahab's steward ([Orthodox Church in America, Prophet Obadiah (Abdia)](https://www.oca.org/saints/lives/2026/11/19/103326-prophet-obadiah-abdia)). Matthew Henry calls that identification "a conjecture which has no ground", and also rejects the view that the prophet was an Edomite by birth ([Matthew Henry, Commentary on the Whole Bible, Obadiah: Introduction](https://www.ccel.org/h/henry/mhc2/MHC31000.HTM)). The ESV Global Study Bible holds that "The only thing known about this prophet is his name" ([ESV Global Study Bible, Introduction to Obadiah](https://www.esv.org/resources/esv-global-study-bible/introduction-to-obadiah/)), and the HarperCollins Bible Dictionary notes that Obadiah was "A common name used of at least eleven biblical personages" ([Bible Odyssey, Obadiah (HarperCollins Bible Dictionary)](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/dictionary/obadiah/)). The Talmud elsewhere holds that "The members of the Great Assembly wrote" the book of "the Twelve Prophets" ([Sefaria, Bava Batra 15a:2](https://www.sefaria.org/Bava_Batra.15a.2)).
- `7` [Brad Anderson, Edom, Bible Odyssey](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/edom/): **how should readers take an oracle of judgement against a neighbouring people?** Anderson notes that during and after the Babylonian conquest of Judah, Edom "seems to become an exemplary villain in the Hebrew Scriptures" ([Psalm 137:7](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/PSA137.htm#V7), [Ezekiel 35:1–15](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/EZK35.htm#V1)), but that "There is no evidence outside of the Bible for Edom's role in these events, and the biblical evidence for such complicity is vague and sparse". "A number of scholars have suggested that Edom became an undeserving scapegoat", while "Others maintain, however, that this widespread anti-Edom tradition must have come from somewhere, and the idea that it emerged without any historical basis is unlikely". He also points to "surprisingly positive elements to Edom's story in the Hebrew Bible", such as the land God gave to Esau ([Deuteronomy 2:4–5](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/DEU02.htm#V4), [Deuteronomy 23:7–8](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/DEU23.htm#V7)). Later readers turned Edom into a symbol: in Jewish tradition "The name 'Edom' is used by the Talmudists for the Roman empire, and they applied to Rome every passage of the Bible referring to Edom or to Esau" ([Jewish Encyclopedia, Edom, Idumea (1906)](https://jewishencyclopedia.com/articles/5434-edom-idumea)); Augustine read Edom as "the Church of the Gentiles" ([Augustine, City of God, Book XVIII, 31](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/120118.htm)), Jerome as "the creature born of earth" ([Jerome, Letter 53, 8](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/3001053.htm)), and Calvin as a people who "were hated by God" ([John Calvin, Commentary on Obadiah, Preface](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/calvin/calcom27.v.i.html)).
