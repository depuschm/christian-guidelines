# Ruth

Ruth is a short story set "In the days when the judges judged" ([Ruth 1:1](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/RUT01.htm#V1)). It is "named for the Moabite woman who commits herself to the Israelite people by an oath to her mother-in-law Naomi and becomes the great-grandmother of David by marriage to Boaz of Bethlehem", and the introduction to the New American Bible counts it among "the classic short stories of world literature" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Ruth](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/ruth/0)). Famine and death leave Naomi a widow without sons; Ruth stays with her, gleans in the fields of Boaz, a relative of Naomi's husband, and marries him, and their son Obed is "the father of Jesse, the father of David" ([Ruth 4:17](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/RUT04.htm#V17)). God is rarely seen acting directly, but, in the words of the BibleProject, "he works through their everyday faithfulness" ([BibleProject, Ruth](https://bibleproject.com/videos/ruth/)). Christine Hayes calls Ruth "a paragon of *hesed*, this quality of steadfast love and covenantal loyalty that binds the members of the covenant community to one another and to God" ([Christine Hayes, Introduction to the Old Testament, Lecture 23, Open Yale Courses](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-23)), and Leila Leah Bronner notes that the Hebrew word *hesed* is "one of the key words controlling the text", used at the beginning, in the middle and at the end of the story ([Leila Leah Bronner, Ruth and Lovingkindness, My Jewish Learning](https://www.myjewishlearning.com/article/ruth-and-lovingkindness/)).

The book names no author. The Babylonian Talmud records the tradition that "Samuel wrote his own book, the book of Judges, and the book of Ruth" ([Sefaria, Bava Batra 14b](https://www.sefaria.org/Bava_Batra.14b.12)). The USCCB introduction notes that the story is told "from a point some time after the course of events", since it explains an old custom ([Ruth 4:7](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/RUT04.htm#V7)), and that "The date of composition is disputed" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Ruth](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/ruth/0)); the positions are set out under the open questions below.

Every church reads Ruth among the historical books of the Old Testament ([Orthodox Church in America, The Orthodox Faith: Old Testament, History](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/doctrine-scripture/old-testament/history)). "In the Greek and Latin canons, Ruth follows Judges" and precedes Samuel, "serving as transition from Israel as tribal union to monarchy". In the Hebrew canon it stands among the "Writings", "immediately after the Book of Proverbs, which ends with a powerful portrayal of 'the woman of worth'" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Ruth](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/ruth/0); [Proverbs 31:10–31](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/PRO31.htm#V10)); Boaz calls Ruth "a worthy woman" ([Ruth 3:11](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/RUT03.htm#V11)). Ruth is also Jewish Scripture: "one of the five megillot (scrolls)", it "is traditionally read on the holiday of Shavuot" ([Sefaria, Ruth](https://www.sefaria.org/Ruth)), the feast of Weeks.

## How the churches read it

Where the churches, or their interpreters, read the book differently, or where it has played a part in their history. Each entry starts with its source's score.

- `7` [John Chrysostom, Homily 3 on Matthew](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/200103.htm) (New Advent): on **Ruth among the ancestors of Christ** ([Matthew 1:3–6](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MAT01.htm#V3)), the church father John Chrysostom explains that Matthew names Ruth and Rahab, "the one an alien, the other an harlot, that you may learn that He came to do away with all our ills". The **Catholic** introduction to the book in the New American Bible calls Ruth "an ancestor in the messianic line that leads to Jesus", and notes that she is called "the Moabite" so often that "the audience of the story is constantly reminded of the universality of the embrace of salvation" ([USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Ruth](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/ruth/0)). The **Reformed** commentator Matthew Henry, a Presbyterian minister ([Christian Classics Ethereal Library, Matthew Henry](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/henry)), writes that "In the conversion of Ruth the Moabitess, and the bringing of her into the pedigree of the Messiah, we have a type of the calling of the Gentiles" ([Matthew Henry, Commentary on the Whole Bible, Ruth: Introduction](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/henry/mhc2.Ru.i.html)).
- `7` [Orthodox Church in America, Sunday before the Nativity](https://www.oca.org/saints/lives/2026/12/20/81-sunday-before-the-nativity): on the Sunday before Christmas, the **Orthodox** Church commemorates the righteous of the Old Testament from Adam and Eve onwards, among them "the righteous Boaz, son of Salmon, the righteous Obed, son of Boaz and Ruth" and, among the righteous women, "the righteous Ruth, wife of Boaz".
- `6` [Matthew Henry, Commentary on the Whole Bible, Ruth 4](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/henry/mhc2.Ru.v.html) (Christian Classics Ethereal Library): on **Boaz as a figure of Christ the Redeemer** ([Ruth 4:9–10](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/RUT04.htm#V9)), Matthew Henry writes: "Our Lord Jesus is our Goel, our Redeemer, our everlasting Redeemer. He looked, like Boaz, with compassion on the deplorable state of fallen mankind", and "purchased a peculiar people, whom he would espouse to himself, though strangers and foreigners, like Ruth, poor and despised". John Chrysostom had drawn the same likeness: Boaz "neither despised her poverty nor abhorred her mean birth, as Christ having received the Church, being both an alien and in much poverty, took her to be partaker of the great blessings" ([John Chrysostom, Homily 3 on Matthew](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/200103.htm)).
- `6` [USCCB, Readings for Saturday of the Twentieth Week in Ordinary Time (23 August 2025)](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/readings/082325.cfm): **Ruth in worship**. In 2025, for example, the weekday readings of the **Catholic** Mass read Ruth's pledge to Naomi on Friday 22 August ([USCCB, Readings for 22 August 2025](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/readings/082225.cfm)), and Ruth's gleaning, marriage and son on the next day, the Saturday of the twentieth week in Ordinary Time. The Revised Common Lectionary, used by most mainline **Protestant** churches in Canada and the United States ([Vanderbilt Divinity Library, Revised Common Lectionary: FAQ](https://lectionary.library.vanderbilt.edu/faq/)), reads Ruth on two Sundays after Pentecost in Year B, Proper 26 and Proper 27 ([Vanderbilt Divinity Library, Revised Common Lectionary: Sunday citations](https://lectionary.library.vanderbilt.edu/sunday-citations/)).

## Open questions

Questions on which the churches or scholars disagree. These guidelines don't pick a side.

- `7` [USCCB, Introduction to the Book of Ruth](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/ruth/0) (New American Bible, Revised Edition): **when was Ruth written, and why?** The Talmud ascribes it to Samuel ([Sefaria, Bava Batra 14b](https://www.sefaria.org/Bava_Batra.14b.12)). The USCCB introduction notes that "Many authors date it early in the monarchy, and valid arguments can be presented for that position", while "Others argue for a postexilic date", reading the book's favourable picture of a Moabite woman "as a counter to the stringent measures of Ezra and Nehemiah against marriage with Moabites and other non-Jews" ([Ezra 10:2–3](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/EZR10.htm#V2), [Nehemiah 13:1](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/NEH13.htm#V1), [Nehemiah 13:23–27](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/NEH13.htm#V23)); the Law had said that "An Ammonite or a Moabite shall not enter into the LORD's assembly" ([Deuteronomy 23:3](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/DEU23.htm#V3)). Christine Hayes holds that the book "was certainly written later" than the time it describes, and that "whether it was post-exilic or pre-exilic is not certain"; either way, she argues, for Jews after the exile it "would have stood in opposition to the negative view of foreigners, the ban on intermarriage and the purely genealogical definition of Israelite identity" promoted by Ezra and Nehemiah ([Christine Hayes, Introduction to the Old Testament, Lecture 23, Open Yale Courses](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-23)).
- `7` [USCCB, Ruth, chapter 3](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/ruth/3) (New American Bible, Revised Edition, with notes): **what happened on the threshing floor?** Ruth "came softly, uncovered his feet, and lay down", and asks Boaz to "spread the corner of your garment over your servant"; she lies "at his feet until the morning" ([Ruth 3:7–9](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/RUT03.htm#V7), [Ruth 3:14](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/RUT03.htm#V14)). The notes of the New American Bible hold that "Israelite custom and moral expectations strongly suggest that there is no loss of virtue involved in the scene", and that the words about the garment are "a request for marriage", as in [Ezekiel 16:8](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/EZK16.htm#V8). Matthew Henry, citing the "Chaldee paraphrase", the ancient Aramaic translation, holds that Boaz "did not offer to violate her chastity, though he had all the opportunity that could be" ([Matthew Henry, Commentary on the Whole Bible, Ruth 3](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/henry/mhc2.Ru.iv.html)). Other scholars read the scene as more charged: Tod Linafelt notes that "'feet' is sometimes a code word in the Hebrew Bible for a more private part of the body" ([Tod Linafelt, Ruth, Bible Odyssey](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/ruth/)), and Christine Hayes calls the uncovering of the feet "possibly a sexual euphemism" ([Christine Hayes, Introduction to the Old Testament, Lecture 23, Open Yale Courses](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-23)).
