# The Eastern Orthodox churches

The Eastern Orthodox Church is a communion of self-governing (autocephalous) churches that share one faith and one sacramental life. The Holy and Great Council of 2016 described it as fourteen local autocephalous churches "recognized at a pan-Orthodox level" ([Holy and Great Council, Encyclical, 5](https://www.holycouncil.org/encyclical-holy-council)): the Ecumenical Patriarchate of Constantinople, the Patriarchates of Alexandria, Antioch and Jerusalem, and the Churches of Russia, Serbia, Romania, Bulgaria, Georgia, Cyprus, Greece, Poland, Albania, and the Czech Lands and Slovakia ([Holy and Great Council, Churches](https://www.holycouncil.org/churches)). The Ecumenical Patriarch of Constantinople traditionally holds the title "first among equals". In 2017 the Pew Research Center counted nearly 260 million Orthodox Christians. That number includes the Oriental Orthodox churches of Egypt, Ethiopia, Eritrea, India, Armenia and Syria, about a fifth of the total, which form a separate family of churches and will get their own page ([Pew Research Center, Orthodox Christianity in the 21st Century](https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2017/11/08/orthodox-christianity-in-the-21st-century/)).

The Eastern and Western churches have been divided since the Great Schism of 1054, in which a dispute over the authority of the pope weighed heavily ([Pew Research Center](https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2017/11/08/orthodox-christianity-in-the-21st-century/)). Orthodox teaching holds that the Orthodox Church is "the One, Holy, Catholic, and Apostolic Church". The Orthodox churches take part in the World Council of Churches, except the Churches of Georgia and Bulgaria, which withdrew in 1997 and 1998 ([Holy and Great Council, Relations of the Orthodox Church with the Rest of the Christian World](https://www.holycouncil.org/rest-of-christian-world)).

Orthodox teaching calls the life of the Church, handed down from the apostles and guided by the Holy Spirit, Holy Tradition. Within it, Thomas Hopko writes, "the Bible holds the first place", followed by the liturgy, the decisions of the councils, the writings of the Church Fathers, the lives of the saints, the canons and the icons ([Orthodox Church in America, Tradition](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/doctrine-scripture/sources-of-christian-doctrine/tradition)). The seven ecumenical councils, from Nicaea in 325 to the second council of Nicaea in 787, are received as "essential sources of Orthodox Christian doctrine" ([Orthodox Church in America, The Councils](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/doctrine-scripture/sources-of-christian-doctrine/the-councils)). The Longer Catechism of St Philaret of Moscow, approved by the Holy Synod of the Russian Church in 1830, is a widely used summary of the faith ([Schaff, Creeds of Christendom](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/schaff/creeds2.vi.iii.html)).

## Adopted references

- `8` [The Orthodox Faith](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith) (Thomas Hopko, Orthodox Church in America): a four-volume introduction to Orthodox doctrine and Scripture, worship, church history and spirituality, published online by the Orthodox Church in America.
- `8` [Official Documents of the Holy and Great Council of the Orthodox Church](https://www.holycouncil.org/official-documents) (Crete, 2016): the council's encyclical and message, and its documents on fasting, relations with other Christians, marriage, the mission of the Church in today's world, the Orthodox diaspora and autonomy.
- `8` [Liturgy of the Byzantine Rite](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Christianity/LiturgyoftheByzantineRite) (Damaskinos Olkinuora, St Andrews Encyclopaedia of Theology): the history and structure of the liturgical tradition that the Eastern Orthodox churches share: its daily, weekly and yearly cycles and the Divine Liturgy.
- `7` [The Longer Catechism of the Orthodox, Catholic, Eastern Church](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/schaff/creeds2.vi.iii.html) (St Philaret of Moscow, 1830, in Philip Schaff's *Creeds of Christendom*): an introduction on Tradition and Scripture, then the faith of the Creed, hope and prayer, and love and the Ten Commandments, in questions and answers.
- `7` [Christian Ethics: Eastern Orthodox perspectives](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Christianity/ChristianEthics#section4.2) (St Andrews Encyclopaedia of Theology): Orthodox ethics as an ethics of transfiguration, aimed at deification and the transfiguration of creation, and lived through the virtues and prayer.

## Teaching

In the order of the Orthodox tradition's own sources: Tradition and Scripture, the faith of the Creed and the councils, salvation, the Church and its worship, and the Christian life. *Related rules* are the general rules the teaching matches; the cell is empty where a teaching is the tradition's own.

## Differences within the tradition

- `8` [Cyril Hovorun, Orthodox Political Theology](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Christianity/OrthodoxPoliticalTheology) (St Andrews Encyclopaedia of Theology): not all the churches took part in the Holy and Great Council of 2016, among them the Russian Orthodox Church, and Hovorun describes two positions in Orthodox thought today, one that accepts modern liberal democracy and one that criticises it. The Russian Orthodox Church set out its social teaching in 2000, and the Holy Synod of the Ecumenical Patriarchate later adopted its own document, *For the Life of the World: Toward a Social Ethos of the Orthodox Church* ([University of Fribourg, Institute for Ecumenical Studies](https://www.unifr.ch/iso/en/news/news/24780/next)).
- `7` [Patriarchal and Synodal Tomos for the Orthodox Church in Ukraine](https://ec-patr.org/en/14/01/00/00/patriarchal-and-synodal-tomos-for-the-bestowal-of/) (Ecumenical Patriarchate, 6 January 2019): the Ecumenical Patriarchate granted autocephaly to the Orthodox Church of Ukraine. The Holy Synod of the Russian Orthodox Church, which regards Ukraine as part of its canonical territory, had declared in October 2018 that it could no longer remain in Eucharistic communion with Constantinople ([Vatican News, 16 October 2018](https://www.vaticannews.va/en/world/news/2018-10/russian-orthodox-break-communion-with-ecumenical-patriarch.html)). The Ukrainian church's status is therefore disputed among the Orthodox churches.
- `7` [Towards a Common Date for Easter](https://www.oikoumene.org/resources/documents/towards-a-common-date-for-easter) (World Council of Churches and Middle East Council of Churches, Aleppo, 1997): in 1923 some Orthodox churches revised their calendar for fixed feasts, a decision that "led to several schisms" with those who keep the old calendar, while all of them still calculate Easter by the Julian calendar. The churches of Constantinople, Alexandria, Antioch, Greece, Cyprus, Romania, Poland and Bulgaria use the revised calendar for fixed feasts, while the churches of Jerusalem, Russia and Serbia and the monasteries of Mount Athos keep the Julian calendar and celebrate Christmas on 7 January ([Utrecht University, A Brief History of the Reformed Orthodox Calendar](https://webspace.science.uu.nl/~gent0113/easter/easter_text5d.htm)).
