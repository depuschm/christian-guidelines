# The Methodist churches

The Methodist churches trace their origins to the movement that John and Charles Wesley led within the Church of England in the eighteenth century. The Wesleys organised a network of societies, divided into smaller band and class meetings, as "structures for accountable discipleship", and Methodism soon became known as an "evangelistic order" within the Church of England ([St Andrews Encyclopaedia of Theology, Methodist Theology, 1.1](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Christianity/MethodistTheology#section1.1)). On 24 May 1738, while hearing Luther's preface to the Epistle to the Romans read at a society in Aldersgate Street, John Wesley "felt my heart strangely warmed" ([Methodist Church in Britain, Assurance of God's love](https://www.methodist.org.uk/about/what-is-distinctive-about-methodism/assurance-of-gods-love/)). The World Methodist Council, which grew out of a conference held at Wesley's Chapel in London in 1881, is made up of 80 Methodist, Wesleyan and related Uniting and United churches, representing over 80 million members in 138 countries ([World Methodist Council, About Us](https://worldmethodistcouncil.org/about-us/), [World Methodist Council, Member Churches](https://worldmethodistcouncil.org/member-churches/)).

Methodists don't understand themselves as a "confessional" tradition, although they hold to the Apostles' Creed. Almost all Methodist churches name a small set of "doctrinal standards", among them John Wesley's Standard Sermons and his Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament. In 1784 Wesley adapted twenty-five Articles of Religion from the Church of England's Thirty-nine Articles for the Methodist church in America, and in 1968 The United Methodist Church added the Confession of Faith of the Evangelical United Brethren Church to its doctrinal standards ([St Andrews Encyclopaedia of Theology, Methodist Theology, 1.2.2](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Christianity/MethodistTheology#section1.2.2)). The United Methodist Book of Discipline holds that Scripture is "the primary source and criterion for Christian doctrine", and describes Wesley's belief that the living core of the Christian faith "was revealed in Scripture, illumined by tradition, vivified in personal experience, and confirmed by reason" ([Book of Discipline, ¶105](https://www.umc.org/en/content/book-of-discipline-105-our-theological-task)). The theologian Albert Outler named this the "Wesleyan Quadrilateral" ([St Andrews Encyclopaedia of Theology, Methodist Theology, 1.2.4](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Christianity/MethodistTheology#section1.2.4)).

## Adopted references

- `8` [The Articles of Religion of the Methodist Church](https://www.umc.org/en/content/articles-of-religion) (The United Methodist Church, Book of Discipline): the twenty-five articles that Wesley adapted from the Church of England's Thirty-nine Articles, part of the United Methodist doctrinal standards, from the Trinity and Scripture to justification and the sacraments.
- `8` [Confession of Faith of the Evangelical United Brethren Church](https://www.umc.org/en/content/confession-of-faith) (The United Methodist Church, Book of Discipline): sixteen articles of faith, added to the United Methodist doctrinal standards in 1968, including justification and regeneration, sanctification and Christian perfection, and the Lord's Day.
- `8` [Book of Discipline, ¶105: Our Theological Task](https://www.umc.org/en/content/book-of-discipline-105-our-theological-task) (The United Methodist Church): how United Methodists do theology with Scripture as the primary source, read in the light of tradition, experience and reason.
- `8` [Methodist Theology](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Christianity/MethodistTheology) (Paul Chilcote, St Andrews Encyclopaedia of Theology): the Wesleys' practical theology, Methodist doctrinal standards and the quadrilateral, the streams of Methodist theology from holiness to liberal and contextual theologies, and the witness of global Methodism.
- `7` [The General Rules of the Methodist Church](https://www.umc.org/en/content/the-general-rules-of-the-methodist-church) (The United Methodist Church): the rules John Wesley gave the Methodist societies, which The United Methodist Church acknowledges as a guide for theological reflection and action: do no harm, do good, and attend upon all the ordinances of God.

## Teaching

## Differences within the tradition

- `7` [Historic assembly sets United Methodists on new path](https://www.umnews.org/en/news/historic-assembly-sets-united-methodists-on-new-path) (UM News, 2024): **the separation over sexuality**. More than 7,600 churches in the United States, a quarter of the denomination's US churches, left The United Methodist Church under a disaffiliation policy passed by a special General Conference in 2019. Most of them were theologically conservative and supported the church's bans relating to LGBTQ people, and departures accelerated after the launch in 2022 of the Global Methodist Church, "a theologically conservative denomination" ([Global Methodist Church, Our Beliefs & Governance](https://www.globalmethodist.org/our-beliefs---governance) gives its official launch as 1 May 2022). In 2024 the United Methodist General Conference revised the church's Social Principles, removing the statement that "the practice of homosexuality… is incompatible with Christian teaching" and broadening the description of marriage.
- `7` [Methodist Theology, 2.3: The holiness theology stream](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Christianity/MethodistTheology#section2.3) (St Andrews Encyclopaedia of Theology): **the holiness churches**. In the nineteenth century, Phoebe Palmer's teaching on entire sanctification as a "second definite work of grace" shaped a holiness movement that gave birth to "the Church of the Nazarene, the Free Methodist Church, the Wesleyan Church, and the Salvation Army", although many Methodists viewed her interpretation as "an aberration of authentic Wesleyan teaching". The Church of the Nazarene, organised in 1908, describes itself as "a Protestant Christian church in the Wesleyan-Holiness tradition" with about 2.7 million members ([Church of the Nazarene, Our History](https://nazarene.org/our-history/)). Classical Pentecostals in turn "inherited the concerns for holiness and Christian perfection" from the Holiness Movement ([St Andrews Encyclopaedia of Theology, Pentecostal Theology, 3.2](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Christianity/PentecostalTheology#section3.2)).
- `7` [Methodist Theology, 2: Divergent streams](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Christianity/MethodistTheology#section2) (St Andrews Encyclopaedia of Theology): **streams of Methodist theology**. After the Wesleys, Methodist theology diverged first into British and American traditions, and then into further streams: the holiness, liberal, neo-Wesleyan and contextual theologies, the last including Black and Womanist, liberation and feminist, and Indigenous theologies.
- `6` [When did the church first ordain women?](https://www.umc.org/en/content/ask-the-umc-when-did-the-church-first-ordain-women) (The United Methodist Church): **the ordination of women**. Anna Howard Shaw was refused ordination by the General Conference of the Methodist Episcopal Church in 1880 and ordained that year by the Methodist Protestant Church, and The Methodist Church in the United States granted women full clergy rights in 1956. In Britain, women could be deaconesses from 1890, but were first ordained as presbyters on 2 July 1974 ([Methodist Church in Britain, 50 years of women's ordination as presbyters](https://www.methodist.org.uk/about/news/the-methodist-church-in-great-britain-celebrates-50-years-of-womens-ordination-as-ministers/)).
