# The Anglican churches

The Anglican churches are the Church of England and the churches in communion with the See of Canterbury, which together form the Anglican Communion. The Lambeth Conference describes the Communion as "a network of independent and interdependent churches in communion" with the See of Canterbury, based in 165 countries and "representing tens of millions of Christians" ([Lambeth Conference, What is the Anglican Communion?](https://www.lambethconference.org/about/what-is-the-anglican-communion/)). It is made up of about forty autonomous churches, also called provinces, with an estimated membership of around 85 million people, including four United Churches of the Indian subcontinent ([St Andrews Encyclopaedia of Theology, Anglican Theology, 5.1](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Christianity/AnglicanTheology#section5.1)).

Anglican theology "emerged within Western Catholicism during the sixteenth-century Reformation" in England ([St Andrews Encyclopaedia of Theology, Anglican Theology](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Christianity/AnglicanTheology)). The Communion has no central legislative authority: the Lambeth Conference of 1930 described its churches as bound together "by mutual loyalty sustained through the common counsel of the Bishops in conference" ([St Andrews Encyclopaedia of Theology, Anglican Theology, 5.1](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Christianity/AnglicanTheology#section5.1)). Each province governs itself, guided by four Instruments of Communion: the Archbishop of Canterbury, the Lambeth Conference, the Primates' Meeting and the Anglican Consultative Council ([Lambeth Conference, What is the Anglican Communion?](https://www.lambethconference.org/about/what-is-the-anglican-communion/), [St Andrews Encyclopaedia of Theology, Anglican Theology, 6.1](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Christianity/AnglicanTheology#section6.1)).

In the Church of England, Canon A5 holds that the church's doctrine "is grounded in the Holy Scriptures, and in such teachings of the ancient Fathers and Councils of the Church as are agreeable to the said Scriptures", and is found in particular in the Thirty-nine Articles of Religion, the Book of Common Prayer and the Ordinal ([Canons of the Church of England, A 5](https://www.churchofengland.org/resources/legal/canons-church-england/section-a)). Across the Communion, the Chicago–Lambeth Quadrilateral, adopted by the bishops of the Episcopal Church in Chicago in 1886 and by the Lambeth Conference in 1888, names four elements that Anglicans hold in common: the Scriptures, the Apostles' and Nicene Creeds, the two sacraments of baptism and the Lord's Supper, and the historic episcopate ([St Andrews Encyclopaedia of Theology, Anglican Theology, 5.3](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Christianity/AnglicanTheology#section5.3)). Other provinces have adapted the formularies and their prayer books to their own contexts ([St Andrews Encyclopaedia of Theology, Anglican Theology, 6.1](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Christianity/AnglicanTheology#section6.1)).

## Adopted references

- `9` [The Book of Common Prayer](https://www.churchofengland.org/liturgy/book-of-common-prayer) (Church of England): the prayer book that has been part of the Church of England's worship since 1662: Morning and Evening Prayer, the Litany, Holy Communion, baptism, confirmation, marriage, the burial of the dead, the Psalter, the Catechism, the Articles of Religion and the Ordinal.
- `8` [Articles of Religion](https://www.churchofengland.org/liturgy/book-of-common-prayer/articles-religion) (Church of England): the Thirty-nine Articles, which set out the Church of England's teaching on the questions disputed at the Reformation, from Scripture and the creeds to justification, the church and the sacraments.
- `8` [Anglican Theology](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Christianity/AnglicanTheology) (Stephen Spencer and Joseph Galgalo, St Andrews Encyclopaedia of Theology): how Anglican theology developed, its historic formularies, its high church, evangelical and broad traditions, the Anglican Communion and its instruments, and the divisions of recent decades.
- `7` [Canons of the Church of England, Section A](https://www.churchofengland.org/resources/legal/canons-church-england/section-a) (Church of England): the canons on the Church of England, including A 5 on the sources of its doctrine.
- `7` [Common Worship](https://www.churchofengland.org/liturgy/common-worship) (Church of England): the texts which, together with the Book of Common Prayer, make up the official liturgy of the Church of England, including Holy Communion, Daily Prayer, baptism and confirmation.

## Differences within the tradition

- `7` [Anglican Theology, 4: Varieties of Anglican theology](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Christianity/AnglicanTheology#section4) (St Andrews Encyclopaedia of Theology): **three traditions**. Since the eighteenth century Anglicans have followed three main ways: high church, later Anglo-Catholic, which stresses apostolic succession and the sacraments; evangelical, later including charismatic evangelicalism, which stresses justification by faith on the basis of Scripture; and broad, later including ecumenical and social theology, which looks to F. D. Maurice. All three are found across the Communion.
- `7` [Anglican Theology, 6.2: Innovation and division](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Christianity/AnglicanTheology#section6.2) (St Andrews Encyclopaedia of Theology): **the ordination of women**. In 1944 Florence Li Tim-Oi from Hong Kong became the first woman ordained priest; her province authorised the ordination of women in 1971, the Episcopal Church in the United States in 1976, and about ten more provinces by 2000. The Lambeth Conference of 1978 recognised "the legal right of each Church to make its own decision" about ordaining women. The Church of England enacted the legislation allowing women to be ordained bishops in 2014 ([Church of England, Legislation on women bishops becomes law at General Synod](https://www.churchofengland.org/news/legislation-women-bishops-becomes-law-general-synod)). Those opposed hold the practice to be contrary to Anglican biblical teaching and ecclesiology. The article describes the question as contentious but "not fatal to unity".
- `7` [Anglican Theology, 5.2: Consultation rather than regulation](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Christianity/AnglicanTheology#section5.2) (St Andrews Encyclopaedia of Theology): **marriage and sexuality**. Resolution I.10 of the Lambeth Conference of 1998 rejected homosexual practice "as incompatible with Scripture" and advised against blessing same-sex unions, in the face of strong opposition from a minority of bishops. Divisions deepened after 2002 and 2003, when a Canadian diocese authorised a liturgy for blessing same-sex unions and the Episcopal Church consecrated a bishop in a same-sex partnership, and some parishes left the Episcopal Church to form the Anglican Church in North America ([Anglican Theology, 6.2](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Christianity/AnglicanTheology#section6.2)). The Global Anglican Future Conference (GAFCON), formed in 2008, upholds "the unchangeable standard of Christian marriage between one man and one woman" and does "not accept that Anglican identity is determined necessarily through recognition by the Archbishop of Canterbury" ([GAFCON, The Jerusalem Statement](https://gafcon.org/about/the-jerusalem-statement/)). The Episcopal Church amended its canons in 2015 "to permit any couple the rite of holy matrimony" ([The Episcopal Church, LGBTQ+ in the Church](https://www.episcopalchurch.org/who-we-are/ministries/gender-justice/lgbtq-in-the-church/)). In the Church of England, ministers may choose to offer same-sex couples the Prayers of Love and Faith, prayers of dedication, thanksgiving and blessing within a regular service, and the church says that "there are still deep divisions" on these questions ([Church of England, Relationships, sexuality and marriage](https://www.churchofengland.org/about/our-views/relationships-sexuality-and-marriage)).
