# The Anabaptist churches

The Anabaptist churches come from the reforming movements that sprang up in the 1520s, alongside Luther's and Zwingli's reformations, in and around the Holy Roman Empire ([St Andrews Encyclopaedia of Theology, Anabaptist Theology, 2.1](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Christianity/AnabaptistTheology#section2.1)). In Zurich in 1525, a group broke with Ulrich Zwingli's reformation ([Anabaptist Theology, 1.2](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Christianity/AnabaptistTheology#section1.2)). They rejected infant baptism and baptised one another as adults, so their opponents called them Anabaptists, "rebaptizers". Catholic and Protestant authorities persecuted them severely: in the movement's first century, as many as 2,500 were executed ([Young Center for Anabaptist and Pietist Studies, Anabaptists](https://groups.etown.edu/amishstudies/religion/anabaptists/)). In 1527 the Swiss Brethren agreed the Schleitheim Confession, seven short articles on baptism, the ban, the Lord's Supper, separation from the world, pastors, the sword and the oath ([Anabaptist Theology, 3.1](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Christianity/AnabaptistTheology#section3.1), [The Schleitheim Confession](https://courses.washington.edu/hist112/SCHLEITHEIM%20CONFESSION%20OF%20FAITH.htm)). In the Netherlands, the Mennonites kept to the teaching of the leader Menno Simons ([Anabaptist Theology, 2.2.2](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Christianity/AnabaptistTheology#section2.2.2)).

The family today includes Mennonites, Amish, Hutterites, the Brethren in Christ, and the Brethren churches that began in Schwarzenau, Germany, in 1708 ([Young Center, Anabaptists](https://groups.etown.edu/amishstudies/religion/anabaptists/)). Mennonite World Conference counts 2.03 million baptised believers in 87 countries in churches rooted in the Anabaptist movement, about two-thirds of them African, Asian or Latin American. Its own members are 109 Mennonite and Brethren in Christ national churches from 60 countries, with close to 950,000 baptised believers ([Mennonite World Conference, Membership, map and statistics](https://mwc-cmm.org/en/membership-map-and-statistics/)).

Anabaptist churches have written many confessions of faith. Scholars note that these confessions were written less to fix the content of the faith than to state the lived convictions that the churches hold in common and to promote discipleship ([Anabaptist Theology, 3.1](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Christianity/AnabaptistTheology#section3.1)). Mennonite World Conference's Shared Convictions (2006) say that its churches "accept the Bible as our authority for faith and life, interpreting it together under Holy Spirit guidance, in the light of Jesus Christ" ([Shared Convictions](https://mwc-cmm.org/en/shared-convictions/)). The Confession of Faith in a Mennonite Perspective (1995), written when two large North American Mennonite churches merged, has been taken up by several Mennonite communities around the world ([Anabaptist Theology, 3.1](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Christianity/AnabaptistTheology#section3.1)). The Church of the Brethren says that its central emphasis "is not a creed, but a commitment to follow Christ in simple obedience" ([Church of the Brethren, Beliefs](https://www.brethren.org/about/beliefs/)).

## Adopted references

- `9` [Shared Convictions](https://mwc-cmm.org/en/shared-convictions/) (Mennonite World Conference, 2006): the convictions that the Anabaptist-related churches of the world hold in common, on God, Jesus, the church, the Bible, peace, worship, and witness in the world.
- `8` [Confession of Faith in a Mennonite Perspective](https://www.mennoniteusa.org/who-are-mennonites/what-we-believe/confession-of-faith/) (1995, on the site of Mennonite Church USA): 24 articles, each with Bible references and a commentary, from God and Scripture to baptism, foot washing, church discipline, truth and oaths, peace and nonresistance, and the church's relation to government.
- `8` [Anabaptist Theology](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Christianity/AnabaptistTheology) (Jamie Pitts and Luis Tapia Rubio, St Andrews Encyclopaedia of Theology): the history of the Anabaptist movements and traditions, the status of their confessions, and their teaching on Scripture, Christ, discipleship, salvation and the church.
- `7` [The Schleitheim Confession of Faith, 1527](https://courses.washington.edu/hist112/SCHLEITHEIM%20CONFESSION%20OF%20FAITH.htm) (J. C. Wenger's translation from the Mennonite Quarterly Review, 1945, on a University of Washington course page): the seven articles of the Swiss Brethren, written with Michael Sattler as chief author.

## Differences within the tradition

- `8` [Called Together to be Peacemakers](https://www.christianunity.va/content/unitacristiani/en/dialoghi/sezione-occidentale/conferenza-mennonita-mondiale/documenti-di-dialogo/en.html) (report of the international dialogue between the Catholic Church and Mennonite World Conference, 2003): on **relations with the churches that once persecuted them**, Catholics and Mennonites call for a "purification of memories" and "a spirit of repentance—a penitential spirit—on both sides". In 2010 the Lutheran World Federation's assembly in Stuttgart unanimously asked for forgiveness "from God and from our Mennonite sisters and brothers" for the Lutheran persecution of Anabaptists ([Lutheran World Information, 8/2010](https://lutheranworld.org/sites/default/files/2022-02/LWI-201008-EN-low.pdf)).
- `7` [Amish Origins](https://groups.etown.edu/amishstudies/religion/amish-origins/) (Young Center for Anabaptist and Pietist Studies, Elizabethtown College): the **Amish** began in 1693, when Jakob Ammann sought to renew the Swiss Anabaptist church with communion twice a year, foot washing and stricter discipline. Old Order Amish live by the *Ordnung*, district rules, "usually unwritten", that apply separation from the world to clothing, media, technology and leisure, and they use technology selectively rather than rejecting it ([Young Center, Regulations](https://groups.etown.edu/amishstudies/cultural-practices/regulations/), [Young Center, Technology](https://groups.etown.edu/amishstudies/cultural-practices/technology/)).
- `7` [Hutterites, Summary of Beliefs](https://hutterites.org/our-beliefs/summary-beliefs/): the **Hutterites**, founded in 16th-century Moravia and now living in the North American plains, hold that sharing all goods in community is central to discipleship, while other Anabaptist churches practise voluntary mutual aid instead ([Anabaptist Theology, 2.2.4](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Christianity/AnabaptistTheology#section2.2.4), [3.6.2](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Christianity/AnabaptistTheology#section3.6.2)).
- `7` [Church of the Brethren, History](https://www.brethren.org/about/history/): the **Brethren** churches began in August 1708, when five men and three women, among them Alexander Mack, a miller influenced by both Pietism and Anabaptism, were baptised as believers in the Eder River at Schwarzenau. The Brethren in Christ, who began in Pennsylvania in the 18th century, also describe "strong Anabaptist and Pietist backgrounds" ([Brethren in Christ U.S., Our History](https://bicus.org/about/history/)).
- `7` [Church Discipline](https://groups.etown.edu/amishstudies/religion/church-discipline/) (Young Center for Anabaptist and Pietist Studies): **church discipline** varies. Among the Amish, members who will not repent may be excommunicated, and shunning typically follows, which can bring a lifetime of estrangement. The Mennonite Confession of Faith describes a discipline that is "exercised in a redemptive manner" ([Confession of Faith, Article 14](https://www.mennoniteusa.org/who-are-mennonites/what-we-believe/confession-of-faith/discipline-in-the-church/)). Disputes over discipline have divided Anabaptist churches many times ([Anabaptist Theology, 3.6.2](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Christianity/AnabaptistTheology#section3.6.2)).
- `7` [Mennonite Church USA, Delegate Assembly Minutes, May 2022](https://www.mennoniteusa.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/09/Delegate-Assembly-Minutes-May-2022.pdf): on **sexuality and membership**, Mennonite Church USA's delegates approved a resolution on the role of its Membership Guidelines by 404 votes to 84. The minutes record that some saw it as a question of church order, others as a biblical and theological question tied to the Confession of Faith, whose article on marriage holds that "God intends marriage to be a covenant between one man and one woman for life" ([Confession of Faith, Article 19: Marriage](https://www.mennoniteusa.org/who-are-mennonites/what-we-believe/confession-of-faith/marriage/)). Scholars note that debates over gender and sexuality raise questions about the status of confessions in churches formed by free decision ([Anabaptist Theology, 3.1](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Christianity/AnabaptistTheology#section3.1)).
