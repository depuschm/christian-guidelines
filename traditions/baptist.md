# The Baptist churches

The Baptist churches trace their beginning to a group of Puritans from the East Midlands of England who emigrated to Amsterdam in 1607, where they were influenced by the Waterlanders, a Mennonite group. The group was led by John Smyth, a clergyman of the established church, and Thomas Helwys, a lawyer. Helwys and some of the members returned to England and met in Spitalfields, London, where Helwys wrote an early plea for religious toleration, *The Mistery of Iniquitie*, and died in prison by 1616. By the 1640s, immersion had become the standard practice of baptism, and there were both Arminian (General) and Calvinist (Particular) Baptist congregations ([Baptist Historical Society, 17th Century](https://www.baptisthistory.org.uk/Groups/404874/17th_Century.aspx)). Today the Baptist World Alliance, founded in 1905, is a fellowship of 283 conventions and unions in 138 countries and territories, with 53 million baptised believers in 180,000 churches ([Baptist World Alliance, Members](https://baptistworld.org/members/)). The Southern Baptist Convention in the United States voted in 2004 to withdraw from it ([Baptist World Alliance, Resolution 2004.4](https://baptistworld.org/bwa-resolutions/withdrawal-of-the-southern-baptist-convention-from-the-family-of-the-baptist-world-alliance/)).

In Baptist teaching, Scripture is the final authority, and confessions of faith serve it without binding the conscience. The Southern Baptist Convention's Baptist Faith and Message quotes a statement of 1925: "the sole authority for faith and practice among Baptists is the Scriptures of the Old and New Testaments. Confessions are only guides in interpretation, having no authority over the conscience" ([Baptist Faith and Message 2000, Preamble](https://bfm.sbc.net/preamble/)). The Declaration of Principle of the Baptist Union of Great Britain names Jesus Christ "the sole and absolute authority in all matters pertaining to faith and practice, as revealed in the Holy Scriptures", and holds "that each Church has liberty, under the guidance of the Holy Spirit, to interpret and administer His laws" ([Baptist Union of Great Britain, Declaration of Principle](https://www.baptist.org.uk/Groups/220595/Declaration_of_Principle.aspx)). Local Baptist churches are self-governing, with "no hierarchy of bishops or priests exercising authority over their members", and they associate with one another in regional, national and international bodies ([Baptist Union of Great Britain, Who are Baptists?](https://www.baptist.org.uk/Groups/437957/Who_are_Baptists.aspx)).

## Differences within the tradition

- `7` [17th Century](https://www.baptisthistory.org.uk/Groups/404874/17th_Century.aspx) (Baptist Historical Society): **General and Particular Baptists**. By the 1640s there were both Arminian (General) and Calvinist (Particular) Baptist congregations. The Calvinists adopted the Second London Confession in 1689, which became their standard statement for many years and still is in some places; the General Baptists produced their own Standard Confession in 1678.
- `7` [Baptist Faith and Message 2000, VI: The Church](https://bfm.sbc.net/bfm2000/#vi) (Southern Baptist Convention): **women in ministry**. The Southern Baptist Convention's confession holds that "while both men and women are gifted for service in the church, the office of pastor/elder/overseer is limited to men as qualified by Scripture", an article it amended in 2023. The Baptist Union of Great Britain has had women ministers since the pioneering work of Violet Hedger, Edith Gates and Maria Living-Taylor in the 1920s, and its first woman General Secretary in 2013 ([Baptist Historical Society, 20th Century](https://www.baptisthistory.org.uk/Groups/404877/20th_Century.aspx)); its Project Violet studies the obstacles that women ministers face ([Baptist Union of Great Britain, Project Violet](https://www.baptist.org.uk/Groups/363245/Project_Violet.aspx)).
- `7` [Baptist Faith and Message 2000, VII: Baptism and the Lord's Supper](https://bfm.sbc.net/bfm2000/#vii) (Southern Baptist Convention): **who may belong and receive communion**. The Baptist Faith and Message makes believers' baptism "prerequisite to the privileges of church membership and to the Lord's Supper". The Baptist Union of Great Britain says that many Baptists "will want to welcome and affirm those who have a different story to tell of how they have been baptised and come to faith" ([Baptist Union of Great Britain, Believer's Baptism](https://www.baptist.org.uk/Groups/220594/Believers_Baptism.aspx)), and in a dialogue in Italy, Baptists declared themselves willing to accept as members those in whom "the fruits of baptism" are found, whatever the form and time of their baptism ([St Andrews Encyclopaedia of Theology, Baptism, 6.2](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Christianity/Baptism#section6.2)).
- `7` [Withdrawal of the Southern Baptist Convention from the Family of the Baptist World Alliance](https://baptistworld.org/bwa-resolutions/withdrawal-of-the-southern-baptist-convention-from-the-family-of-the-baptist-world-alliance/) (Baptist World Alliance, Resolution 2004.4): **the world fellowship**. The Southern Baptist Convention, meeting in Indianapolis in June 2004, voted to withdraw from the Baptist World Alliance, which it had helped to found. The Alliance's General Council acknowledged the convention's "enormous contribution" and regretted the decision as a "loss of unity".
- `7` [Members](https://baptistworld.org/members/) (Baptist World Alliance): **Baptist families in the United States**. Besides the Southern Baptist Convention, Baptists in the United States belong to many conventions, among them the American Baptist Churches USA, the Cooperative Baptist Fellowship and three of the major historically Black Protestant denominations, all members of the Alliance: the National Baptist Convention, USA (founded in 1880), the National Baptist Convention of America International (1915) and the Progressive National Baptist Convention (1961), which Martin Luther King Jr and his supporters founded ([Pew Research Center, A brief overview of Black religious history in the U.S.](https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2021/02/16/a-brief-overview-of-black-religious-history-in-the-u-s/)).
