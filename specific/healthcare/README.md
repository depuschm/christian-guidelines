# Healthcare and the end of life

Scope: people who are ill and their families, doctors, nurses and other health workers, carers, chaplains, and everyone who visits the sick; decisions about treatment, care at the end of life, and the care of the body after death. The general rules come first: care for the sick and the dying and never abandon them (`HOPE-3`), stay with those who suffer and grieve (`HOPE-2`), prepare for your own death (`HOPE-5`), and take thoughts of suicide seriously (`HOPE-6`), all in [Suffering, death and hope](../../general/suffering.md), together with equal dignity for every person (`JUST-2`), conscience (`DISCERN-3`) and truthful speech (`SPEECH-1`). Ministers who visit the sick also keep confidences within clear limits (`LEAD-3`) and refer people to those who can help them better (`LEAD-6`), in [Leading a church and pastoral care](../church-leadership/README.md). This page adds what the churches teach together about decisions on treatment, care for the dying, the work of health workers and the body after death. The churches whose teaching documents are cited in the key rules reject euthanasia and assisted suicide, but not every church does: that question, and others on which the churches differ, are set out below and in the [suffering page's open questions](../../general/suffering.md#open-questions). Questions at the beginning of life, such as abortion, in vitro fertilisation and embryo research, are outside this page, and these guidelines don't cover them yet. This is moral and pastoral guidance, not medical or legal advice: decisions about treatment belong to patients, those who speak for them and their doctors, and the law differs from country to country. If you or someone near you is in danger now, call your local emergency number or a crisis line.

> Entries are sorted by score from 1 to 10 ([criteria](../../RANKING.md)): 9–10 start here or fundamental, 6–8 recommended, 1–5 further reading or situational.

## Adopted references

- `8` [Samaritanus bonus](https://www.vatican.va/roman_curia/congregations/cfaith/documents/rc_con_cfaith_doc_20200714_samaritanus-bonus_en.html) (Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith, 2020): Catholic teaching on the care of people in the critical and final stages of life, with sections on aggressive medical treatment, basic care, palliative care, the family and hospice, pain relief and sedation, conscientious objection, and the sacraments. **The fullest teaching document on this area.**
- `7` [Declaration on Euthanasia](https://www.vatican.va/roman_curia/congregations/cfaith/documents/rc_con_cfaith_doc_19800505_euthanasia_en.html) (*Iura et bona*, Sacred Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith, 1980): the earlier Catholic declaration on the value of human life, euthanasia, the use of painkillers, and "due proportion in the use of remedies".
- `7` [New Charter for Health Care Workers](https://www.humandevelopment.va/content/dam/sviluppoumano/pubblicazioni-documenti/documenti/nuova-carta-degli-operatori-sanitari/Nuova-Carta-Operati-Sanitar---EN.pdf) (Pontifical Council for Pastoral Assistance to Health Care Workers, English translation by the National Catholic Bioethics Center, PDF): the Catholic charter for doctors, nurses and other health workers, in three parts, "Procreating", "Living" and "Dying", including palliative care, telling the truth to the dying and conscientious objection.
- `7` [Catechism of the Catholic Church, 2276–2283](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P7Z.HTM): Catholic teaching on euthanasia, on giving up "over-zealous" treatment, on painkillers, and on suicide.
- `7` [Catechism of the Catholic Church, 2288–2301](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P80.HTM): Catholic teaching on respect for health, scientific research and organ transplants, care for the dying, and respect for the bodies of the dead.
- `7` [Bases of the Social Concept, XII: Problems of bioethics](https://old.mospat.ru/en/documents/social-concepts/xii/) (Russian Orthodox Church, 2000): Orthodox teaching on medicine, including the transplantation of organs (XII.7) and the end of life, palliative care and euthanasia (XII.8).
- `7` [Christian Decision-Making and the End of Life](https://files.lcms.org/dl/f/ctcr-report-christian-decision-making-and-the-end-of-life) (Commission on Theology and Church Relations, Lutheran Church–Missouri Synod, 2023, PDF): a report that supplements the Synod's 1993 report *Christian Care at Life's End*, with sections on refusing and withdrawing treatment, artificial nutrition and hydration, palliative care and sedation, decision-makers, hospice care, and pastoral care.
- `7` [Statement on Euthanasia](https://media.methodist.org.uk/media/documents/pi_euthanasia_74_bNiGJld.pdf) (Methodist Church in Britain, Conference 1974, PDF): the Methodist statement on painkillers that shorten life, on when not to prolong survival, and against legalising euthanasia.
- `7` [The URC's Position on Assisted Dying](https://urc.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/Paper-F1-On-Assisted-Dying.pdf) (United Reformed Church, Assembly Executive, 2026, PDF): a paper that reprints the Assembly's 2007 resolution on the end of life, on treatment that prolongs life, palliative treatment, assisted dying, advance directives and hospice care, and asks whether that position still stands.
- `7` [Fund palliative care instead of 'unworkable and unsafe' assisted suicide law](https://www.churchofengland.org/news/fund-palliative-care-instead-unworkable-and-unsafe-assisted-suicide-law-synod) (Church of England, 2025): the General Synod's motion reaffirming "that every person is of immeasurable and irreducible value" and asking the government to fund palliative care, passed by 238 votes to 7.
- `7` [On Assisted Suicide and the Sanctity of Life](https://www.sbc.net/resource-library/resolutions/on-assisted-suicide-and-the-sanctity-of-life/) (Southern Baptist Convention, 2026): a resolution opposing euthanasia and assisted suicide and urging pain management, hospice care and palliative support.
- `7` [Position Paper of the Abrahamic Monotheistic Religions on Matters Concerning the End of Life](https://press.vatican.va/content/salastampa/en/bollettino/pubblico/2019/10/28/191028f.html) (Holy See Press Office, 2019): an interreligious paper, signed in the Vatican by representatives of the Abrahamic religions ([Vatican News](https://www.vaticannews.va/en/vatican-city/news/2019-10/abrahamic-religions-life-euthanasia-suicide-palliative.html)), on withholding treatment, euthanasia, conscientious objection, spiritual care and palliative care.

## Key rules

### Caring for the sick and the dying

- `8` **Give the sick and the dying good palliative care: relieve their pain and distress, even with medicine that may shorten life, but never with the aim of ending it.** The Samaritan "bound up his wounds, pouring on oil and wine" and "took care of him", and Jesus says "I was sick and you visited me". The Catholic Church calls palliative medicine "a precious and crucial instrument in the care of patients during the most painful, agonizing, chronic and terminal stages of illness", and teaches that painkillers may be used "even at the risk of shortening their days" if death "is not willed as either an end or a means", since "Palliative care is a special form of disinterested charity". The Russian Orthodox Church teaches that when intensive care becomes impossible "its place should be taken by palliative aid (anaesthetisation, nursing and social and psychological support) and pastoral care". The Methodist Church in Britain holds that when the treatment needed to control pain shortens life, "it is appropriate for the treatment to be given"; the United Reformed Church accepts palliative treatment that "can also hasten death" "as long as the intention of the treatment is pain relief and comfort of the patient"; and the Lutheran Church–Missouri Synod's report says of raising the dose of morphine for a dying patient in extreme pain that "Double-effect reasoning would support this as a moral decision and good medical practice". The Southern Baptist Convention urges "effective pain management, hospice care, and palliative support", and the Church of England's General Synod has asked for "desperately needed" palliative care. ([Luke 10:33–34](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/LUK10.htm#V33), [Matthew 25:36](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MAT25.htm#V36), [Samaritanus bonus, V.4 and V.7](https://www.vatican.va/roman_curia/congregations/cfaith/documents/rc_con_cfaith_doc_20200714_samaritanus-bonus_en.html), [Catechism of the Catholic Church, 2279](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P7Z.HTM), [Russian Orthodox Church, Bases of the Social Concept, XII.8](https://old.mospat.ru/en/documents/social-concepts/xii/), [Methodist Church in Britain, Statement on Euthanasia, 3](https://media.methodist.org.uk/media/documents/pi_euthanasia_74_bNiGJld.pdf), [United Reformed Church, Assembly 2007, Resolution 51](https://urc.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/Paper-F1-On-Assisted-Dying.pdf), [Lutheran Church–Missouri Synod, Christian Decision-Making and the End of Life](https://files.lcms.org/dl/f/ctcr-report-christian-decision-making-and-the-end-of-life), [Southern Baptist Convention, On Assisted Suicide and the Sanctity of Life (2026)](https://www.sbc.net/resource-library/resolutions/on-assisted-suicide-and-the-sanctity-of-life/), [Church of England, Fund palliative care instead of 'unworkable and unsafe' assisted suicide law (2025)](https://www.churchofengland.org/news/fund-palliative-care-instead-unworkable-and-unsafe-assisted-suicide-law-synod))
- `8` **You need not accept every possible treatment: treatment that is futile or too burdensome may be refused or withdrawn, but ordinary care must go on.** "For everything there is a season … a time to be born, and a time to die." The Catechism of the Catholic Church teaches that "Discontinuing medical procedures that are burdensome, dangerous, extraordinary, or disproportionate to the expected outcome can be legitimate", since "Here one does not will to cause death", and the 1980 Declaration on Euthanasia says that such a refusal "is not the equivalent of suicide"; *Samaritanus bonus* adds that "The suspension of futile treatments must not involve the withdrawal of therapeutic care". The Russian Orthodox Church teaches that the prolongation of life by artificial means "cannot be viewed as obligatory". The Lutheran Church–Missouri Synod's report affirms "the principle that withholding or withdrawing treatments may be done when it becomes useless or excessively burdensome"; the United Reformed Church recognises "that there is a time to die and that there are circumstances in which it will be wrong to continue to provide treatment designed to prolong life"; and the Methodist Church in Britain calls a decision not to intervene in such cases "compassionate and responsible. It is not euthanasia." Representatives of the Abrahamic religions agreed in 2019 that "When death is imminent despite the means used, it is justified to make the decision to withhold certain forms of medical treatments that would only prolong a precarious life of suffering". The churches differ on when artificial food and fluids may be withdrawn (see the open questions). ([Ecclesiastes 3:1–2](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ECC03.htm#V1), [Catechism of the Catholic Church, 2278](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P7Z.HTM), [Declaration on Euthanasia, IV](https://www.vatican.va/roman_curia/congregations/cfaith/documents/rc_con_cfaith_doc_19800505_euthanasia_en.html), [Samaritanus bonus, V.2](https://www.vatican.va/roman_curia/congregations/cfaith/documents/rc_con_cfaith_doc_20200714_samaritanus-bonus_en.html), [Russian Orthodox Church, Bases of the Social Concept, XII.8](https://old.mospat.ru/en/documents/social-concepts/xii/), [Lutheran Church–Missouri Synod, Christian Decision-Making and the End of Life](https://files.lcms.org/dl/f/ctcr-report-christian-decision-making-and-the-end-of-life), [United Reformed Church, Assembly 2007, Resolution 51](https://urc.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/Paper-F1-On-Assisted-Dying.pdf), [Methodist Church in Britain, Statement on Euthanasia, 5](https://media.methodist.org.uk/media/documents/pi_euthanasia_74_bNiGJld.pdf), [Position Paper of the Abrahamic Monotheistic Religions on Matters Concerning the End of Life](https://press.vatican.va/content/salastampa/en/bollettino/pubblico/2019/10/28/191028f.html))
- `7` **Make sure the sick and the dying can receive the prayer, visits and sacraments of their church.** "Is any amongst you sick? Let him call for the elders of the assembly, and let them pray over him, anointing him with oil in the name of the Lord." The Catechism of the Catholic Church teaches that relatives "must see to it that the sick receive at the proper time the sacraments that prepare them to meet the living God", and *Samaritanus bonus* that "Palliative care should include spiritual assistance for patients and their families"; the Russian Orthodox Church joins "pastoral care" to palliative care at the end of life. The Book of Common Prayer begins its order for visiting the sick: "When any person is sick, notice shall be given thereof to the Minister of the Parish"; and the Lutheran Church–Missouri Synod's report notes that "It may be necessary for the family to request that their pastor rather than the hospice chaplain provide spiritual care and counsel for the dying". The 2019 interreligious position paper holds that "Spiritual and religious assistance is a fundamental right of the patient and a duty of the faith community". ([James 5:14](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JAS05.htm#V14), [Catechism of the Catholic Church, 2299](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P80.HTM), [Samaritanus bonus, V.4](https://www.vatican.va/roman_curia/congregations/cfaith/documents/rc_con_cfaith_doc_20200714_samaritanus-bonus_en.html), [Russian Orthodox Church, Bases of the Social Concept, XII.8](https://old.mospat.ru/en/documents/social-concepts/xii/), [Book of Common Prayer, The Visitation of the Sick](https://www.churchofengland.org/liturgy/book-of-common-prayer/visitation-sick), [Lutheran Church–Missouri Synod, Christian Decision-Making and the End of Life](https://files.lcms.org/dl/f/ctcr-report-christian-decision-making-and-the-end-of-life), [Position Paper of the Abrahamic Monotheistic Religions on Matters Concerning the End of Life](https://press.vatican.va/content/salastampa/en/bollettino/pubblico/2019/10/28/191028f.html))

### Truth, consent and planning ahead

- `7` **Tell the sick the truth gently, and let them take part in the decisions about their own care.** Paul calls Christians to speak "truth in love", and the prophet Isaiah told the sick King Hezekiah plainly, "Set your house in order, for you will die". The Catholic Church's charter for health workers holds that "A person has the right to be informed" about his own state of health, that the physician has "a duty to respectfully communicate the patient’s conditions", and that the prospect of death "does not exempt the physician from truthfulness"; the Catechism of the Catholic Church teaches that decisions about stopping treatment "should be made by the patient if he is competent and able or, if not, by those legally entitled to act for the patient". The Lutheran Church–Missouri Synod's report wants patients and physicians "to discuss and deliberate on how to proceed with a course of treatment", with "a candid disclosure of the potential risks and the rewards", and notes that "patients are not obliged to submit themselves to every recommendation" by a physician. ([Ephesians 4:15](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/EPH04.htm#V15), [Isaiah 38:1](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ISA38.htm#V1), [New Charter for Health Care Workers, 156](https://www.humandevelopment.va/content/dam/sviluppoumano/pubblicazioni-documenti/documenti/nuova-carta-degli-operatori-sanitari/Nuova-Carta-Operati-Sanitar---EN.pdf), [Catechism of the Catholic Church, 2278](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P7Z.HTM), [Lutheran Church–Missouri Synod, Christian Decision-Making and the End of Life](https://files.lcms.org/dl/f/ctcr-report-christian-decision-making-and-the-end-of-life))
- `7` **Plan ahead: tell those close to you and your doctors how you wish to be cared for, and name someone you trust to speak for you if you can't.** Isaiah's word to Hezekiah, "Set your house in order", is a call to prepare. *Samaritanus bonus* teaches that giving up treatments that would only prolong life painfully "can also mean respect for the will of the dying person as expressed in advanced directives for treatment, excluding however every act of a euthanistic or suicidal nature"; the United Reformed Church holds that "a Living Will or Advance Directive which has been prepared by a patient of sound mind, can be helpful for carers and relatives", but adds: "we do not believe such a document should be used to facilitate a person’s death"; and the Lutheran Church–Missouri Synod's report favours naming a proxy, "a durable power of attorney (POA) for health care", which "rightfully burdens those who loved her with asking a far more relevant question": what is best for her now. ([Isaiah 38:1–5](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ISA38.htm#V1), [Samaritanus bonus, V.2](https://www.vatican.va/roman_curia/congregations/cfaith/documents/rc_con_cfaith_doc_20200714_samaritanus-bonus_en.html), [United Reformed Church, Assembly 2007, Resolution 51](https://urc.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/Paper-F1-On-Assisted-Dying.pdf), [Lutheran Church–Missouri Synod, Christian Decision-Making and the End of Life](https://files.lcms.org/dl/f/ctcr-report-christian-decision-making-and-the-end-of-life))

### Health workers

- `8` **Respect the conscience of doctors, nurses and other health workers, and never press them to take part in ending a life.** The Hebrew midwives "feared God, and didn't do what the king of Egypt commanded them, but saved the baby boys alive"; and the apostles answered, "We must obey God rather than men." The Catholic Church teaches that "Governments must acknowledge the right to conscientious objection in the medical and healthcare field", and that the physician "keeps the right and the duty not to carry out wishes that conflict with his own conscience"; the Russian Orthodox Church "calls upon the state to recognise the right of medics to refuse to procure abortion for the reasons of conscience"; and the Lutheran Church–Missouri Synod's report holds that doctors are not "required to follow bad medicine or unethical practice no matter how strongly their patients plead for it". The 2019 interreligious position paper holds that "No health care provider should be coerced or pressured to either directly or indirectly assist in the deliberate and intentional death of a patient". ([Exodus 1:15–17](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/EXO01.htm#V15), [Acts 5:29](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/ACT05.htm#V29), [Samaritanus bonus, V.9](https://www.vatican.va/roman_curia/congregations/cfaith/documents/rc_con_cfaith_doc_20200714_samaritanus-bonus_en.html), [New Charter for Health Care Workers, 150](https://www.humandevelopment.va/content/dam/sviluppoumano/pubblicazioni-documenti/documenti/nuova-carta-degli-operatori-sanitari/Nuova-Carta-Operati-Sanitar---EN.pdf), [Russian Orthodox Church, Bases of the Social Concept, XII.2](https://old.mospat.ru/en/documents/social-concepts/xii/), [Lutheran Church–Missouri Synod, Christian Decision-Making and the End of Life](https://files.lcms.org/dl/f/ctcr-report-christian-decision-making-and-the-end-of-life), [Position Paper of the Abrahamic Monotheistic Religions on Matters Concerning the End of Life](https://press.vatican.va/content/salastampa/en/bollettino/pubblico/2019/10/28/191028f.html))

### After death

- `7` **Treat the bodies of the dead with respect, and consider giving your organs after death as a free gift.** Joseph of Arimathaea and Nicodemus took Jesus' body "and bound it in linen cloths with the spices", and Jesus told his disciples, "Freely you received, so freely give." The Catechism of the Catholic Church teaches that "The bodies of the dead must be treated with respect and charity, in faith and hope of the Resurrection", and that "Donation of organs after death is a noble and meritorious act and is to be encouraged as a manifestation of generous solidarity", but only with "explicit consent". The Russian Orthodox Church holds that Christian burial expresses "the reverence that befits the body of a dead", and that "the posthumous giving of organs and tissues can be a manifestation of love spreading also to the other side of death", which "cannot be considered a duty" and needs "the voluntary consent of a donor in his lifetime". The Church of England's General Synod has called on parishes to encourage their members "to consider, as part of their Christian giving", "registering as organ donors and making their wishes known to their families". ([John 19:38–40](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/JHN19.htm#V38), [Matthew 10:8](https://ebible.org/eng-webbe/MAT10.htm#V8), [Catechism of the Catholic Church, 2296 and 2300–2301](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P80.HTM), [Russian Orthodox Church, Bases of the Social Concept, XII.7](https://old.mospat.ru/en/documents/social-concepts/xii/), [Church of England, General Synod backs call to encourage blood and organ donation (2016)](https://www.churchofengland.org/news/general-synod-backs-call-encourage-blood-and-organ-donation))

## Open questions

Questions on which the churches disagree. These guidelines don't pick a side.

- `7` [The URC's Position on Assisted Dying](https://urc.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/Paper-F1-On-Assisted-Dying.pdf) (United Reformed Church, 2026, PDF): **should the law allow assisted dying, and may a Christian ever choose it?** The Catholic Church calls euthanasia "morally unacceptable" whatever its motives ([Catechism of the Catholic Church, 2277](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P7Z.HTM)); the Russian Orthodox Church holds that "euthanasia is a form of homicide or suicide" ([Russian Orthodox Church, Bases of the Social Concept, XII.8](https://old.mospat.ru/en/documents/social-concepts/xii/)); the Church of England's General Synod asked the government to fund palliative care "instead of enacting a law that puts the most vulnerable at risk" ([Church of England, 2025](https://www.churchofengland.org/news/fund-palliative-care-instead-unworkable-and-unsafe-assisted-suicide-law-synod)); and the Southern Baptist Convention opposes "euthanasia and assisted suicide in all its forms" ([Southern Baptist Convention, 2026](https://www.sbc.net/resource-library/resolutions/on-assisted-suicide-and-the-sanctity-of-life/)). The United Church of Canada, by contrast, adopted a statement in 2017 that said "We are not opposed in principle to the legislation allowing assistance in dying and to such assistance being the informed, free choice of terminally ill patients", while urging "a cautious approach" ([United Church of Canada, Medical Assistance in Dying, 2020 Statement](https://generalcouncil.ca/media/660/download)). Some churches are reviewing their positions: the United Reformed Church, whose Assembly said in 2007 that it "would therefore oppose any change in the law to permit voluntary euthanasia or assisted suicide", is asking whether that decision "is still our understanding of the Mind of Christ"; and the Methodist Church in Britain, which "Historically … has opposed any change to the law" ([Methodist Church in Britain, Assisted dying (2024)](https://www.methodist.org.uk/about/news/assisted-dying/)), has set up a task group to review its position and report "not later than 2028" ([Methodist Church in Britain, The debate over assisted dying](https://www.methodist.org.uk/faith/life-and-faith/death-and-dying/the-debate-over-assisted-dying-physician-assisted-suicide/)). The [suffering page](../../general/suffering.md#open-questions) asks the related question of where refusing treatment ends and ending a life begins.
- `7` [Responses to Certain Questions Concerning Artificial Nutrition and Hydration](https://www.vatican.va/roman_curia/congregations/cfaith/documents/rc_con_cfaith_doc_20070801_risposte-usa_en.html) (Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith, 2007): **when may artificial food and fluids be withdrawn, for example from someone in a permanent vegetative state?** The Catholic Church teaches that giving food and water "even by artificial means is, in principle, an ordinary and proportionate means of preserving life", and that it may not be stopped only because a patient "will never recover consciousness"; *Samaritanus bonus* adds "provided that it does not cause harm or intolerable suffering to the patient" ([Samaritanus bonus, V.3](https://www.vatican.va/roman_curia/congregations/cfaith/documents/rc_con_cfaith_doc_20200714_samaritanus-bonus_en.html)). The Lutheran Church–Missouri Synod's report holds that tube feeding "should never be deemed useless or futile if it sustains life and prevents death by starvation or dehydration", but also that "In many end-of-life situations, the continued administration of hydration and nutrition may create rather than ameliorate problems" ([Lutheran Church–Missouri Synod, Christian Decision-Making and the End of Life](https://files.lcms.org/dl/f/ctcr-report-christian-decision-making-and-the-end-of-life)). The Methodist Church in Britain's 1974 statement counted "the indefinite maintenance by extraordinary means of the physical phenomena of life in a person whose medical problems have resulted in a permanent loss of consciousness" among the treatments that "need careful consideration" ([Methodist Church in Britain, Statement on Euthanasia, 15](https://media.methodist.org.uk/media/documents/pi_euthanasia_74_bNiGJld.pdf)), and the Russian Orthodox Church holds that prolonging life "by artificial means, in which in fact only some organs continue to function, cannot be viewed as obligatory" ([Russian Orthodox Church, Bases of the Social Concept, XII.8](https://old.mospat.ru/en/documents/social-concepts/xii/)).
